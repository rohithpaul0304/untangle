# Opening storyboard v2 — “Could a ban help them?”

5 October 2026. Proposed revision using storytelling-hooks and the founder’s chat feedback. Text only; no prototype changes. Preserve v1 beside this document. This proposal does not replace the full chapter or its practice questions.

## What the review changed

The previous opening had an interesting question but prescribed every move. It also ended with a research explanation that felt detached from the scene. This version gives one real decision, preserves both outcomes, and uses the rival’s changed position as the visual payoff. It restores the assumption check that v1 omitted.

Audience: curious young adults who habitually scroll and want useful insights about work and decisions. Format: interactive mobile lesson, not a social video. Goal: an understood discovery followed by voluntary continuation. Tone: intriguing, warm, clear, never patronising.

Use the skill’s tension, surprise and matching opening/ending. Do not treat its claims about brains, algorithms or two-second attention as verified evidence for this app. Voice is optional; every line is readable with sound off.

## Hook options considered

Editorial checks: clear tension; relevance to competitive decisions; honest claim; visual payoff. Pass counts below are writing checks, not engagement scores.

| Hook | Storytelling devices | Checks | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| “Your ads are banned. Could that be good news?” | Personal stakes, unanswered question, surprise | 4/4 | Recommended. Short, connects directly to the shared restriction. |
| “You cut a cost. So why did you keep less?” | Familiar goal, conflict, surprise | 4/4 | Strong alternative for the fictional experiment; weaker historical opening. |
| “Could losing an advantage leave you better off?” | Conflict, unanswered question | 2/4 | Too abstract and hard to picture. |
| “Would you pay to advertise—or save the money?” | Personal stakes, conflict | 3/4 | Concrete, but little reason to care before context. |
| “Both companies lose their ads. Could both keep more?” | Symmetry, unanswered question, surprise | 4/4 | Clear alternative; reveals more of the solution upfront. |

Recommend the first. It places the learner inside the question without claiming all bans help businesses. The historical card specifies cigarette TV/radio advertising; the experiment is explicitly fictional.

## Storyboard

### 1. A restriction that might help

**First frame:** two televisions cut to black. Company signs remain. No cigarettes, smoking animations, or fabricated historical profit counters.

**Context text:** “Cigarette advertising · TV and radio ban”

**Fox, eyebrows raised:** “Your ads are banned. Could that be good news?”

**Action:** `Try the experiment`

Transition into a labelled fictional scene. Do not ask for a prediction and then another navigation tap before anything happens.

### 2. Your first decision

**Scene label:** “Made-up example · Before the ban”

Two companies: **You** and **Rival**. Both televisions play. Only your money is counted prominently.

Coins arrive, then the ad bill takes its share:

**100 in − 40 on ads = 60 left after ads**

**Fox:** “You want to keep more money. Your rival will keep advertising.”

**Rule strip:** “Customers spend the same total. Ads affect who gets their business.”

**Fox’s question:** “Will you keep your ads or stop paying for them?”

**Actions:** `Keep my ads` / `Stop my ads`

Optional `See the rules` shows the complete model before choosing. These outcomes are fixed, not generated to punish a choice. No answer receives a green or red verdict.

### 3. Your move plays out

Replace the decision screen with its consequence. Keep the same company positions, so the learner can follow the change.

**If Stop:** Your television turns off. The 40-coin bill disappears. Customers shift toward the rival’s playing television. Your money finishes at **40 left after ads**.

**Fox follows the customers with its gaze:** “You saved 40 on ads, but lost 60 in sales. You kept less.”

A small receipt is available: 40 in − 0 on ads = 40 left.

**If Keep:** Both televisions continue. Your bill is paid; your money stays at **60 left after ads**.

**Fox nods toward the rival:** “You kept your customers—and your ad bill.”

Optional `Compare stopping` opens the authored stop outcome, labelled “What if you stopped?” It does not rewrite the learner’s actual choice.

**Same next action on either branch:** `See what the ban changes`

### 4. The other television goes dark

New scene: **“Now neither company can advertise.”**

Both TVs are off. If yours was already off, it stays off; only the rival’s changes. If yours was on, both turn off together.

Customers divide evenly. The bill spaces show **0 on ads**. Your money settles at **100 left after ads**.

**Fox, surprised smile, points across both companies:** “Now your rival can’t advertise either. You both keep more.”

**One comparison:** “Both advertised: 60 each → Neither advertises: 100 each.”

**Action:** `What made the difference?`

This is a rule change, not a player negotiating cooperation. Do not imply that voluntary cooperation has already been explained.

### 5. Let the player spot the cause

Two pictures, both labelled **“Your ads: OFF.”**

- Rival ON → You keep 40.
- Rival OFF → You keep 100.

For the Keep branch, label the first picture “If you had stopped alone.” Never pretend they chose it.

**Fox:** “You stopped advertising in both pictures. What changed?”

**Actions:** `The rival’s advertising` / `Your advertising bill`

A response opens a replacement explanation screen:

- Rival: “Yes. Your rival lost the advantage of advertising alone.”
- Bill: Highlight zero ad cost in both pictures. “Your bill was zero both times. The rival’s advertising changed.”

Then the same takeaway for everyone:

**Strategic interdependence**

“Your result depends on your choices AND other people’s choices.”

Keep the pictures visible behind the explanation. Fox points between them; no unrelated celebration.

**Action:** `Does stopping together always help?`

### 6. Make the insight accurate

**Visual:** Both televisions stay off. The customer-spending container becomes smaller; no invented new earnings figure appears.

**Fox:** “We kept total spending fixed. What if customers bought much less?”

**Actions:** `The saving guarantees a gain` / `We need to compare the amounts`

On a fresh response screen:

- Saving: “The ad bill disappears. But income could fall by even more.”
- Compare: “Exactly. Compare the income lost with the advertising cost saved.”

**For everyone:** “Stopping together can help. It isn’t a guarantee.”

**Action:** `Back to the real ban`

### 7. Resolve, then offer a next story

Return to the historical television scene. Remove all model money.

**Fox:** “Researchers found stock-market evidence that investors expected established tobacco companies to benefit.”

Next line on the same scene, replacing the first rather than adding a paragraph:

“One explanation: new brands had fewer ways to reach customers.”

**Small plain-language boundary:** “Our made-up numbers illustrate a possibility. They aren’t the historical results.”

**Closing line:** “Bad news for your options can be good news for your position—depending on what changes for everyone else.”

**Saved takeaway:** “Before changing your move, ask what others can still do.”

A completed story marker appears. Pause is available without losing progress. No claim of mastered understanding from one guided question.

**Next-story invitation:** “Your idea saves five hours. Your manager says no.”

**Action:** `Find out what worries them`

**Secondary:** `Pause here`

The next story uses investigation rather than another ad-style experiment. Preserve its already drafted evidence branches.

## Pacing and visual rules

- First visible contrast is the target for the opening minute; do not promise the entire lesson finishes in 60 seconds. No countdown or forced reading pace.
- Each committed response replaces the screen. Nothing piles up below earlier choices.
- One sentence of fox speech at a time; no typewriter delay or compulsory voice playback. Keep navigation available when the text appears.
- Customers move to show lost business. Coins move to show receipts and costs. Motion explains the model; it does not invent rewards.
- Fox stays compact near the bottom: question, point, follow customers, react, explain. No continuous jiggling. Use approved character assets later.
- Motion can be shortened or disabled; labels still communicate all changes. No dependence on colour or audio.
- Save the choice, scene and outcome. Returning does not reroll a result.

## Internal model and source notes

Full fixed model: shared spending 200, ad bill 40 per advertiser. Both on: 60/60 left. Both off: 100/100. Only one on: advertiser receives 160 and keeps 120; other receives and keeps 40. Other costs excluded. Neither company’s actual historical accounts are represented.

Source: supplied Mitchell/Mulherin paper in the attachments, read alongside the previous opening review and master script. It studies stock-market evidence and entry barriers; the coin model is our own teaching device. Do not imply observed profits of the shown size or make claims about public-health effects. Professor’s supplied transcript supports assumptions and interdependence; his cigarette lecture was not supplied.

## Editorial check

Addressed: prescribed actions; abstract financial wording; too many initial taps; generic success banner; no explanation for a correct initial choice; omitted model assumption check. Historical question and model remain explicitly distinct.

Unresolved: the numerical setup may still demand too much attention. Seven beats contain additional response screens. This is clearer text, not evidence of retention. A user can understand the comparison yet still confuse investor expectations with realised profits.

Next test: text-only walkthrough with a new learner. Observe where they need help, ask them to explain why stopping alone differs from stopping together, then offer the next story without persuasion. Do not show both variants to the same person and call it an independent first impression.
