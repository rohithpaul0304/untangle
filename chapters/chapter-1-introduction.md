## First concept reveal — What is a game?

After the learner’s first short interaction, contrast it with the professor’s ice-cream choice: fixed prices, no shortage and no bargaining, so picking a flavour in that simplified scene does not require predicting someone else.

Fox: “Here, what happens depends on what you do and what the other player does.”

Game theory: the study of situations where outcomes depend on interacting choices.

Use the exact played scene to introduce players, goals, available choices, outcomes, information and rules. A strategy is a plan for choosing what to do; later multi-stage games may need a plan covering different situations. Do not teach that any decision is a game merely because a button was pressed, or that cooperation does not count.

Required professor content: game structure; rationality as goal-directed optimisation; why it is a modelling benchmark; real-world behavioural limits; common knowledge, including shared awareness of rationality where assumed. These foundations accompany the games rather than becoming a separate opening lecture.

## Rationality — What are they trying to achieve?

Fox: “The goalkeeper is trying to stop a goal. You’re trying to score.”

Rationality: choosing consistently with goals and beliefs. Introduce the professor’s optimisation idea in plain words: trying to get the outcome one values most among the available choices.

Goals may include earning money, reducing costs, fairness, friendship, pride or avoiding conflict. Emotions do not automatically make a choice irrational. Someone can value fairness more than the largest monetary reward.

Why start with this assumption? It gives a clear benchmark for reasoning. Compare a stated goal and a possible move; then consider what changes if the person misreads the situation, has limited attention or holds a different belief.

Behavioural economics studies actual behaviour and departures from simplified predictions. Do not promise humans always behave according to the model, or turn every unexpected action into “irrationality.” Common knowledge of rationality is a separate, stronger assumption than one person being rational. Neither assumption is mandatory in every game-theory model.

## Common knowledge — Knowing that the knowledge is shared

Start with a team captain changing the assembly point. A private instruction received by one player differs from a clear public announcement to the whole team. Reveal who has heard the instruction and what each knows about others hearing it.

Common knowledge: everyone knows the fact, everyone knows that everyone knows it, and this shared awareness continues through further levels. Public announcement is an idealised example assuming everyone hears, understands and shares awareness of the announcement; ordinary hearing alone does not guarantee it.

Fox: “Knowing the instruction yourself is different from knowing it’s shared.”

The prisoner puzzle follows as a worked explanation of why shared awareness can change reasoning. It is not the first game and is not a test the learner must solve without help.

## Guided mini-story — Green-eyed prisoners

Simplified reconstruction of the puzzle referenced by the professor. The exact linked video was not supplied. Preserve its reasoning, explain unfamiliar rules before asking for a move, and avoid a threatening opening. No death or graphic punishment is needed for the lesson.

### Three rules, introduced separately

1. A prisoner can see other people’s eyes, but cannot see their own. Their own eyes might be green or another colour.
2. Only a prisoner who has worked out with certainty that their own eyes are green may leave. Departure opportunities occur together each night; everyone observes the result by morning.
3. There are no mirrors or private messages about eye colour. Everyone reasons correctly and knows the others do too. A truthful visitor speaks publicly before the first departure opportunity: “At least one of you has green eyes.”

The audience may see both faces, but a character does not know their own eye colour. Make that difference explicit instead of expecting the learner to infer it.

### Start with one prisoner

Show only one person. The visitor makes the announcement.
Fox: “There’s only one person here. Who must the visitor mean?”
Highlight that person.
Fox: “They can work out their own eyes are green. They leave the first night.”

This establishes the starting case. It is not another full puzzle to struggle through.

### Then two prisoners

Show two prisoners, both with green eyes. Each sees one green-eyed person but cannot see their own eyes. Both hear the same truthful announcement.

Take one person’s viewpoint.
Fox: “You can see their green eyes. But yours could still be another colour.”

Show that imagined possibility beside the real scene, clearly labelled “What they’re considering,” not as a fact.
Fox: “If your eyes weren’t green, they would see nobody with green eyes.”
Fox: “They’d know the visitor meant them—and leave tonight.”

Action: Watch the first night.
Neither person leaves.

Fox: “They stayed. So they must see someone else with green eyes.”
Highlight the observing prisoner.
Fox: “That someone is you.”

Switch briefly to the other person. The same reasoning applies.
Action: Watch the second night.
Both leave.

### What changed?

Fox: “The announcement started a shared chain of reasoning. Each person could learn from what the other person didn’t do.”

Keep the exact announcement “at least one,” not “everyone has green eyes.” Saying everyone has green eyes would reveal each person’s colour immediately and create a different puzzle. Explain one inference per screen; offer replay from either viewpoint.

The main path stops at two prisoners. Three or more people are optional deeper practice; no 100-person calculation is required to finish Chapter 1. An incorrect answer gets a guided comparison, not another larger puzzle.

Teacher note: before the announcement, the shared reasoning lacks its public starting point. Two people may each see at least one green-eyed person without each knowing the other has that same knowledge. The three-person extension adds another layer. In the standard idealised version, n green-eyed people leave on the nth departure opportunity after the public announcement; that generalisation is not a beginner prerequisite.

Additional verification: [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Common Knowledge](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/common-knowledge/) explains the distinction between mutual and common knowledge. It is a conceptual check, not the source of the professor’s video.

## Everyday application — Taxi payment

Show a completed taxi ride, passenger and driver. The agreed fare is visible.
Fox: “Why do people usually pay after the ride?”
Action: Look at both people.
Explore possible goals: fair payment, keeping a promise, earning a living and avoiding conflict.

Fox: “If we only count money, we may miss something they care about.”

The supplied Mostly Economics post discusses Basu’s thought experiment: explaining why a passenger pays requires considering the driver’s response too, and a purely selfish account creates difficulties. Shared values can help explain ordinary exchange. This is a thought experiment, not a survey proving everyone’s motive.

Do not teach that a fair choice is irrational, or reward fare evasion. Keep the book’s separate taxi anecdote separate: its later driver letter is explicitly invented by the authors, not real testimony.

## Final teaching game — Run a railway

Source read in full from the founder’s pasted text: Hagen Lindstädt and Jürgen Müller, “Making game theory work for managers,” McKinsey, 1 December 2009. This is a historical article and model illustration, not a description of today’s rail market or a verified forecast of what subsequently occurred.

### The idea we carry into the app

Game theory helps you compare possible actions and responses under different assumptions. It does not remove uncertainty or guarantee one winning move in every future. The authors propose examining successive choices and choosing a strategy that handles opportunities and risks across plausible conditions. They call this a “best robust option.” In our words: a plan that holds up reasonably well across the futures we are considering.

Attribute this approach to the article’s authors. Do not teach that all other game theory can only handle one fixed future; game theory also has methods for uncertainty and changing information.

### Scene 1 — A rival arrives

A rail map shows your existing network, including local feeder routes connecting to busy intercity trains. A differently coloured rival train appears on one busy route.

Labels: “Your railway” and “New railway.” Explain the roles before using incumbent or entrant; those terms simply mean established operator and newcomer.

Fox: “You run this railway. A new company wants to carry some passengers too.”
Fox: “What happens next depends on their move—and your response.”

Your goal: keep a viable, profitable service. The rival’s goal: establish a viable, profitable service of its own. Passenger choices matter too. The simplified player map models the two operators as strategic players and passenger demand as an explicit response assumption; do not claim passengers have no goals or choices in reality.

Action: Look at their service.

### Scene 2 — Understand the rival’s choices

The article identifies four entry approaches. Present four short inspectable examples, not all their explanations at once:

- Copy the existing service: similar trains and schedules.
- Compete directly: a cheaper or more frequent alternative.
- Serve a small gap: for example, selected peak-hour trips.
- Offer something different: for example, a slower, lower-cost leisure service.

Fox: “A few extra morning trains are a different challenge from a rival competing all day.”

The learner can inspect two contrasting moves. Keep schedules and prices descriptive here; do not revive the confusing lunch-price terminology or fabricate price and profit amounts from the article.

### Scene 3 — Your available responses

Keep your response choices in a stable action area:

- Keep our service as it is. The article calls this ignoring the entrant: no competitive response, not failing to notice it.
- Compete directly. Adjust fares, frequency or schedules on the challenged route.
- Make room for both. Accept some rival service and adapt the routes you operate.
- Leave this route. Stop your service there.

Fox: “Before we choose, let’s look at what could change the outcome.”

Each response is meaningful only against a stated rival move and market conditions. Do not mark a response universally correct because it has a friendly label.

### Scene 4 — Four things to investigate

The learner taps a relevant part of the map to inspect one factor at a time:

1. More passengers? A better offer might attract people who previously drove or flew. Or both railways might compete for the same existing passengers.
2. Running costs? The newcomer may operate more cheaply; a large established railway may have costly operations as well as scale advantages.
3. Useful connections? Your feeder routes may make the whole network more valuable even where an individual route earns little.
4. Price-sensitive passengers? How much cheaper would a rival need to be before passengers switch?

Fox: “Cheaper trains don’t tell us the whole story. Connections and new passengers can matter too.”

These are the four drivers discussed in the article, translated into beginner language. Show current assumptions explicitly before a decision. Changed assumptions must be visible before replay, not secretly swapped after a learner acts.

### Scene 5 — Room for both

State the conditions: you have a strong connected network; the rival proposes a small niche service; improved options attract additional rail travellers. The rival has lower operating costs, but enters only a small part of the network.

Fox: “Some of their passengers may be new train travellers. They won’t all come from your trains.”
Fox: “Would competing aggressively be worth its cost here?”

Action: choose a response, then inspect the authored case comparison.

Explain the article’s model result: under the described conditions, the established operator remains dominant; tolerating a small entry can be preferable to a costly fight over a small share. A price war could sacrifice more value than that share is worth. If the rival instead attacks aggressively, a competitive response may be preferable, though profits for both can fall.

Do not show exact money, passenger counts or numerical payoff bars until a separate consistent teaching model is reviewed. At text level, the sequence is a qualitative guided case. It does not reproduce McKinsey’s proprietary model or supply an independently verified optimal strategy.

### Scene 6 — Same map, changed conditions

Replay with a clearly announced change: total rail demand does not increase. Also show whether your network advantage is strong or weak and whether your cost disadvantage is large.

Fox: “This time, there aren’t more passengers. Both companies are competing for the same pool.”

Explain that the article’s model can then produce very different results. With limited demand and high fixed costs, two operators may not both be viable on a route. A weak established operator can lose to the newcomer; a strong one may have incentives to cut fares before entry to discourage it. When circumstances are intermediate, coexistence can remain possible.

Do not infer a unique winner from demand alone. Hold visible the other relevant factors. The lesson is sensitivity to assumptions, not “always cut prices,” “always fight,” or “always share.”

### Scene 7 — Think beyond the first move

Show a short progression: proposed entry → your response → the rival reassesses → you review your plan. Players can adjust actions as conditions and other moves become clearer.

Fox: “Your first move changes what the other company may do next.”

Let the learner inspect two possible next responses, visibly labelled as possibilities rather than certain predictions. Explain that the article compares sequences and upside/downside risks across plausible situations, not just isolated snapshots.

### Scene 8 — What would make you change your plan?

Concept reveal: “Robust strategy — a plan that accounts for risks and opportunities across plausible conditions.”

Fox: “We’re looking for a plan that can handle more than one possible future.”

A simple comparison identifies the conditions under which a response looks attractive and the conditions under which it stops looking attractive. A break point is a change large enough to make a different plan preferable. Do not invent a precise passenger or price threshold: the pasted article does not supply usable numerical thresholds.

Reflection: “Which assumption would you want to check before choosing?” The learner may inspect demand, costs, connections or price sensitivity. Several choices are defensible; the fox explains why the selected information matters rather than awarding a single correct answer.

### Transfer from the article’s other examples

Machinery: the article describes established firms facing a potential new multipurpose factory. Different assumptions could support price cuts, waiting to react, or leaving the newcomer a limited niche. Use this as a short additional contrast showing that the rail lesson is not restricted to trains.

The authors also mention applying their approach to pharmaceuticals and chemicals, but provide too little detail here for us to claim a complete additional reviewed game. Record those examples as further applications, not invented simulations attributed to McKinsey.

### Learner takeaway

Fox: “Understanding the other player matters. Understanding what our prediction depends on matters too.”

The learner should recognise that entry may grow the overall market, network connections matter, a rival can adjust its move, and a recommended response can change when assumptions change.

Source link: [Making game theory work for managers](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/making-game-theory-work-for-managers). Supplied article includes textual descriptions and captions of Exhibits 1 and 2 but not their images; do not claim the plotted relationships or numerical values have been inspected. Its rail analysis describes model scenarios and strategic implications, not observed proof of their outcomes.

## Ending 1 — MCQ understanding check

After the railway capstone, show twelve multiple-choice questions, one per screen. These are deliberately different from the game journey. They check the starting assumptions, interacting choices, goals and values, common knowledge, looking ahead, responses, risk incentives, commitments, collective action, unpredictability, private information and robust decisions.

Fox: “Let’s see what stuck—and what needs another look.”

Give a short reviewed explanation after each response. AI uses the chosen option and previous evidence to recommend a useful explanation or fresh follow-up. A correct definition does not prove successful application; a wrong option is a provisional clue, not a confident diagnosis.

Question example: “Which two assumptions did we introduce as a starting model?” Correct: rationality and common knowledge. This qualification preserves the professor’s course model without falsely claiming every game-theory model requires these assumptions.

Question example: “Which situation requires considering another person’s choice?” A meeting that succeeds only if two separately chosen destinations match is strategic; a fixed personal preference with no interaction is not. Avoid an ambiguous bare action such as turning left with no context.

## Ending 2 — Separate practice quiz

A distinct practice activity comes AFTER the MCQ understanding check. New contexts; no copied question-and-answer repeats. Default text proposal: twelve questions selected from twenty-four authored variants, one per objective. Extended practice can use additional unrevealed variants; no short-duration constraint.

Fox: “Now try these ideas somewhere new.”

Independent first attempt: no answer or hint shown before submitting. Provide the score, explanations and concept-specific review after the set, not between items that could reveal each other’s answers. Let a learner leave to get help, but record that attempt as assisted rather than independent.

The AI may change question order, choose eligible variants and propose targeted reviewed explanations. It does not invent the correct option or silently change rules. Correctness comes from published keys. Convex will store the question version, response, evidence type, feedback exposure, and next-question reason. This backend behaviour is a design requirement, not implemented functionality.

Missed concept → one short explanation or scene replay → a new unrevealed variant in a new attempt. Correct fresh application → move on. Repeated difficulty → change the teaching approach, offer pause/resume, preserve progress. Do not trap the learner in endless near-identical questions. No arbitrary three-follow-up cap.

Keep the first submitted score, corrected scores and hinted retries separate. Never make a revealed-item retry count as fresh understanding. Do not punish hints by removing XP. Do not invent a new unlock percentage; existing access rules remain pending a separate completion-policy decision.

## Final recap — What you learned

Name the concepts again and connect them to actual scenes: the hiding player anticipated a search; the defender changed an opportunity; the leader and follower wanted different things; a locked offer removed flexibility; private information affected an offer; a railway response depended on market conditions.

Personalise the evidence: “You recognised interacting choices and compared two demand scenarios.” Where evidence is unresolved: “Next, practise distinguishing what everyone knows from what is common knowledge.” Avoid a mastery claim based solely on completing guided actions or getting lucky in a game.

Chapter 2 bridge: “What if both people understand their choices, yet each has a reason to take a move that leaves both worse off?”
