## Learning contract

Every meaningful scene introduces an idea, reveals a consequence, or checks understanding. No filler rounds solely for coins. Use clear rules before action, a consequence the learner can see, a short concept explanation after discovery, and a changed situation that tests the limits of the idea. Everyday transfer must reveal a mechanism, not merely advise people to plan ahead or communicate.

One short fox prompt and one clear action at a time. The fox has purposeful expressions and gestures, stays compact at the bottom, and never reveals every independent answer. Do not equate a round win with understanding. A first good choice must not be punished to force a twist.

The learner can pause during play, explanations and assessments. Resume the same problem and committed result. The chapter may be long; sessions do not have to be.

## Complete journey


---

### Other people have plans

- **Find my hidden number:** Search, then hide from a known guessing rule. Learn: Players, goals and interacting choices.
- **Win the final:** Choose whether to stay in a tournament round; inspect later consequences. Learn: Think beyond the next win.
- **One more basket:** Shoot or pass; defenders change position; choose again. Learn: Other players react to your choices.
### Your goal changes your move

- **Lead or catch up:** Choose a sailing lane, then swap leader and follower roles. Learn: The same move can serve different goals.
- **A binding offer:** Compare flexible acceptance with a minimum-offer rule; then change the partner’s alternative. Learn: Commitments change others’ choices—and can block a deal.
- **Help your future self:** Arrange a work period; discover when an exception matters. Learn: Change future choices without losing necessary flexibility.
- **Who goes first?:** Volunteer or wait; inspect shared benefits and a new incentive. Learn: Individual costs can block a shared benefit.
### What do they know and value?

- **Do we both know?:** Compare private messages with a visible shared signal. Learn: Knowing differs from knowing others know.
- **Don’t become predictable:** Play rock–paper–scissors against different opponent rules. Learn: A repeating pattern is different from random choice.
- **The Mystery Box:** Bid, reveal, compare estimates; then buy a useful wheel. Learn: Winning may select optimism; personal use changes value.
- **The other person isn’t you:** Explore the taxi viewpoints and why someone might reject an unequal offer. Learn: Rationality depends on goals, including fairness.
### Put it together

- **Run a railway · McKinsey:** Inspect a new rival, respond, change market conditions, reconsider. Learn: A plan depends on assumptions and possible responses.
- **Check what stuck:** 12 MCQs, with an explanation after each response. Learn: Identify understanding and concepts to revisit.
- **Try it somewhere new:** Separate 12-question practice drawn from 24 reviewed starter variants. Learn: Apply ideas in fresh settings; review follows submission.

## Foundations woven into play

## First concept reveal — What is a game?

After the learner’s first short interaction, contrast it with the professor’s ice-cream choice: fixed prices, no shortage and no bargaining, so picking a flavour in that simplified scene does not require predicting someone else.

Fox: “Here, what happens depends on what you do and what the other player does.”

Game theory: the study of situations where outcomes depend on interacting choices.

Use the exact played scene to introduce players, goals, available choices, outcomes, information and rules. A strategy is a plan for choosing what to do; later multi-stage games may need a plan covering different situations. Do not teach that any decision is a game merely because a button was pressed, or that cooperation does not count.

Required professor content: game structure; rationality as goal-directed optimisation; why it is a modelling benchmark; real-world behavioural limits; common knowledge, including shared awareness of rationality where assumed. These foundations accompany the games rather than becoming a separate opening lecture.

## Rationality — What are they trying to achieve?

Fox: “The goalkeeper is trying to stop a goal. You’re trying to score.”

Rationality: choosing consistently with goals and beliefs. Introduce the professor’s optimisation idea in plain words: trying to get the outcome one values most among the available choices.

Goals may include earning money, reducing costs, fairness, friendship, pride or avoiding conflict. Emotions do not automatically make a choice irrational. Someone can value fairness more than the largest monetary reward.

Why start with this assumption? It gives a clear benchmark for reasoning. Compare a stated goal and a possible move; then consider what changes if the person misreads the situation, has limited attention or holds a different belief.

Behavioural economics studies actual behaviour and departures from simplified predictions. Do not promise humans always behave according to the model, or turn every unexpected action into “irrationality.” Common knowledge of rationality is a separate, stronger assumption than one person being rational. Neither assumption is mandatory in every game-theory model.

## Common knowledge — Knowing that the knowledge is shared

Start with a team captain changing the assembly point. A private instruction received by one player differs from a clear public announcement to the whole team. Reveal who has heard the instruction and what each knows about others hearing it.

Common knowledge: everyone knows the fact, everyone knows that everyone knows it, and this shared awareness continues through further levels. Public announcement is an idealised example assuming everyone hears, understands and shares awareness of the announcement; ordinary hearing alone does not guarantee it.

Fox: “Knowing the instruction yourself is different from knowing it’s shared.”

The prisoner puzzle follows as a worked explanation of why shared awareness can change reasoning. It is not the first game and is not a test the learner must solve without help.

## Guided mini-story — Green-eyed prisoners

Simplified reconstruction of the puzzle referenced by the professor. The exact linked video was not supplied. Preserve its reasoning, explain unfamiliar rules before asking for a move, and avoid a threatening opening. No death or graphic punishment is needed for the lesson.

### Three rules, introduced separately

1. A prisoner can see other people’s eyes, but cannot see their own. Their own eyes might be green or another colour.
2. Only a prisoner who has worked out with certainty that their own eyes are green may leave. Departure opportunities occur together each night; everyone observes the result by morning.
3. There are no mirrors or private messages about eye colour. Everyone reasons correctly and knows the others do too. A truthful visitor speaks publicly before the first departure opportunity: “At least one of you has green eyes.”

The audience may see both faces, but a character does not know their own eye colour. Make that difference explicit instead of expecting the learner to infer it.

### Start with one prisoner

Show only one person. The visitor makes the announcement.
Fox: “There’s only one person here. Who must the visitor mean?”
Highlight that person.
Fox: “They can work out their own eyes are green. They leave the first night.”

This establishes the starting case. It is not another full puzzle to struggle through.

### Then two prisoners

Show two prisoners, both with green eyes. Each sees one green-eyed person but cannot see their own eyes. Both hear the same truthful announcement.

Take one person’s viewpoint.
Fox: “You can see their green eyes. But yours could still be another colour.”

Show that imagined possibility beside the real scene, clearly labelled “What they’re considering,” not as a fact.
Fox: “If your eyes weren’t green, they would see nobody with green eyes.”
Fox: “They’d know the visitor meant them—and leave tonight.”

Action: Watch the first night.
Neither person leaves.

Fox: “They stayed. So they must see someone else with green eyes.”
Highlight the observing prisoner.
Fox: “That someone is you.”

Switch briefly to the other person. The same reasoning applies.
Action: Watch the second night.
Both leave.

### What changed?

Fox: “The announcement started a shared chain of reasoning. Each person could learn from what the other person didn’t do.”

Keep the exact announcement “at least one,” not “everyone has green eyes.” Saying everyone has green eyes would reveal each person’s colour immediately and create a different puzzle. Explain one inference per screen; offer replay from either viewpoint.

The main path stops at two prisoners. Three or more people are optional deeper practice; no 100-person calculation is required to finish Chapter 1. An incorrect answer gets a guided comparison, not another larger puzzle.

Teacher note: before the announcement, the shared reasoning lacks its public starting point. Two people may each see at least one green-eyed person without each knowing the other has that same knowledge. The three-person extension adds another layer. In the standard idealised version, n green-eyed people leave on the nth departure opportunity after the public announcement; that generalisation is not a beginner prerequisite.

Additional verification: [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Common Knowledge](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/common-knowledge/) explains the distinction between mutual and common knowledge. It is a conceptual check, not the source of the professor’s video.

## Game 1 — Find my hidden number

Book connection: #1, Pick a Number. Concept: strategic interdependence and taking another person’s perspective.

### Scene and goal

Twelve numbered stones, 1–12, sit on a small game table. A machine or another character chooses a secret number. You have three guesses. The secret is locked before the first guess. After each miss, the table truthfully says higher or lower, and removes impossible stones. Finding it wins the round; missing reveals it without losing chapter progress. These small numbers and three guesses are our rules, not the book’s original numerical sequence.

Fox: “Find the hidden number. You have three guesses.”
Action: tap a numbered stone. Remaining guesses are visible.

### Two different choosers

Round A: a machine chooses uniformly at random. Fox: “The machine doesn’t care whether you win.” No simulated human motive is assigned to it. A middle guess helps narrow the remaining range; do not promise it always wins within three guesses.

Round B: a character chooses the number and wants it to remain hidden. Fox: “This player wants to make your search difficult.” The same truthful higher/lower rules apply. The character cannot move the number after seeing guesses. Its reviewed policy and any information about the learner’s previous play are recorded before the round.

### Switch roles

You hide a number. The machine opponent uses a disclosed fixed search rule: choose the lower middle of the remaining interval. For example, a hidden 12 survives guesses 6, 9 and 11. Any other selected number must follow the same truthful interval update, not a scripted sequence imposed regardless of the number.

Fox: “You could think about how the searcher would act before choosing your hiding place.”

### Concept reveal

Game theory: outcomes depend on interacting choices. Perspective-taking: consider the other player’s goal and how they may act.

Fox: “A person choosing against you is different from a machine choosing at random.”

Practice: change the searcher’s published rule or let the opponent consider a different guess pattern. Do not teach 12 as universally best against people or imply that one lucky find establishes understanding. Early scores measure the round outcome only; assess reasoning separately.

## Game 2 — Win the final, not every round

Book connection: #2, Winning by Losing, the Survivor example. Concept: looking ahead and distinguishing a local win from the final goal.

### Scene and goal

Three contestants remain in an original camp tournament: You, Strong competitor and Popular competitor. Clear labels matter more than names. First comes a balance challenge; its winner chooses an opponent for a final audience vote. You previously promised the popular contestant a place in the final if you get to choose.

Fox: “Winning this challenge lets you choose the finalist. But the audience decides the champion.”

Reveal the relevant assumptions one at a time: the popular contestant is expected to win the audience vote against either rival; the strong contestant is expected to beat them in the balance challenge; the strong contestant prefers you as a final opponent; breaking your visible promise may lose audience support.

### Action and consequences

Actions: Stay in the challenge / Step down. Staying can later lead to choosing a finalist; stepping down lets the remaining two compete. Show possible branches before presenting a retrospective result. In one reviewed scenario, Strong competitor wins, chooses you and the audience favours you. If Popular competitor wins, they choose you and are expected to win the vote. If you win, choosing Popular competitor risks losing to their popularity; choosing Strong competitor risks losing votes over the broken promise.

Fox: “Sometimes winning the next round makes the final harder.”

### Concept reveal

Looking ahead: consider how your move changes later choices and outcomes. Your objective is winning the final, not maximising challenge wins.

Practice: Strong competitor becomes less likely to win the challenge. Reconsider stepping down. The book itself discusses waiting as an alternative; neither early withdrawal nor staying is universally correct. No fixed historical probabilities or guaranteed votes are supplied by the book. Our authored branch outcomes must be explicit before implementation, with uncertainty distinguished from deterministic game rules. This is a strategic planning game, not a reflex test or a promise that losing always helps.

## Game 3 — Pass to the open teammate

Book connection: #3, The Hot Hand. Concept: opponents respond, and individual success can help the team indirectly.

### Scene and rules

A basketball court shows You, Star teammate, Open teammate and two defenders. This is a diagram-like game with clear paths, not a realistic physics simulation. In the first teaching setup, a directly marked shot is blocked and an unmarked shot scores. State that simplified rule before play.

Fox: “Both defenders are watching our star. Who has space?”
Actions: Shoot with the star / Pass to the open teammate. The chosen character performs the action; defenders move; the result appears at the basket.

### Changed situation

Defenders notice the passing play and spread out. The star now has space while the previous receiver is marked.
Fox: “They changed their defence. Look again before passing.”
Actions remain tied to the actual scene. Do not keep the old answer correct after the defence changes.

### Concept reveal

Strategic response: another player can change their behaviour in reaction to yours. Team goals may favour a pass even if it reduces the star’s own shot attempts.

Practice: swap which teammate is marked and ask the learner to use the new arrangement. Assess noticing the changed defence, not memorising “always pass.” The old hot-hand statistics in the book are not a current scientific claim we need to repeat; our lesson concerns defensive reactions, not whether streak shooting exists.

## Game 4 — Protect a lead or catch up

Book connection: #4, To Lead or Not to Lead, the sailing example. Concept: goals and relative position change risk incentives.

### Scene and rules

Two identical boats race towards a finish. Yours starts ahead. A fork leads through left and right wind lanes. The trailing boat takes the left lane first; you can see its choice. In our simplified rules, equal boats following the same lane keep their order. Different lanes expose them to different wind and can change the order. No collision, acceleration advantage or hidden skill roll is added.

Fox: “You’re ahead. Do you want the fastest possible trip—or to stay ahead of that boat?”
Actions: Follow their lane / Take the other lane.

Run a disclosed wind condition. Following preserves the lead under the stated model; splitting may help or harm relative position. Replay with the same wind to compare, explicitly labelled as a controlled comparison.

### Switch positions

Now you are behind. Copying the leader preserves the deficit under these same rules; a different lane gives a chance to change the order, with a risk of falling further behind.

### Concept reveal

Goal-dependent strategy: a leader protecting position and a challenger trying to overtake can prefer different risks.

Practice: change the stated goal to fastest arrival rather than beating the other boat, and consider how the choice changes. Do not teach that leaders should always imitate in real sailing or business; the book highlights contextual limits. No universal optimal move is claimed without the wind information and objective.

## Stage 5 — A binding offer

## 2. Can removing a choice get you a better offer?

Placement: strengthen the commitment stage, replacing the current obviously bad locked-ticket example as its main event. Source: tale 5, Here I Stand. This numerical interaction is our model, not the book's historical negotiation.

Play: a partner proposes how to split ten game tokens. Your stated goal in this round is your own token count; the partner's stated goal is theirs. In the flexible version, you can accept a positive offer or reject it and both receive zero. There is one offer and no later negotiation. Explain that the partner is a scripted, goal-directed model, not a prediction of all people.

Before the offer, you may install a visible rule that automatically rejects offers below four tokens for you. This changes what the partner knows can be accepted. With integer offers, a partner seeking the largest own share and expecting you to accept any positive amount offers one in the flexible model and four with the binding rule. Show the two sequences side by side. Rejection of zero is an explicit tie rule in the model. A spoken claim that you will reject is not the same as the enforced rule.

Reveal: the rule removed your ability to accept one, but changed the other player's offer. The gain came from their response to a credible restriction, not stubbornness by itself.

Changed situation: the partner has an outside option worth seven tokens. A split leaving them only six no longer beats that alternative. Holding the same rule may mean no deal. State the outside option before the learner chooses a commitment. Show both outcomes without changing rules after the choice.

Concept: credible commitment — a restriction others believe will really bind your future actions. It can influence their choices and also destroy a possible agreement.

Everyday connection: agreeing to a genuine minimum contribution before organising a shared activity changes what others can expect from you. Do not teach fake claims about authority, coercion or that hard rules always improve relationships. Keep fairness and repeated relationships visible as later limits.

## Stage 6 — Help your future self

## Game 6 — Help your future self

Book connection: #6, Thinning Strategically. Concept: self-commitment and changing future incentives or options.

### Scene and goal

An original character wants to finish a small stage prop before an evening show. They also want to play a game now. A planning screen shows the goal and the likely distraction. Avoid the book’s dieting, body-shaming or public humiliation mechanisms.

Fox: “You care about finishing. Later, the distraction may feel more tempting.”
Actions: Leave everything as it is / Arrange a protected work period. The second action temporarily places the distraction out of reach in the story; it does not change the user’s actual phone settings.

### Consequences

Run two clearly labelled authored possibilities: an uninterrupted work period supports progress; leaving the temptation available allows a competing choice to interrupt it. The learner can inspect both immediate enjoyment and later project progress. These are modelled behaviours, not a prediction that the real learner will lack self-control.

Then introduce an urgent, legitimate need to contact someone. A completely inflexible arrangement can make that harder; a narrow restriction with an emergency exception can preserve the goal while allowing necessary action.

### Concept reveal

Self-commitment: make a plan now that changes what your future self can do or finds attractive. Fewer options can sometimes support a goal, but flexibility has value too.

Practice: choose between a helpful arrangement and an unrelated restriction. Do not score a learner’s actual personal priorities or reward missing an urgent need. Explain the mechanism rather than promise a productivity result.

## Stage 7 — Who goes first?

## Game 7 — Who goes first?

Book connection: #7, Buffett’s Dilemma and collective action. Concept: incentives and coordinating a group. Formal prisoner’s dilemma remains in Chapter 2.

### Scene and rules

Two camp teams want a clean shared campsite and also value their break. One team can clean it; doing so takes that team’s break. If at least one team helps, both enjoy a clean camp. If neither helps, the camp stays dirty. If both help, both spend their breaks and the camp is clean. Display both the shared result and each team’s effort—no fake score hiding the cost.

Fox: “Everyone wants a clean camp. Everyone would also like someone else to do the work.”
Actions: Volunteer our team / Wait for the other team. The partner team uses a disclosed authored action in guided practice and a reviewed hidden policy in independent practice. Show all outcome branches, without declaring one action rational for every possible set of preferences.

### Change the incentive

A camp organiser introduces a visible reward for helpers. The learner compares which costs and benefits changed, then considers whether a team might change its move. Do not force a changed action without a stated preference model.

### Concept reveal

Collective action: a group may benefit from an action while each member has a reason to wait for someone else. Incentives change the appeal of actions. This clean-camp adaptation is not mathematically the same game as Buffett’s proposal or the Chapter 2 prisoner’s dilemma.

### The original book connection

Explain Buffett’s hypothetical proposal in a short source card: a promised large donation to the side that most supported reform if reform failed could change the political stakes. It was an imagined incentive scheme, not a real donation or a general proof that any reward creates cooperation. The political objective involved relative competitive advantage, not just each side’s cash. Preserve those limits; do not replace them with a made-up cash table attributed to Buffett.

Practice: identify which changed incentive could plausibly affect a team’s choice. The purpose is understanding motives, not shaming a learner for weighing effort against shared benefit. Chapter 2 later formalises a specific dilemma with reviewed payoffs.

## Stage 8 — Do we both know?

## 4. Everybody knows. Why is everybody still waiting?

Placement: improve the shared-knowledge opening; retain the optional small green-eyed puzzle. Source: professor's distinction between knowing and knowing that others know.

Play: two teammates must press their start buttons together. Each privately receives “the equipment is ready,” but neither can see whether the other received it. Show both viewpoints to the learner while clearly distinguishing the player's overview from each character's knowledge. Starting alone fails the team task under the announced rules. Characters are waiting for confirmation of readiness and a shared start signal, not lacking the readiness fact themselves.

A public countdown is now seen and heard by both. Make mutual visibility explicit. The equipment has not changed; the characters can now coordinate on a start. The countdown supplies a coordination signal as well as shared awareness, so do not attribute the entire effect solely to common knowledge of readiness.

Concept reveal: distinguish shared facts, knowledge about others' information, and coordination. In the ideal model, everyone observing and understanding the same public event, with shared awareness of that observation, can establish common knowledge. A normal notification or two read receipts does not automatically prove infinitely nested shared awareness.

Transfer: compare private messages cancelling an old meeting location with a shared announcement and an agreed confirmation process. Ask what uncertainty each step removes, not whether pressing “send” magically ensures coordination.

Use the guided green-eyed one/two-person example when demonstrating the pure information reasoning more precisely. Its exact announcement remains “at least one,” never “everyone.”

## Stage 9 — Don’t become predictable

## Game 8 — Don’t become predictable

Book connection: #8, Mix Your Plays. Concept: unpredictability and mixed strategies at an intuitive level.

### Scene and rules

Play rock–paper–scissors against a labelled opponent. Ordinary rules apply; ties are visibly distinguished from losses. Both choices are committed before reveal. The opponent never reads the learner’s unrevealed current move.

First opponent follows an explicitly disclosed Rock every round policy. The learner can choose Paper to exploit that exact policy. Then switch to an opponent allowed to learn from disclosed past rounds. If the learner repeats a move, a later counter-move can exploit it. Adaptation must use real history, not a fabricated pattern.

Fox: “A move that works against a predictable player may stop working against a different player.”
Actions: Rock / Paper / Scissors. Later add Shuffle my move, explained as an equal-chance random selection before the opponent’s current move is revealed.

### Concept reveal

Unpredictability makes it harder for an opponent to exploit your pattern. A mixed strategy chooses among actions according to probabilities, not a fixed visible rotation.

Practice: compare Rock–Paper–Scissors–Rock in a publicly announced cycle with genuine random selection. Equal random choice is the appropriate baseline for this symmetric game; it does not guarantee winning any round or exact one-third results in a short run. Defer calculating mixed equilibria to a later chapter. Do not infer a universal opponent policy from three observed moves.

## Stage 10 — The Mystery Box: full storyboard

## Learning promise

Before: “If I beat the other buyers, I did well.”
After: “Winning and getting a good deal are different. If we are estimating the same unknown value, winning can mean my estimate was unusually optimistic.”

Transfer: distinguish an uncertain resale value shared by buyers from a known use that is particularly valuable to one buyer. Do not teach that winning always implies a mistake, that the lowest estimate is correct, or that an arbitrary fixed discount guarantees a good bid.

## World and roles

A small repair market. One sealed box of reusable parts, two other buyers, and the learner’s workbench. Supporting characters have plain role labels, Buyer 1 and Buyer 2. The approved fox sits at the bottom and gestures towards the relevant object; it is the learner’s guide, not an auction rival.

The scene uses practice coins only. Start with 100 practice coins, labelled “This round.” Never deduct lesson rewards or account currency. No timer, flashing urgency, real purchase or forced loss. The market keeps the part values fixed throughout the round.

## Fixed rules for the author

- Everyone buys this first box only to resell its contents. A resale counter pays the same total to any buyer: 60 coins, initially hidden.
- Three estimate cards are imperfect: yours 80, Buyer 1's 40, Buyer 2's 60. Your estimate is not a promised price. The other cards are hidden until the bids are revealed.
- In this worked round, the two rivals bid their estimates: 40 and 60. This is a simple scripted policy, not optimal bidding. Explain before commitment: “These two buyers bid their own estimates.”
- You choose an integer bid from 1 to 100, or skip. Nothing is preselected. Highest bid wins and pays exactly that bid. Others pay nothing. If bids tie, Buyer 2 has priority, then Buyer 1, then you. Display a concise rule beside the control: “Equal bids: earlier registered buyer wins.” Rival order and tie treatment are available in the rules panel before commitment.
- A bid is final only after pressing “Seal my bid.” No rival, value or estimate changes in response to it. Replay compares the same authored case and is labelled as such; it is not fresh learning evidence.

## Scene 1 — What are we trying to do?

Visual: the sealed box arrives on the market table. A resale-counter icon sits beyond it. Your 100 practice coins rest in a tray.

Fox: “Buy the box, then sell the parts. Can you finish with more coins?”

Action: “Inspect the box.”

Learning: the goal is a profitable result, not ownership alone. No theory term yet.

## Scene 2 — An estimate is not a price tag

Visual: tap your estimate card. It reads “Estimated resale: 80” and, directly underneath, “Could be wrong.” The actual resale value is still hidden. Two rivals have face-down estimate cards.

Fox: “Your estimate says 80. The other buyers have estimates too.”

A second short beat, if needed: “Nobody has opened the box.”

Action: “Choose my bid.”

Learning: everyone faces uncertainty about the same object; nobody has been promised an 80-coin sale. A player can revisit this card before committing.

## Scene 3 — Your move

Visual: move practice coins into your bid tray with accessible minus/plus controls or a number entry. Show “Pay if you win: [bid]” and “Coins left after purchase: [100 − bid].” Do not label estimated profit as guaranteed profit.

Rules above the action: “Highest bid gets the box. Winner pays their bid. Everyone else keeps their coins.”

Fox: “What will you offer?”

Actions: “Seal my bid” or “Skip this box.” Back/inspect remain available before sealing. An unset bid cannot be submitted. No suggested winning bid, green recommended number or answer hint.

Learning: choosing what to pay is separate from estimating the contents. No score labels this uncertain decision right or wrong.

## Scene 4 — Did you win?

Visual: envelopes open together. Reveal rival bids 40 and 60 and your actual bid or skip. Move the box to the actual winner. Transfer only the winner’s coins to the seller, with a clear ledger.

If your bid exceeds 60, you win. Fox: “The box is yours. Now let's see what it sells for.”

If your bid is 60 or lower, or you skipped, Buyer 2 wins at 60 under the stated tie rule. Fox: “They got the box. Let's see what they bought.”

Do not celebrate ownership as success. Do not change the rivals to make the learner win. Everyone proceeds to inspect the contents, including learners who did not buy.

Action: “Open the box.”

Learning: bid outcome is only part of the result.

## Scene 5 — The result you actually care about

Visual: the box opens and the resale counter totals the parts to 60 coins. If you bought it, show exactly two coin movements: purchase payment out, resale payment in. Keep the ledger visible after animation.

For a bid of 75:

- Paid: 75
- Sold for: 60
- Round result: −15
- Your practice coins: 85

Fox: “You won the box, but paid 15 more than it sold for.”

For any winning bid b, profit is 60 − b and final wallet is 160 − b. Use the actual amount in dialogue. No fake +15 celebration or lesson-XP deduction.

If you did not buy: show “Your change: 0” and “Your practice coins: 100.” Buyer 2 paid 60 and received 60, so their result is 0. Fox: “Keeping your coins wasn't a loss. Winning the box wouldn't automatically mean a profit.” Do not claim the learner knew its value or proved skill by declining.

Action: “Compare our estimates.”

Learning: evaluate the goal using the net result, with price and resale clearly separated.

## Scene 6 — Why did winning matter?

Visual: align three estimate cards above one actual-value marker: 40, 60, 80 versus actual 60. Animate no additional coin rewards here.

Fox, one thought per beat:

“We were all estimating the same box.”
“Your estimate was the highest.”
“In this round, that estimate was too high.”

Then show a separate, clearly labelled illustrative estimate line where everyone underestimated a different box. This is an explanation image, not a rerolled result: actual value 90, estimates 50, 60, 80. The highest estimate need not be too high. No additional auction is implied.

Fox: “The highest estimate isn't always wrong. But winning is a reason to check your confidence.”

Concept card: “Winner's curse.” Explanation: “When people estimate the same unknown value, the winner may be the most optimistic buyer—and may pay too much.”

Teacher limit: this authored round illustrates the mechanism; no optimal bid, probability or expected return is derivable from these three cards alone. The important selection effect is conditional on winning among uncertain estimates. Do not define every bad purchase as winner’s curse.

Learning: connect winning to selected optimism while preventing the opposite false rule, “winners always overpay.”

## Scene 7 — Same market, different reason to buy

Start a separate labelled scenario with a fresh 100 practice coins. Do not disguise the reset as a refund or reward.

Visual: an inspected replacement wheel, your cart with a broken wheel, and a repair-service quote of 90. The wheel fits, is guaranteed to work in the story, and you can install it without another cost. If you buy it you avoid the 90-coin repair. You want the cheaper way to get the same working cart. Two other buyers want only to resell it; its known resale price is 60 for everyone.

Fox: “This wheel saves you a 90-coin repair. The other buyers only want to resell it.”

One rival has already made a standing bid of 60. You are offered a final 70-coin bid; the rivals have finished bidding. These changed auction rules are visible before action, not sprung after it. Actions: “Buy for 70” / “Use the 90-coin repair.”

Buy: wheel repairs cart, cost 70, wallet 30. Compared with the equally effective repair, you save 20. Show saving as a comparison, not an extra 20-coin payment into the wallet.

Repair: the service repairs cart, cost 90, wallet 10. Show the wheel alternative would have cost 20 less under the stated rules. Learner can compare without shame or lost chapter progress.

Fox: “The wheel sells for 60, but it saves you 90. Your reason for buying is different.”

Concept: personal use can make an item worth more to one buyer. Paying more than others is not by itself evidence of a mistake. This scene is a guided contrast; one prompted choice is not independent proof of understanding.

## Stage 11 — The other person isn’t you

## Game 10 — The other person isn’t you

Book connection: #10, Game Theory Can Be Dangerous to Your Health. Additional source: Mostly Economics taxi-payment thought experiment. Concept: preferences, norms and checking assumptions about people.

### Scene and actions

An original passenger arranges a van ride after a camp event. Show the driver’s offered fare and whether it was agreed before the trip. No trapping, threatening driving or confrontation mechanic.

Fox: “Before deciding what to say, check what each person believes was agreed.”
Actions: Inspect the passenger’s view / Inspect the driver’s view. Each view contains stated information, not the fox inventing a real person’s mind. Passenger values reaching home, money and time. Driver values fair payment, time and respectful treatment. Their goals need not have the same ranking.

A guided decision offers Agree a clear fare before riding / Leave the fare unclear. Consequences show the extra uncertainty created by missing agreement. Later, compare a reasonable accepted offer with a disagreement; an authored response may depend on stated values as well as money. Show the character model before using it to score a factual inference.

### Concept reveal

Perspective-taking means considering their goals and beliefs, not assuming they have yours. Rationality need not mean maximising cash; fairness and pride can be part of what someone values. A model can miss important motives or context.

Follow with the short taxi-payment story in chapter-1-introduction.md. Treat it as a thought experiment about values in exchange, not a morality quiz or a proven account of every taxi ride. The book’s purported driver letter is explicitly fictional; identify it as the authors’ imagined alternative viewpoint. Do not repeat it as evidence from an actual driver.

Practice: a new character knowingly accepts less money to honour a promise. Explain why that can fit their goals. Keep passenger-payment fairness distinct from the false rule that any emotional action is irrational.

## Stage 12 — McKinsey: full scenario

## Final teaching game — Run a railway

Source read in full from the founder’s pasted text: Hagen Lindstädt and Jürgen Müller, “Making game theory work for managers,” McKinsey, 1 December 2009. This is a historical article and model illustration, not a description of today’s rail market or a verified forecast of what subsequently occurred.

### The idea we carry into the app

Game theory helps you compare possible actions and responses under different assumptions. It does not remove uncertainty or guarantee one winning move in every future. The authors propose examining successive choices and choosing a strategy that handles opportunities and risks across plausible conditions. They call this a “best robust option.” In our words: a plan that holds up reasonably well across the futures we are considering.

Attribute this approach to the article’s authors. Do not teach that all other game theory can only handle one fixed future; game theory also has methods for uncertainty and changing information.

### Scene 1 — A rival arrives

A rail map shows your existing network, including local feeder routes connecting to busy intercity trains. A differently coloured rival train appears on one busy route.

Labels: “Your railway” and “New railway.” Explain the roles before using incumbent or entrant; those terms simply mean established operator and newcomer.

Fox: “You run this railway. A new company wants to carry some passengers too.”
Fox: “What happens next depends on their move—and your response.”

Your goal: keep a viable, profitable service. The rival’s goal: establish a viable, profitable service of its own. Passenger choices matter too. The simplified player map models the two operators as strategic players and passenger demand as an explicit response assumption; do not claim passengers have no goals or choices in reality.

Action: Look at their service.

### Scene 2 — Understand the rival’s choices

The article identifies four entry approaches. Present four short inspectable examples, not all their explanations at once:

- Copy the existing service: similar trains and schedules.
- Compete directly: a cheaper or more frequent alternative.
- Serve a small gap: for example, selected peak-hour trips.
- Offer something different: for example, a slower, lower-cost leisure service.

Fox: “A few extra morning trains are a different challenge from a rival competing all day.”

The learner can inspect two contrasting moves. Keep schedules and prices descriptive here; do not revive the confusing lunch-price terminology or fabricate price and profit amounts from the article.

### Scene 3 — Your available responses

Keep your response choices in a stable action area:

- Keep our service as it is. The article calls this ignoring the entrant: no competitive response, not failing to notice it.
- Compete directly. Adjust fares, frequency or schedules on the challenged route.
- Make room for both. Accept some rival service and adapt the routes you operate.
- Leave this route. Stop your service there.

Fox: “Before we choose, let’s look at what could change the outcome.”

Each response is meaningful only against a stated rival move and market conditions. Do not mark a response universally correct because it has a friendly label.

### Scene 4 — Four things to investigate

The learner taps a relevant part of the map to inspect one factor at a time:

1. More passengers? A better offer might attract people who previously drove or flew. Or both railways might compete for the same existing passengers.
2. Running costs? The newcomer may operate more cheaply; a large established railway may have costly operations as well as scale advantages.
3. Useful connections? Your feeder routes may make the whole network more valuable even where an individual route earns little.
4. Price-sensitive passengers? How much cheaper would a rival need to be before passengers switch?

Fox: “Cheaper trains don’t tell us the whole story. Connections and new passengers can matter too.”

These are the four drivers discussed in the article, translated into beginner language. Show current assumptions explicitly before a decision. Changed assumptions must be visible before replay, not secretly swapped after a learner acts.

### Scene 5 — Room for both

State the conditions: you have a strong connected network; the rival proposes a small niche service; improved options attract additional rail travellers. The rival has lower operating costs, but enters only a small part of the network.

Fox: “Some of their passengers may be new train travellers. They won’t all come from your trains.”
Fox: “Would competing aggressively be worth its cost here?”

Action: choose a response, then inspect the authored case comparison.

Explain the article’s model result: under the described conditions, the established operator remains dominant; tolerating a small entry can be preferable to a costly fight over a small share. A price war could sacrifice more value than that share is worth. If the rival instead attacks aggressively, a competitive response may be preferable, though profits for both can fall.

Do not show exact money, passenger counts or numerical payoff bars until a separate consistent teaching model is reviewed. At text level, the sequence is a qualitative guided case. It does not reproduce McKinsey’s proprietary model or supply an independently verified optimal strategy.

### Scene 6 — Same map, changed conditions

Replay with a clearly announced change: total rail demand does not increase. Also show whether your network advantage is strong or weak and whether your cost disadvantage is large.

Fox: “This time, there aren’t more passengers. Both companies are competing for the same pool.”

Explain that the article’s model can then produce very different results. With limited demand and high fixed costs, two operators may not both be viable on a route. A weak established operator can lose to the newcomer; a strong one may have incentives to cut fares before entry to discourage it. When circumstances are intermediate, coexistence can remain possible.

Do not infer a unique winner from demand alone. Hold visible the other relevant factors. The lesson is sensitivity to assumptions, not “always cut prices,” “always fight,” or “always share.”

### Scene 7 — Think beyond the first move

Show a short progression: proposed entry → your response → the rival reassesses → you review your plan. Players can adjust actions as conditions and other moves become clearer.

Fox: “Your first move changes what the other company may do next.”

Let the learner inspect two possible next responses, visibly labelled as possibilities rather than certain predictions. Explain that the article compares sequences and upside/downside risks across plausible situations, not just isolated snapshots.

### Scene 8 — What would make you change your plan?

Concept reveal: “Robust strategy — a plan that accounts for risks and opportunities across plausible conditions.”

Fox: “We’re looking for a plan that can handle more than one possible future.”

A simple comparison identifies the conditions under which a response looks attractive and the conditions under which it stops looking attractive. A break point is a change large enough to make a different plan preferable. Do not invent a precise passenger or price threshold: the pasted article does not supply usable numerical thresholds.

Reflection: “Which assumption would you want to check before choosing?” The learner may inspect demand, costs, connections or price sensitivity. Several choices are defensible; the fox explains why the selected information matters rather than awarding a single correct answer.

### Transfer from the article’s other examples

Machinery: the article describes established firms facing a potential new multipurpose factory. Different assumptions could support price cuts, waiting to react, or leaving the newcomer a limited niche. Use this as a short additional contrast showing that the rail lesson is not restricted to trains.

The authors also mention applying their approach to pharmaceuticals and chemicals, but provide too little detail here for us to claim a complete additional reviewed game. Record those examples as further applications, not invented simulations attributed to McKinsey.

### Learner takeaway

Fox: “Understanding the other player matters. Understanding what our prediction depends on matters too.”

The learner should recognise that entry may grow the overall market, network connections matter, a rival can adjust its move, and a recommended response can change when assumptions change.

Source link: [Making game theory work for managers](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/making-game-theory-work-for-managers). Supplied article includes textual descriptions and captions of Exhibits 1 and 2 but not their images; do not claim the plotted relationships or numerical values have been inspected. Its rail analysis describes model scenarios and strategic implications, not observed proof of their outcomes.

C. One party’s ability to selectively offer a side after seeing the outcome
D. The need to define any rules

Key: C. The procedure removes this particular information advantage, not risk itself.

### P23 — robust-decisions
A new train service attracts travellers who previously drove. Which conclusion is supported?

A. Every new rider must have left the existing railway
B. Total rail demand may grow, but profits still depend on other factors
C. Both companies are guaranteed to earn more
D. Costs no longer matter

Key: B. Market growth differs from redistribution; costs, networks and price responses still matter.

### P24 — robust-decisions
A plan works only if one exact demand forecast is correct. What additional analysis fits the McKinsey approach?

A. Treat that forecast as certain
B. Ignore the rival’s next move
C. Hide the assumptions from the decision-maker
D. Compare plausible demand and rival-response scenarios, including downside risks

Key: D. The authors seek decisions informed by a range of conditions, with assumptions and break points visible.

## Journey map, companion and saving

## Interest and progression

The map shows the whole chapter and makes the main path clear. The initial interest choice can start learners at an accessible mystery or sports activity; foundational teaching follows whichever opening they play. Completed activities remain completed if they return to the default sequence. Advanced stages require their prerequisites, rather than arbitrary engagement locks.

An unready destination can show its name, goal and the prerequisite: “Learn about players and choices first.” Do not imply that all remaining stages are playable already or all candidates have been published. Unreleased stages say Coming soon; a visual roadmap must not misrepresent a text draft as a shipped course.

Optional deeper examples appear as branches, not barriers across the main path. AI-recommended review is a separate relevant stop with an explanation of why it was suggested. It must not reset the main chapter progress or scatter new compulsory nodes after every mistake.

## Stage states

- Ready: available to start, with a concrete goal.
- In progress: unfinished; shows the saved activity and Continue.
- Completed: activity requirements finished; replay available. This does not automatically mean permanent mastery.
- Suggested review: relevant concept needs another look; keep completion evidence intact.
- Not ready: required prerequisite is stated.
- Coming soon: not released; do not promise an unlock through playing or installing.

Within a game, discovery, explanation and changed-situation practice are saved separately. Finishing the first round does not prematurely mark the whole stage complete. A guided correction and an independent answer are distinct evidence.

## Pause at any time

Provide a visible Return to map action throughout games, explanations and quizzes. Do not make a person finish a whole game before leaving, lose earned progress for closing the app, or display a punishment for taking a break.

Save after each meaningful action: stage and scene, player’s committed move, opponent’s committed move, authored rules/version, outcome already revealed, reflection/explanation already shown, quiz responses and their feedback exposure. Save temporary answer selection too, while distinguishing it from a submitted answer.

Returning should restore the same problem, not generate a different hidden number, deal, opponent move or question. A pause must not let the learner reroll an unfavourable result. An outcome committed before a pause stays committed. Restore animation at a sensible settled point with a short recap; do not needlessly replay a long animation or advance beyond an unseen explanation.

Examples:

- Hidden number, guess two: return to the same secret, remaining guesses and eliminated stones.
- Basketball after a pass: restore the resolved outcome and the next useful action without performing a duplicate pass.
- Concept explanation: return to the same explanation step, not the beginning of the whole chapter.
- MCQ check: preserve which answers and explanations were seen.
- Independent practice: restore the same question set, submitted and draft answers, and which help was requested. Preserve the original score separately from retries.

On return, show one short line: “You were comparing the new defence.” Continue places the learner back at that step. Replay and Start over are separate choices; replay does not erase completion or award duplicate XP.

## Save honesty and access

Convex remains the planned backend. This document defines expected behaviour; current prototypes are not evidence that chapter state sync works. Account-linked progress should restore on other supported devices when sync has completed. Without an account, any local progress must be described as tied to the current device until linked.

If a move cannot sync, preserve it locally where supported and show a brief pending-save state. Never claim progress is safely synced before confirmation. Reconcile saved moves and rewards without duplicate attempts when connection returns. Changing published content must not silently replace a paused problem; preserve its version or explain an explicit migration.

Respect reduced motion and larger text. Labels convey progress without relying on colour. Controls remain readable and usable without decorative motion. A long map may scroll, but Continue must remain easy to reach and the current stop easy to locate.

## Before implementation

Review the text-level stages and game rules, then prepare a static map design for founder review. After the story and map are agreed, build and test exact resume behaviour: leave before committing a move, after commitment but before its animation ends, during teaching, on an unanswered question, after quiz submission, and during a failed save. Verify no rerolls, skipped teaching or duplicate rewards. No design or runtime implementation is implied by this draft.

## Independent player review — remaining editorial decisions


Review date: 5 October 2026. Method: a critical walkthrough of the authored chapter, game anthology, roadmap and assessment design from a new player's perspective. This is not a playtest, retention measurement, or claim of real player enjoyment. No new game has been run. Proposed changes below preserve source coverage and require story review before implementation.

## Verdict

The draft is a good source map, but not yet a compelling game journey. Its strongest moments are visible changes that make a previously sensible move stop working: defenders moving, switching from leading to trailing, and a railway market changing. Its weakest moments are obvious choices, lengthy setup, and inspection screens presented as games. More scenarios will not solve those problems.

The desired hook is: “I understand what I can do. I want to see what happens. Now something changed, and I want another try.” Coins and an expressive fox support that loop; neither replaces it. A long chapter can contain short satisfying sessions, with voluntary continuation and reliable saving.

## Where a first-time player may leave

1. Before the first move: choosing an interest, reading map labels, learning rules and meeting characters could delay play. Offer one clear default opening; let interests change later destinations without a required intake questionnaire.
2. During hidden numbers: guessing numbers can feel like schoolwork before its strategic twist arrives. The opening also risks looking like a luck test. Keep the role reversal, but move this behind a more visual first encounter.
3. During the tournament: three people, a challenge, finalist selection, a promise and audience preferences demand too much early memory. Move this later; show one future branch at a time and pin the final goal visibly.
4. During obvious choices: “pass to the open teammate,” an explicitly losing token offer, and “protect work time” tell players the answer. State rules clearly, but do not have the fox solve every independent decision first.
5. Between games: a new unrelated cast and setting every stop makes the chapter feel like a collection. Carry forward the fox and recognisable role-labelled supporting characters; bridge with a specific question from the previous experience. Do not force every source into an artificial festival plot.
6. At the ending: twelve MCQs followed by twelve more questions may feel like a second exam. Keep both requested assessments, but make their different purposes clear, save after every response, and offer a natural break between them. Do not silently shorten coverage or turn practice into an endless adaptive gate.

## Proposed opening: One more basket

This is a concrete candidate for text review, not a newly approved chapter order. Target first action within about twenty seconds, then validate that target with players. No live timer or speed pressure.

### Beat 1 — An immediately readable goal

A small court shows your player holding the ball, one teammate, the basket and two defenders. Labels identify your team and the other team. Fox stays at the bottom, looks towards the ball, and says: “Let's score a basket. Tap our player with the ball.”

Only that player's touch target is highlighted. This first tap teaches the control, not the concept. A short rule explains the authored model: a guarded shot is blocked; a clear shot scores. No real basketball skill is assumed.

### Beat 2 — A move with a visible result

Both defenders guard the ball carrier. Selecting the ball carrier exposes Shoot; selecting the teammate exposes Pass. Fox: “You can shoot or pass. Your move.”

If the player shoots, a defender visibly blocks it. Fox follows the ball: “That defender blocked us. Try another move.” Reset the same arrangement without taking away progress or reward. If the player passes, the teammate receives the ball; a clear Shoot action completes the basket. Fox reacts to the actual sequence. There is no hidden penalty for exploring.

### Beat 3 — The reason to play again

The opponents visibly change their defence. One guards the former receiver while the ball carrier now has a clear shot. Fox notices the movement: “They saw our pass. Look where they moved.”

The same controls remain. The learner chooses; the fox does not name the open player. No forced wrong answer: a successful first choice still produces the next scene. This is a simplified response demonstration, not a claim that these scripted defenders independently optimise.

### Beat 4 — Name what happened

After both arrangements have been encountered, show a compact comparison of defender positions and shot outcomes.

“Game theory: thinking about choices when other people's choices affect what happens.”

Fox: “Your move mattered. So did theirs.”

Then identify the two teams as players, scoring and stopping a score as their goals, and shoot/pass as available choices. Introduce rationality as choosing towards a stated goal in a separate short beat; save the full values and limits discussion for its later application. Keep the required concept teaching, but do not attach a glossary to every successful shot.

### Beat 5 — A small independent transfer

Change positions again under the same visible rules. Ask the learner to make a move without a directional hint. After the outcome, explain the specific connection between defender position and available shot. Record prompted practice separately from independent application; do not call one basket mastery.

### Beat 6 — A satisfying stop and an invitation

Show one completed encounter, a brief fox celebration with both hands, and one short takeaway: “Look again when the other player changes their move.” If using coins, award a clearly labelled learning reward once; keep it separate from the basketball score. No arbitrary coin amount is fixed here.

Offer “Back to map” and “Try the boat race.” Preview the next question: “Would you make the same move if you were already winning?” Preserve a proper stopping point. No guilt for leaving.

## Pacing and companion rules

- Each meaningful move changes the visible scene, available options, information or outcome. A decorative button that reveals another paragraph does not count as a strategic choice.
- The fox gives a goal, reacts to what actually happened, or explains a concrete consequence. Avoid generic praise after every tap and idle movement that competes with the decision.
- Reveal the concept after a meaningful experience; keep its name visible in the recap and optional notebook. Review notes remain accessible without forced rereading.
- Show consequences at their source: ball at basket, boats changing relative position, tickets moving between trays. Keep learning coins separate from scenario quantities.
- Keep one clear current goal and stable controls. During the first independent decision, hints remain available on request rather than revealing the answer in advance.
- Optional deeper readings retain citations. The main path still teaches the source's relevant idea; putting a link in a drawer is not content coverage.
- No shame, artificial loss of saved progress, deceptive urgency or compulsive reward schedule. Curiosity and competence should motivate return.

## McKinsey pacing revision

Keep McKinsey last before assessment. First encounter: a small rival morning service appears on one route. Inspect the rival, then compare the cost of responding with leaving the current service unchanged under explicitly stated growing-demand and network assumptions. Reveal a qualitative comparison, not fabricated profits.

Second encounter: the market no longer grows. Make that change visible and keep cost/network assumptions on screen. Ask what further information matters; do not imply demand alone determines the winning move.

Third encounter: compare an aggressive entrant and subsequent responses. Expand to all four entry approaches, response options and drivers through the existing scenes. Finish by identifying what would make the learner reconsider a plan. Follow with the brief machinery comparison. This preserves the article while reducing the number of new ideas introduced at once.

## What must be tested before calling this engaging

Run a first-play prototype with people unfamiliar with the draft. Observe without explaining controls. Ask afterwards where they thought the next action was and what changed their decision. Record behaviour and comments without converting a small sample into a retention claim.

Check: can they make the first move without assistance; do they notice the changed defence; can they explain why their move changed; do they voluntarily try another scenario; can they pause and return to the exact scene? Also record boredom, misread labels and skipped explanations. Voluntary continuation is a useful signal, not proof that the full chapter retains players.

No engagement guarantee is justified yet. The next concrete step is reviewing this short opening storyboard, then building and testing only that encounter before producing all fourteen stages.
