# Chapter 1: You’re not choosing alone

**Course rewrite · Script draft 1 · 5 October 2026**

**Status:** for story review, not approved for implementation. Prototype development is paused. This document is the proposed replacement for Chapter 1’s earlier learner-facing scripts. Earlier documents remain references; their dialogue and screen order are not instructions to build. No app code changes accompany this rewrite.

## The promise to the learner

“Play through situations where the obvious move leaves something out. Discover what other people’s choices change—and use that insight outside the game.”

The alternative is another few minutes of scrolling. We earn those minutes with an unresolved question, a meaningful move and a satisfying explanation. Animation makes a consequence understandable; it cannot supply the missing idea. A long chapter is acceptable. A long setup before anything interesting happens is not.

## How to read this script

Each numbered beat is a separate screen. **See** describes the scene. **Fox** is exact proposed dialogue. **Do** names the player’s action. **Then** gives the consequence and branch. Author notes and answer keys never appear as learner dialogue.

After a committed action: replace the action screen with its consequence, let the fox respond, then let the learner continue. No growing chat log. No sentence-by-sentence slideshow where nothing changes. Keep the relevant objects visible across transitions so the learner follows the cause.

The fox is an honest companion, never a trickster secretly changing the rules. Its name remains undecided. Other characters have their own goals. “You chose differently” is not automatically “you were wrong.” A prediction can be mistaken; a personal preference is not graded.

Every encounter ends with an explicit concept, a new application and a short takeaway. Learners can stop at any beat. Returning restores that beat and the choices already made. No lost lives, forced timers or repeated rewards.

## 1. The ads go dark

**Seven-beat rewrite · awaiting story review, not implementation.** This version addresses [Opening review 2](chapter-1-opening-review-2.md). The model tests a mechanism; it does not prove an initial historical prediction wrong. Prototype remains unchanged.

**Opening recommendation, revised after founder feedback:** start with a real-world puzzle, not the box or number game. This opening teaches strategic interdependence. Chapter 2 later revisits it through the formal prisoner’s-dilemma model and a fuller historical discussion.

**First line on screen:** “Cigarette ads are about to disappear from TV and radio. What happens to the companies?”

**Hook structure:** prediction → experiment → contrasting result → explanation → fresh application. Save the initial prediction without grading it. Never assume the learner got it wrong. The historical result is not a universal rule about bans.

**Learner role:** investigate the puzzle by operating a small comparison model. You are not asked to promote cigarettes, approve a health policy, or guess an obscure historical fact. You control one company’s advertising decision, then compare a rule applying to both companies.

**The interesting misconception:** if losing an advertising channel hurts one company, the same restriction must hurt the whole industry in the same way.

### Author rules — never confuse this with historical data

Two fictional companies, A and B. In this model only, the total money available before advertising costs stays at 200 coins. Advertising changes how it is divided, not its total. The model ignores production costs to isolate the advertising mechanism. “Money left” means the amount after the displayed advertising cost, not a fully estimated real-world profit.

- If both advertise, each gets 100 and spends 40: each keeps 60.
- If neither advertises, each gets 100 and spends 0: each keeps 100.
- If only one advertises, it gets 160 and spends 40: it keeps 120. The other gets 40 and spends 0: it keeps 40.

These are authored illustration numbers. They are not tobacco-company accounts, measured effects of the ban, or assumptions established by the cited study. The rival’s choice is visibly fixed during each controlled comparison. No hidden reaction changes after a learner taps.

### Exact opening script — seven beats

A beat contains a decision and its consequence, not necessarily one screen. A response always replaces the decision screen. No appended transcript. The first numerical contrast arrives in beat 3; no extra rival-inspection screen or forced replay stands in its way. “Continue” advances a scene; it is not presented as a strategic decision.

#### 1.1 — Predict

**See:** a television goes dark beside a news card: “United States · 2 January 1971 · Cigarette ads banned on TV and radio.” No profit chart, celebration or title suggesting the answer.

**Fox:** “Would you expect this to hurt the established cigarette companies financially?”

**Choose:** “Yes, hurt them” / “No, it could help” / “I’m not sure.”

**Response:** save the exact prediction without grading it. Fox says, “Hold that thought. Let’s look at what changes for competitors.”

**Transition:** the news card gives way to “Before the restriction · a made-up example.” No extra “Try it” confirmation. Do not treat a plausible expectation about history as a misconception established by this toy model.

#### 1.2 — Make your move

**See:** two companies and two advertising switches. Label A “Your company”; label B “Competitor.” Both switches start on. Each company shows one main figure: “Money left after ads: 60.”

**Fox:** “Ads cost 40. In this example, customers spend the same total amount; ads change who gets their business.”

**On-board prompt:** “Your competitor keeps advertising. Keep your ads, or stop?”

**Choose:** “Keep my ads” / “Stop my ads.” Commit by choosing; no separate confirmation tap. An optional receipt explains 100 received − 40 advertising = 60 left. Rules remain available throughout.

**If keep:** both televisions stay on; both retain 60. Fox: “You both keep advertising—and paying for it.”

**If stop:** A’s television goes dark; customer-spending tokens shift toward B. A’s main figure changes from 60 to 40; B’s from 60 to 120. Fox: “You saved the ad cost, but your competitor gained business. You kept less.”

**Both branches:** a small comparison strip shows A’s result with B still advertising: “A keeps ads: 60 · A stops ads: 40.” It is labelled “Compare both choices.” This makes the unchosen result explicit without forcing a replay or pretending the learner selected it.

**Next:** “What if neither could advertise?”

#### 1.3 — Watch the condition change

**See:** “New condition: neither company can advertise.” Both switches become unavailable and both televisions go dark. The learner is observing an external restriction, not being asked to choose a law or break one.

Both advertising bills disappear. Reveal A’s and B’s main figures: 100 each. Retain only the comparison needed: “Both advertised: 60 each → neither advertises: 100 each.”

**Fox:** “Here, both keep more. Stopping alone and stopping together are different situations.”

**Prediction-aware second line:**
- Predicted harm: “This shows a possibility your first prediction may have left out.”
- Predicted benefit: “This gives one possible reason for your prediction.”
- Unsure: “Now we have one possible explanation to investigate.”

Do not say “You were wrong,” “You were right about history,” or “The ban caused profits to rise.” Those do not follow from the invented receipts.

**Next:** “What made the difference?”

#### 1.4 — Explain what changed

**See:** two miniature comparisons, with A’s ads off in both. Left: B on, A keeps 40. Right: B off, A keeps 100.

**Fox:** “A stopped advertising in both pictures. Why did A’s result change?”

**Choose:** “The competitor stopped too” / “A saved its ad bill only in the second picture.” Optional spoken or typed explanation follows the same rubric.

**First response:** “Yes. A saved its own ad bill in both. The difference was whether its competitor could keep advertising.”

**Second response:** highlight the zero ad bill in both pictures. “A paid no ad bill in either picture. Look at B: it could advertise in one, but not the other.” Do not withhold the concept until another attempt.

**Concept reveal on the response screen:** **Strategic interdependence.** “Your result depends on your choices and other people’s choices.”

**Fox:** “Game theory starts by looking at both sides of that decision.”

**Next:** “Would this always work?”

#### 1.5 — Challenge the explanation

**See:** the two dark televisions remain, but the total customer-spending pool is now labelled “Could shrink.” Do not invent a new exact outcome.

**Fox:** “We held customer spending steady. What if people bought much less when the ads disappeared?”

**Choose:** “Saving the ad bill still guarantees a gain” / “We must compare lost income with the saved cost.”

**Response to guarantee:** “A smaller bill isn’t enough if income falls by more. The assumption about customer spending mattered.”

**Response to compare:** “Exactly. The saved bill and the lost income both matter. We cannot predict the gain from the bill alone.”

**Fox:** “We found a possible mechanism, not a rule that every ban helps companies.”

**Next:** “What did the researchers find?”

#### 1.6 — Return to the real case

**See:** leave the model entirely. A source card names Mark L. Mitchell and J. Harold Mulherin, *Finessing the Political System: The Cigarette Advertising Ban*.

**Fox:** “Their study found stock-market evidence consistent with benefits to established tobacco companies while the policy developed.”

**Supporting line:** “That concerns investors’ expectations. It is not our fictional receipt or a measured profit increase of 40 coins.”

**Fox:** “The authors also pointed to barriers facing new brands. The real case had more going on than our two-company example.”

**Optional “Read the evidence”:** describe the reported abnormal returns, the authors’ interpretation, entry barriers and the anti-smoking broadcasting context from the supplied paper. Do not fabricate stock movements on the day the ban took effect, claim all companies benefited equally, or present this as proof of secret industry coordination. Company benefits and public-health effects are different questions; this lesson does not resolve the latter.

**Close the original question:** “A restriction can hurt one option while changing competition in ways that favour established firms. To understand the result, investigate both.”

**Next:** “Try the idea somewhere else.”

#### 1.7 — Use the idea

**See:** your café and a nearby rival both offer delivery. Your delivery costs have risen. You are considering cancelling it. No advertising, bans or historical facts in this setting.

**Fox:** “Before deciding whether cancelling delivery saves you money, what would you investigate?”

**Do:** open two clearly labelled evidence cards, in either order. Each opens a replacement screen with one fact:
- **Delivery cost:** “Cancelling removes this cost.”
- **Customer choices:** “Some customers may switch to the rival if it still delivers.”

**After both:** “Is the delivery bill enough to decide?”

**Choose:** “Yes, cancelling removes the bill” / “No, compare the saving with business we could lose.”

**Response to yes:** “Removing the bill helps. Losing customers could offset it. The rival’s service belongs in your decision too.”

**Response to no:** “Yes. Consider your saving and what customers can get from the other café. We need both before recommending a choice.”

**Takeaway:** “Before judging your move, ask what everyone else can do.”

**Fox:** “Next, we’ll look at what those other people want.”

**Finish action:** “Save this idea.” The full opening is complete here. Offer a genuine stopping point and preserve progress.

## 2. Why refuse the money?

**Source:** professor on rationality, book’s alternative taxi perspective, and the supplied taxi-payment thought experiment. **Question:** are we predicting their priorities, or projecting our own?

**Author rules:** an original fictional performer, Mira, promised to play at a friend’s event for ₹500. A second event offers ₹800 at the same time. She cannot do both. No cancellation fee. Her stated preference is to honour this promise rather than earn the additional ₹300. This is a preference in the story, not a moral answer for the learner.

**2.1 — Make an offer.** See the two event bookings, amount and same time clearly marked. Fox: “You book performers. Mira already promised to play at her friend’s event. You can offer ₹800. Will money alone tell us what she chooses?” Do: send the offer. Mira declines; the ₹800 card returns unclaimed.

**2.2 — Hear her reason.** Mira: “I gave my word. Keeping it matters more to me than the extra ₹300.” Fox: “She understood the money. She valued something else more.” Do: inspect “What she gains” and “What she gives up” as two separate replacement screens; neither is a judgment banner.

**2.3 — Change the constraint.** The second event moves to the following day; she is free. Fox: “Now she can do both. Her promise is safe.” Do: send the revised offer. Mira accepts. Response: “Same person. Same values. A different choice because the conflict disappeared.”

**2.4 — Reflect and teach.** Ask: “Was declining proof she made no sense?” Choices: “Yes, because ₹800 is more” / “No, she was pursuing a different priority.” Explain after both. **Rationality:** choosing consistently with goals and beliefs. Fox: “Rational does not mean emotionless. It does not always mean earning the most money.” Follow with: “It is a useful starting model. Real people can misjudge, change their minds, or act inconsistently. We check the model against behaviour.”

**2.5 — The taxi connection.** An agreed fare remains visible after a fictional ride. Fox: “A money-only model leaves out why people honour agreements even when an immediate advantage tempts them.” Show “Possible motives: keeping an agreement; fairness; consequences; reputation.” Do: select which assumption a money-only model omitted—not whether they personally would pay. Response: “More than one motive can matter. This thought experiment does not prove one explanation for every passenger.”

**2.6 — Fresh application.** A buyer knowingly picks a ₹700 repair with a reliable warranty over a ₹600 repair without one. Ask: “Does spending more prove irrationality?” Explain: “No. Reliability may be worth the extra cost to that buyer. We would need their priorities and beliefs.” Takeaway: “Before predicting a choice, check what counts as a good outcome for that person.”

**Source card:** the book’s driver reply is imagined by its authors, not testimony from a real driver. The taxi-payment blog is a secondary discussion, not proof about actual passengers. Neither should be presented as observed player data.

## 3. Everyone knew. Why did nobody move?

**Source:** professor’s common-knowledge discussion. The assigned prisoner video itself was not supplied. This is our small, non-threatening reconstruction, not a transcript of that video.

**Author rules:** two players, Mira and Dev, wear green badges on their backs. Each sees the other’s badge but not their own. No mirrors, messages or other clues. They know these rules, each other’s ability to reason, and that this is shared knowledge. At each bell, they may step forward only if certain their own badge is green. Choices are simultaneous. The fox can truthfully announce publicly that at least one badge is green. No punishment, death or escape narrative.

**3.1 — Establish one viewpoint.** See Mira’s view: Dev’s green badge; Mira’s badge hidden. Fox: “Mira sees Dev’s badge. She cannot see her own. Could hers be a different colour?” Do: turn over the two possible-world cards: “Mira green” and “Mira not green.” Both fit what she sees. Response: “Seeing Dev’s green badge doesn’t settle Mira’s badge.”

**3.2 — The announcement.** Fox, facing both: “At least one of you has a green badge.” Both visibly hear it and see the other hearing it. Fox to learner: “You could already see green. Did that announcement do anything?” Do: predict “No new information” / “It might change what they can infer.” Do not mark correct yet.

**3.3 — Try one green badge first.** A clearly labelled imagined version: Mira green, Dev not green. Fox: “If Mira saw no green badge, this announcement would tell her the green one must be hers.” Do: ring the first bell. Mira steps forward. Response: “One green badge would be enough for its wearer to know at the first bell.”

**3.4 — Return to two green badges.** Restore actual Mira and Dev, both green. Do: ring the first bell. Neither steps. Fox: “Mira sees Dev stay. If Mira’s badge were not green, Dev would have seen no green badge—and stepped forward.” Animate the “Mira not green” possibility being crossed out. Do not ask the learner to invent this missing inference alone.

**3.5 — The next bell.** Fox: “Dev can make the same argument about Mira.” Do: ring bell two. Both step forward. Response: “Each learned from the other not moving.”

**3.6 — Name the idea.** Ask: “What did the announcement change?” Choices: badge colours / shared starting point for their reasoning. **Common knowledge:** everyone knows, everyone knows that everyone knows, and so on. Fox: “The announcement did not change the badges. It gave their reasoning a public starting point.” Emphasise that common knowledge alone does not force obedience; this outcome also used the stated reasoning and action rules.

**3.7 — Everyday boundary.** Show two colleagues each privately told a meeting moved to 4. Neither knows what the other was told. Then show a joint conversation where both openly acknowledge 4. Ask: “What uncertainty did the joint conversation reduce?” Explain: “Uncertainty about whether the other person knows. In real communication, noise and attention can still matter. A read receipt is not automatically the ideal common knowledge in our puzzle.”

**Takeaway:** “What people know about each other’s knowledge can change what they do.” A three-person extension stays optional and is not needed to finish this lesson.

## 4. The missing points

**Source:** the book’s defensive-response argument in “The Hot Hand.” Do not repeat its dated empirical claim about shooting streaks as settled science.

**4.1 — A decision worth investigating.** See Asha’s points: previous match 12, latest 6. Crowd bubble: “Bench her?” Fox: “You’re the coach. Her points halved. Find out what happened before replacing her.” Do: open the match replay. These are labelled fictional figures.

**4.2 — Run the possession.** See Asha holding the ball, a defender committed to guarding her, Dev elsewhere. Rules: guarded shot blocked, clear shot scores two; this is an illustration, not basketball physics. Do: shoot with Asha or pass to Dev, then shoot. Blocked branch: ball returns for another try, no lost life. Scoring branch: Dev +2, Asha +0, team +2. Fox: “The scorer got two. Who helped create the opening?”

**4.3 — Remove the pass as an explanation.** Dev starts with the ball. Asha begins near him; the defender obstructs both. Do: move Asha wide or keep her near, then let Dev shoot. Wide: defender follows Asha; Dev scores. Near: shot blocked under these rules. Fox: “This time Asha never touched the ball. Her threat still moved the defender.”

**4.4 — Reflect.** “What would we miss if we counted only Asha’s baskets?” Answer choices: space created / nothing else matters. **Strategic response:** your strength changes another player’s behaviour, which changes opportunities elsewhere.

**4.5 — Reopen the report.** Reveal teammates’ points 8→18 and team points 20→24. Fox: “Her score fell while the team’s rose. The replay shows one possible mechanism. These totals alone don’t prove that mechanism caused the whole change.”

**4.6 — Transfer.** Your café improves breakfast; its rival responds with a price cut; your sales remain flat. Ask what to inspect before calling the improvement useless. Correct reasoning: consider the rival’s response and other conditions, not only before/after sales. Takeaway: “A result includes what others changed because of your move.”

## 5. The final before the final

**Source:** the book’s “Winning by Losing.” Original contestants; not a historical reenactment.

**Author rules:** you, Mira and Dev remain. Challenge winner chooses one finalist; a later audience vote decides the prize. The fictional vote ranking is Dev above You above Mira. You publicly promised to take Dev if you win. In this story you honour that promise. Do not introduce a hidden penalty for breaking it. The first comparison assumes stepping down lets Mira win; a second case explicitly changes who wins. Uncertainty is discussed afterward, not faked through arbitrary surprise results.

**5.1:** Reveal the vote as two comparisons: you lose against Dev; you win against Mira. Fox: “The challenge decides who chooses. The vote decides who wins.” Do: inspect each final separately.

**5.2:** Play from Mira’s chair. Fox: “Mira wants the prize too. Which finalist gives her the better chance?” Do: select you or Dev. Response: “Under this ranking she loses to either, so ranking alone does not determine her choice.” **Additional authored preference:** Mira prefers a close loss to a larger loss; state the voting margins as you beating Mira 6–4 and Dev beating Mira 9–1. With that preference, she chooses you. This preference must be disclosed before asking a scored prediction. This detour deliberately exposes the danger of assuming a losing player’s choice without a reason.

**5.3:** Return to your chair. Reveal your promise to Dev. Fox: “If you win, you take Dev. If Mira wins, she takes you. Which final would you rather reach?” Do: stay and win / step down so Mira wins. Resolve the two-stage chain, one scene at a time. Staying: challenge trophy, then vote loss. Stepping down: challenge loss, then vote win.

**5.4:** Change one condition. “Now stepping down lets Dev win. He chooses you, and beats you in the vote.” Do: inspect this branch before choosing again. Fox: “Giving up a round is not the trick. The later choices were the reason it helped.”

**5.5:** **Looking ahead:** reason through later choices using each player’s goals. Ask what changes if challenge winner is uncertain. Answer: compare possible branches; do not guarantee the favourable one. Mention waiting for more information as a possible option where the challenge rules allow it.

**5.6:** Transfer: winning a work assignment consumes the only evening needed to prepare for a qualification you value more. Ask which final objective the local win serves. Takeaway: “Trace what winning commits you to next.”

**Editorial caution:** this is the most complex early book story. Keep it after the foundations. If its losing-player preference distracts from looking ahead in review, rewrite the tournament model before building; never conceal that missing incentive again.

## 6. Same move, different race

**Source:** “To Lead or Not to Lead.” **Rules:** equal boats; same route preserves their order. Two routes have unknown wind. At the fork the other boat commits first, visibly. Each wind arrangement is equally likely and fixed before choices; taking different routes may reverse or widen the gap. No extra skill roll.

**6.1:** You lead. Rival chooses left. Fox: “Your goal is to finish ahead of this boat. Follow it, or take the other route?” Do: choose route. Follow preserves lead in both wind arrangements. Split wins in the favourable arrangement, loses in the other. Show the actual branch, then allow replay with the same wind.

**6.2:** Swap starting positions only. You trail; leader chooses left. Same choices. Following preserves loss. Splitting gives a chance to overtake. Show both wind possibilities before treating one outcome as evidence of a sensible plan.

**6.3:** Reflect: “Why did the same uncertainty look different from behind?” Explain **goal-dependent risk**: protecting a lead and needing to overtake can justify different choices.

**6.4:** Change the objective: arrive as early as possible, regardless of the other boat. Fox: “Protecting position is no longer the target. If both winds are equally likely and routes otherwise equal, this model gives no uniquely fastest choice.”

**6.5:** Transfer: two projects offer certain second place versus a chance at first or last. Ask whether a team rewarded for top-three finishes necessarily chooses like one rewarded only for first. No universal answer; specify the reward before judging. Takeaway: “A risk makes sense relative to a goal.”

## 8. Tomorrow gets a vote

**Source:** “Thinning Strategically,” adapted without body shame or humiliation.

**Rules:** two fictional versions of you, Now and Later, prefer different options at different times. Tonight-You wants a finished prop for tomorrow’s show. Later-You, when the game is available, chooses playing over prop work. Both preferences are explicit model assumptions, not diagnoses of the real learner.

**8.1:** See the unfinished prop and two time cards. Fox: “Tonight you want the prop ready. Later, playing will feel more tempting. Leave a reminder, or arrange the choices now?” Do: reminder / put the game away until the work period ends.

**8.2:** Advance to Later. Reminder leaves both choices; the fictional Later-You plays. Restriction removes the game; Later-You works. Fox: “The reminder repeated your goal. The restriction changed the choice available when temptation arrived.”

**8.3:** New round with an expected important call. Offer block all phone use / block only the game. Both prevent gaming; only the narrower rule permits the call. Learner chooses; show each cost explicitly.

**8.4:** **Self-commitment:** arranging future options to support a present goal. Ask: “Which part did the work: wanting harder, or changing later choices?” Explain limits: restrictions can impose costs; this model does not prove everyone needs one.

**8.5:** Transfer: choosing a non-refundable booking can motivate attendance but also makes cancellation costly. Ask for both effects. Takeaway: “Make the useful restriction no wider than it needs to be.”

## 9. Someone else will do it

**Source:** collective-action theme and “Buffett’s Dilemma”; this camp model is distinct from his political proposal and from Chapter 2’s prisoner’s dilemma.

**Rules:** two teams share a camp. Cleaning gives each team four satisfaction points. Each team that cleans pays an effort cost of two points. Teams maximise their own net points; these are fictional preference units. If both clean, each still pays two. If nobody cleans, everyone gets zero. Moves are simultaneous and locked before reveal.

**9.1:** Do: clean / leave it to the other team. The partner’s committed teaching move is wait. Resolve clean: you 2, them 4; wait: 0/0. Fox: “When nobody else cleans, helping beats a dirty camp under these values.”

**9.2:** Replay with the partner committed to clean, announced before your action. Clean yields 2/2; wait yields 4/2. Fox: “When someone else does it, your team benefits without the effort.”

**9.3:** Reflect: “Is waiting always best?” No. Contrast the two boards. **Collective action:** shared benefits and individual costs can pull choices apart. This is not a game where waiting wins against every partner action.

**9.4:** Change one rule: each helper also receives a reward worth three points to its team. Same decisions. Against wait: cleaning 5 versus waiting 0. Against clean: cleaning 5 versus waiting 4. Fox: “Now helping pays more in either case. The reward changed an incentive; it didn’t change anyone’s personality.”

**9.5:** Source connection: describe Buffett’s hypothetical political donation as changing the consequences of reform failing, including relative advantage between opposing parties. Label hypothetical, not an actual payment. Do not equate it mathematically with camp points.

**9.6:** Transfer: a group-project contribution benefits everyone but costs the contributor time. Ask what must be compared before predicting effort: individual cost, shared benefit, others’ effort and any incentive. Takeaway: “Agreement on a goal does not guarantee agreement on who bears its cost.”

## 10. They’re reading your moves

**Source:** “Mix Your Plays.” Ordinary rock–paper–scissors rules. Opponents never see a current hidden choice.

**10.1:** Choose a move against an opponent openly committed to rock. Resolve. Fox: “Paper works against this rule. Keep the word ‘this’ in mind.” One round is enough.

**10.2:** Choose a three-move plan. See it laid out face up. Opponent can read the whole plan before committing its responses. Do: run all three rounds. It counters each planned move. Fox: “You used different moves. They were still predictable.”

**10.3:** New round: the plan stays private. The opponent must commit first; neither side sees the other’s current choice. Offer pick manually / shuffle equally. Resolve without inventing a win for shuffle. Loss response: “Random choices can lose. Their purpose is to hide a predictable pattern, not guarantee this round.” Win response: “You won this round. One win doesn’t establish a better strategy.”

**10.4:** **Mixed strategy:** choosing among moves using probabilities. Explain that a fixed rock-paper-scissors cycle is variation, not randomness. In this symmetric game equal random choice prevents an opponent from exploiting a known bias in the long run; three rounds need not contain one of each move.

**10.5:** Transfer: a goalkeeper always dives left, right, left, right. Ask whether varying sides alone makes the sequence hard to anticipate. Takeaway: “Different is not necessarily unpredictable.”

## 11. You won. Now open it.

**Source:** “Never Give a Sucker an Even Bet,” including its auction/private-information connections. **Rules:** one common resale value; estimates can be wrong. All bids locked before reveal. Highest bid pays its own bid. Other bids 40 and 60; tie at 60 goes to the earlier registered other buyer. Resale value 60. Your estimate 80. Start with 100 practice coins. These illustrative values are fixed before your bid.

**11.1:** See sealed spare-parts box and wallet 100. Fox: “Buy the parts, sell them, and finish with more than you started. Your estimate is 80. An estimate can be wrong.” Do: bid 1–100 or pass. Explain highest-bid rule beside the bid control, not after committing.

**11.2:** Reveal bids. Above 60: learner wins and pays bid. Otherwise another buyer pays 60. Do: open box. Resale brings 60. Show actual wallet equation: 100 − bid + 60 for winner; 100 for non-buyer.

**11.3:** Branch dialogue. Winner: “You won because you offered more. The parts were worth less than your estimate.” Non-buyer: “You kept your coins. Now inspect why a buyer offering 80 would lose 20.” Use a labelled hypothetical comparison; never replace the learner’s actual bid with 80.

**11.4:** Reflection: “What could winning tell you about your estimate?” Explain **winner’s curse**: when bidders estimate a shared uncertain value, winning can select the most optimistic error. Winning is a reason to examine the estimate, not proof it was wrong in every auction. One example establishes no optimal bidding formula.

**11.5:** New use, different value. A wheel resells for 60 but repairs your broken cart instead of a 90-coin repair. Price 70; fitting is free. Do: buy or use repair shop. Both restore the cart; buying costs 20 less. Fox: “You had a different use for it. Paying more than resale value need not be a mistake.”

**11.6:** Private-information connection. Seller inspected the item; you have not. Ask what to investigate before treating their offer as a random opportunity. Explain selection: their decision to sell may depend on information you lack. No claim every seller is dishonest.

**11.7:** Transfer: your one-hour job estimate beats bids of two and three hours. Show a checklist: you omitted labelling. Do: revise estimate. Takeaway: “Being selected can be a signal to recheck what you missed.”

## 12. A rival arrives

**Source:** Hagen Lindstädt and Jürgen Müller, *Making game theory work for managers*, McKinsey, 1 December 2009. This is a qualitative case investigation, not an invented numerical railway simulator.

**12.1 — Own a decision.** A rail map shows your connected network and a new company’s two morning trains. Fox: “A rival has arrived. Do you fight for every passenger, or leave room for it?” Do: place a provisional response card: keep service / compete / coexist / leave route. No correct banner; no profit numbers.

**12.2 — Ask where the riders came from.** Do: inspect passengers. Reveal: some are switching from cars, rather than from your trains. Fox: “A rival gaining passengers does not mean you lost all of them.” Then inspect connections: your network serves journeys beyond the rival’s route.

**12.3 — Inspect the other two drivers.** Cost card: rival has lower operating costs; your network also has obligations. Price card: some passengers switch for lower fares. Fox: “Cheaper fares could attract riders—and cut what both companies earn.” Each inspection replaces the screen. Do not display four unexplained metrics at once.

**12.4 — Revisit the first decision.** Known bundle: growing total rail demand, valuable network, small niche entrant. Do: keep or revise the provisional response. Feedback for coexistence: “This resembles a scenario in the article where tolerating a small entrant can preserve more value than a costly battle.” Feedback for fight: “You may defend share, but could spend more fighting than the small threatened share is worth.” Keep-service: “This preserves your current plan; it needs monitoring rather than assuming nothing will change.” Exit: “You would surrender a route despite a valuable network and limited entry; examine what justifies that cost.” These are trade-offs, not a computed ranking.

**12.5 — Change one assumption first.** Remove growth in total demand; keep the other facts visible and unchanged. Fox: “Now the same trains compete for the same pool of passengers. Your previous reason for tolerance is weaker. This fact alone still does not decide the answer.” Do: flag the assumption requiring review: new passengers / fox colour. Only this identification is scored.

**12.6 — Change the competitive position.** Explicit second scenario: demand stays flat, your cost disadvantage is large, your network advantage small. Fox: “This is a tougher bundle of conditions. The article describes cases where a weak incumbent can be squeezed out.” Do: compare protecting this route with withdrawing; show relevant costs and network losses qualitatively. No arbitrary winner.

**12.7 — Give the rival a next move.** Learner selects a tentative response. Show two possible rival branches: stays small / expands aggressively. Ask: “What would make you reconsider?” Accept a reason tied to demand, cost, connections, prices or rival scale. Feedback explains the named link, not “great strategy!”

**12.8 — Name the approach.** **Robust strategy:** compare a plan across plausible conditions, including what could go wrong and what would make you change course. Fox: “Game theory helps you examine possible futures. It does not remove uncertainty or predict one guaranteed ending.”

**12.9 — Leave with a decision rule.** Build a three-part sentence from reviewed choices: “For now I would ___, because ___; I would reconsider if ___.” A valid explanation must connect its reason and trigger; a particular action alone does not prove understanding. Use a reviewed fallback explanation if AI cannot assess it confidently.

**Source card:** the machinery example also compares fighting, waiting and tolerating a niche entrant. Health-care and chemical cases are mentioned in the article but lack enough detail here for separate games. Exhibits were absent from supplied text. We cannot invent break-even thresholds or claim to reproduce the authors’ model.

**Closing line:** “You now have better questions to bring to a decision—not a trick that makes every decision easy.”

## 14. Try it somewhere new — separate practice script

Fox: “New situations now. I’ll save your answers and explain them after the set. You can pause anytime.” This first script has twelve fresh items, not a claim of a large adaptive bank. A learner may request help; mark the attempt as helped and use a different future item for independent evidence.

| ID | Situation and choices | Author key / explanation |
|---|---|---|
| P1 | You choose a lunch from a fixed menu with plenty of stock. Your friend chooses separately and neither choice affects the other. Must you predict their choice to pick your favourite? A Yes; B No. | B. Under these stated conditions this is an individual choice, not strategic dependence. |
| P2 | Two shops both cut prices to compete. Do their higher sales alone prove both earned more? A Yes; B No. | B. Examine money earned after costs and the rival’s response. More sales are not the same as more money kept. |
| P3 | A cyclist selects a slower route because avoiding traffic matters more than speed. Is slowness alone evidence of irrationality? A Yes; B No. | B. Evaluate the choice relative to goals, not your preferred metric. |
| P4 | Each teammate privately receives a plan but cannot tell whether the others received it. What remains uncertain? A Whether others know the plan; B Whether a message contains words. | A. Everyone receiving information is distinct from knowing that everyone received it. |
| P5 | A shop improves delivery; a rival immediately matches it; sales remain unchanged. What can you conclude? A Improvement definitely had no effect; B The rival’s response must be considered. | B. The observed result includes a possible reaction, not only the first change. |
| P6 | Winning a preliminary event forces you to reveal a tactic before the final. What belongs in the decision? A Only the preliminary prize; B The final consequences as well. | B. Trace how a local win changes later opportunities. |
| P7 | Both teams can choose a sure second place or a risky first-or-last result. One is rewarded for top three; the other only for first. Must their choice match? A Yes; B No. | B. The same uncertainty can matter differently under different rewards. |
| P8 | A buyer enforces a spending cap below every supplier’s minimum acceptable price. Does enforcing it guarantee a cheaper agreement? A Yes; B No. | B. Commitment can produce no deal when acceptable terms do not overlap. |

| P10 | A group shares a benefit, but one volunteer bears all its cost. Is agreeing that the benefit is valuable enough to predict volunteering? A Yes; B No. | B. Individual cost, others’ actions and incentives also matter. |
| P11 | A player publicly repeats left-right-middle. Does using three directions make the sequence random? A Yes; B No. | B. Variation is compatible with complete predictability. |
| P12 | Your quote wins a job; every other quote was higher. What is a useful next check? A Whether you omitted part of the work; B Assume being selected proves the estimate accurate. | A. Being the most optimistic estimate can explain selection; inspect scope before treating it as evidence of efficiency. |

These short choices test basic transfer. They are deliberately not proof of mastery. The final railway decision rule provides a more open application. Before publication, expand with less obvious distractors and additional independent variants for every objective; do not recycle these as supposedly fresh questions.

### Response-dependent follow-up: exact authored examples

- **Confuses rational with richest:** Fox: “You compared the money. Mira compared money with keeping a promise.” Show her two priorities. Then ask a new example about choosing a quieter, more expensive room. Accept a reason based on the learner’s stated priorities; do not prescribe which room they should prefer.
- **Thinks everyone knows equals common knowledge:** return to Mira’s view before bell one. Fox: “Mira knows Dev’s badge is green. What does she know about what Dev can infer?” Offer the one-green imagined case before replaying two green. Then use a different communication situation for assessment.
- **Treats unchanged sales as no effect:** show “your move → rival response → observed result.” Fox: “Which link did we leave out?” Follow with a fresh sports example, not another café using swapped numbers.
- **Treats one winning move as universally best:** show the same decision under one changed assumption. Fox: “Which fact made your earlier choice useful?” Assess identification of that fact, not willingness to copy the recommended move.
- **AI unsure what a short explanation means:** Fox: “When you say ‘they know,’ do you mean they know the plan, or they know the other person knows it too?” Use one clarification. Do not silently mark mastery or failure.

Reviewed answers determine MCQ scoring. AI may interpret explanations and select reviewed follow-ups; it may not change payoffs, manufacture opponent motives, or create unreviewed scored questions live.

## Independent editorial review of this draft

**Stronger:** each game now identifies the inference, states the opponent’s information and preferences, and explains both branches. The opening contrasts stopping alone with a restriction affecting both competitors; the professor’s foundations are substantive early lessons. Losing does not block explanation. McKinsey is a case investigation rather than a fake simulator. The full chapter, assessment and source coverage fit one document.

**Still weak or risky:** tournament rules are demanding; its losing-player preference needs story review. Self-commitment and collective action may feel familiar unless consequences are clear. Practice distractors are too easy for a final mastery claim. Twelve encounters may be more than a learner wants consecutively; the chapter should permit stopping, not compel a marathon. The cigarette opening is a proposed script, not a claim its exact treatment is approved or that learners will be hooked.

**Before any more game building:** read encounters 1–3 aloud as a table read, using only their written lines and actions. Record every point requiring an extra explanation. Check whether a learner can explain why the result changed and predict a fresh case. Revise the script where it fails. Then review later encounters the same way. A script is ready to implement when it makes sense without the author standing beside it; animation comes afterward.
