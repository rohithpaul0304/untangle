# Opening review 2 — The ads go dark

5 October 2026. Independent editorial pass by the same assistant, starting from the current script. Not a separate reviewer, live player test, or evidence of engagement. Scope: Chapter 1 opening only; no prototype changes.

## Verdict

Keep the historical question. Do not build the current sequence yet. The premise is stronger than the execution: the learner predicts once, then mostly follows an explanation disguised as controls. Several instructions and repeated receipts delay the surprise. The model illustrates a possibility but cannot validate the initial historical prediction.

## Findings, in order of importance

### 1. Historical question, manufactured answer

The learner predicts what happened to actual companies. We then show a made-up model that guarantees savings when both stop. In 1.6, “You expected less. In this model, both keep more” can still feel like proving the learner wrong by selecting convenient rules. The later caveat is too late to carry that distinction alone.

Correction: keep the initial answer as a hypothesis, never score or overturn it with the model. At the result say, “Here is one way that could happen.” Then separately reveal the paper's reported evidence and interpretation. Ask what could change the model result. That is the professor's useful standard: inspect assumptions, not merely accept the surprising answer.

### 2. Most moves are prescribed

“Switch off A,” “Show me B,” “Apply the restriction,” and “Try restarting” send the learner through the author's demonstration. The one free choice follows an explanation that already makes its result obvious. This is guided demonstration, not yet a compelling decision game.

Correction: after establishing the baseline, let the learner choose “Keep my ads running” or “Stop my ads.” Resolve either honestly. Offer the other choice as a comparison, without pretending the initial choice is meaningless. The shared restriction is then the changed condition, not a second forced action masquerading as strategy. A guided first lesson is reasonable; label its meaningful decisions accurately.

### 3. The reveal is buried under navigation

Before seeing both companies save, the script contains the prediction, acknowledgement, Try it, rewind, fixed-spending explanation, controls, first result, rival receipt, repeat choice and restriction. Ten numbered beats conceal substantially more than ten screens. The learner can lose the original question before its payoff.

Correction: aim for the first contrast within four substantive beats: predict → choose with a rival still advertising → watch the shared restriction → compare. This is a pacing target, not a claim about measured completion time. Move rival receipt inspection into optional detail and remove the repeat choice whose outcome has already been shown.

### 4. “Money they make” is ambiguous

The historical prediction can mean sales, profit or stock value. The simulation displays money after advertising but excludes other costs. The research discusses abnormal stock returns, a different measure again. The learner may think these are interchangeable.

Correction: ask, “Would you expect this to hurt the established companies financially?” Use “Money left after ads” consistently in the model. At the source reveal explicitly say the evidence is from stock returns and concerns investor expectations; it is not the model's receipt or a directly observed profit increase of the displayed size.

### 5. Too much Chapter 2 arrives before the first concept

The voluntary restart and counter-response in 1.7 effectively teach the full incentive trap before naming Chapter 1's simpler idea. They require another rewind and add another narrative question. This delays closure of the opening prediction.

Correction: introduce strategic interdependence immediately after comparing stopping alone and together. Leave “Why didn't they stop voluntarily?” as a visible optional preview or the return question in Chapter 2. Do not require the preview before finishing the introductory insight. If the founder wants the complete dilemma first, consciously change chapter scope rather than half-teaching it while withholding its name.

### 6. The final question barely tests transfer

The late-opening shops example repeats the same collective restriction with different nouns. Its long, cautious option is visibly the expected answer. A learner could select it without explaining what changed.

Correction: use a different action and avoid a yes/no moral. Example: two nearby cafés each offer delivery to avoid losing orders to the other. Ask: “Before predicting whether cancelling delivery would save your café money, what would you investigate?” Show two useful pieces of evidence: delivery cost and whether customers would switch to the rival. The explanation must connect both. This checks identifying the interaction, not calculating an unsupported optimal decision. A later independent question should change the setting again.

### 7. Correct prediction is not yet a strong branch

Someone who anticipates advertising savings receives almost the same elementary walkthrough. Their initial insight should not be treated as proof of understanding, but repeating the reveal offers them little.

Correction: use the same short experiment, then challenge the assumption: “What if customers spend much less when both stop advertising?” Ask whether the savings still guarantee a gain. No new numeric result without authored values. Correct explanation: no; compare reduced money in with saved advertising cost. Offer this assumption check to every learner, with extra guidance where needed.

## Recommended shorter sequence

This is a revision direction, not replacement implementation instructions.

1. **Predict:** a neutral historical headline. “Would you expect this to hurt established companies financially?” Save the response without grading it.
2. **Choose:** explicitly labelled model before the restriction. Briefly state fixed total spending and the advertising cost. Rival stays on. Learner keeps or stops their own ads. Show the chosen result and a compact optional comparison.
3. **Change the condition:** both firms lose the advertising option. Show both costs disappearing and the equal-competition result. No new decision is falsely implied by applying an external rule.
4. **Explain the contrast:** “Stopping alone and stopping together are different situations.” Ask the learner to identify the competitor's changed position. Name strategic interdependence here.
5. **Challenge the model:** could lower overall customer spending reverse the benefit? Explain why the fixed-spending assumption matters. This prevents “bans make firms richer” becoming the learned rule.
6. **Resolve the real-world mystery:** the paper reports stock-market evidence consistent with benefits to established firms and discusses entry barriers. Clearly distinguish that evidence from the illustrative game. Keep details expandable.
7. **Transfer and close:** learner identifies their own cost and the rival response in a fresh setting. End with one usable question: “What will everyone else be able to do after my move?” Voluntary restart becomes a Chapter 2 preview.

## What passes this review

- A concrete, counterintuitive real case provides a reason to investigate.
- The four existing model outcomes are arithmetically consistent: 60/60, 100/100, 120/40 and 40/120.
- The model separates unilateral stopping from a shared restriction.
- Existing source cautions correctly avoid claiming the model reproduces the paper.
- No need for another mascot redesign to address the remaining problems.

## What this review cannot establish

Whether the audience cares about this case, understands the receipts, experiences surprise, or voluntarily continues. Only observed learner behaviour can establish those points. Do not replace these uncertainties with a made-up score out of ten.
