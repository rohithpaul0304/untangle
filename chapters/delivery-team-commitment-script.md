# The promise made before you were asked

5 October 2026. Proposed continuation of the founder-approved design-team setup. Full branches and explanation remain for review. Text only; no prototype changes.

Source principle: *The Art of Strategy*, Chapter 1, “Here I Stand”: a believable restriction changes others' available responses, but inflexibility has costs. This workplace story is original, not a reported book or professor case. The key distinction is commitment versus a request people expect can be overridden. A software form alone does not guarantee credibility.

## 1 — Your decision

You lead a design team. This week is fully booked. Sales promised another client a finished design by Friday without checking with you.

Meeting Friday requires two evenings of overtime. Your team can do it this time. Tuesday is available within normal hours. The client prefers Friday, but has not yet said whether Tuesday is possible.

Fox: “How will you handle this request?”

- “Accept Friday. Ask the team to work late.”
- “Offer Tuesday. Ask Sales to change the deadline.”

Author rule: the fictional client will accept Tuesday after being asked. That fact is not known to the player at decision time. State that it is this client's response, not a guarantee about other clients. Neither first choice is marked right or wrong.

## 2A — If you accept Friday

Two evening blocks fill on the team's calendar. The design is delivered Friday.

Client: “Thanks for getting it ready.”
Team member: “We finished. But we gave up two evenings.”

Fox: “You kept the promised date. Your team carried the extra cost.”

Keep a small record: Friday delivered; two evenings used. Do not erase it when the story continues.

## 2B — If you offer Tuesday

Sales contacts the client before treating the new date as agreed.

Sales: “Our team can confirm Tuesday. Would that work?”
Client: “Tuesday is okay. Please check before promising next time.”

The design is delivered Tuesday without overtime.

Fox: “This client accepted the change. Your team kept its evenings, but Sales had to correct a promise.”

Keep a small record: Tuesday delivered; no overtime. Do not invent a lost client to punish this choice.

## 3 — Find the decision that created the pressure

Both branches show the same order:

Sales promises Friday → team checks capacity → someone absorbs the conflict.

Fox: “Different outcomes. Same problem: the date was promised before your team was asked.”

Button: “Move the capacity check earlier.”

This button advances a guided investigation; it is not a graded strategic decision.

## 4 — Compare a request with a supported rule

Branch-aware setup: after the Friday rescue, leadership wants to avoid routine overtime. After the Tuesday correction, leadership wants to avoid unreliable promises. In either case, leadership agrees to review how future dates are confirmed.

Fox: “You can propose either arrangement. What would you put in place?”

- “Ask Sales to check with us before promising dates.”
- “Require a reserved team slot before confirming dates.”

Show a short trade-off before selection: the request leaves room for quick informal exceptions; the reservation rule can delay confirmation or lose an urgent booking. Neither creates extra capacity.

### Request branch

The next client asks for another fully booked Friday. Sales says, “I know we should check first. Could we confirm it and work it out afterward?”

Fox: “The request makes your preference clear. It still leaves room to pressure you after a promise.”

Do not claim Sales must ignore reminders. This authored response demonstrates an available route, not a universal prediction. If first branch was Tuesday, do not claim Sales learned that overtime is guaranteed. The remaining route is an attempt to negotiate another exception.

Button: “Compare the reserved-slot rule.”

### Reserved-slot branch

Leadership agrees that a date is confirmed only when capacity is reserved. This applies to customer confirmations, not just an internal calendar. Sales and clients can see which dates are available. Leadership commits not to waive the rule merely to preserve a promised date.

The same Friday request arrives. Friday has no slot; Tuesday does.

Sales, to client: “Tuesday is the first date we can confirm.”

Fox: “The conversation changed before an impossible date became a promise.”

If the learner chose this branch first, provide optional “Compare a reminder” rather than forcing repetition.

## 5 — Make the causal difference visible

Before: “Please check first.” Sales could still promise a date and ask the team to make it work.

After: a promise requires a reserved slot, and leadership backs that limit. Sales must discuss an available date before confirming.

Fox: “Your team did not suddenly gain more time. Sales gained a reason to believe that an unreserved date would not be honoured.”

Button: “That changes what they can promise.”

## 6 — Name the idea

Concept card: **Credible commitment**.

Fox: “A commitment is credible when others have a reason to believe you will stick to it.”

Fox: “Here, the booking process and leadership's support made the capacity limit believable. Repeating the warning alone did not remove the route around it.”

Understanding check: “If leadership keeps approving unreserved dates whenever a client pushes, what would Sales have reason to expect?”

- “An exception may still be available.”
- “An unreserved date will definitely be refused.”

Explain after either answer: “Repeated exceptions weaken the commitment. A rule people expect you to override does not create the same constraint.” No penalty or withholding the concept after an incorrect answer.

## 7 — Pay for the commitment

A different client now needs Friday and cannot use Tuesday. No reserved Friday capacity exists.

Client: “Then we'll need another design firm.”

Fox: “The rule protects the team's capacity, but it can cost work. It cannot make every client accept Tuesday.”

Leadership honours the stated rule; the client goes elsewhere. Show the actual trade-off: unplanned overtime avoided; booking lost. No celebration of the loss.

Fox: “If leadership isn't willing to accept that cost, this particular commitment won't hold. They need a different plan—such as real extra capacity—not a stronger warning.”

An exception policy can be designed for genuinely different circumstances. It must be explicit and supported by resources; do not turn this lesson into 'never make exceptions' or a demand that individual employees enforce a policy without authority.

## 8 — Use it at work

Work tip: “When a boundary keeps failing, ask what makes everyone expect it can be overridden.”

Then investigate:
- Where does the promise become official?
- Who can approve an exception?
- Who actually carries its cost?

Fox: “A useful commitment changes the decision before the pressure arrives. It needs support from the people who can enforce it.”

Button: “Save this idea.”

## Branch review

- Friday uses two evenings before Friday, never Saturday work to meet a past deadline.
- Tuesday is offered, not unilaterally imposed; the client's acceptance is explicit.
- Both first branches preserve their different consequences; neither proves every future client will respond the same way.
- The learner proposes a company rule; leadership supplies the authority. The team lead does not magically control Sales or company policy.
- Software is not the source of credibility by itself. The script states the external confirmation process, backing, and consequence of recurring overrides.
- The second client's refusal makes the cost of commitment concrete.
- User approved the initial decision setup, not yet this complete lesson. Editorial review does not establish engagement or learning.
