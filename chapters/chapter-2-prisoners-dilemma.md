## Purpose and cast

Discover why each person can have an individual reason to take a move that leaves both worse off than cooperation. The learner is a treasure hunter; a second hunter is clearly labelled “Other explorer.” The approved fox is the guide and does not secretly play the opponent. ## Part 1 — Experience the treasure dilemma

### Meet the situation

You and another treasure hunter arrive at a forest clearing. An enchanted chest sits between you.

Fox: “You both found this chest. How will you collect the treasure?”

Share: hold out your hands to split the treasure.
Grab: reach in to take more for yourself.

Fox: “Let’s try the different moves together first.”

The chest has fixed, imaginary rewards. This must be clear; do not pretend its mechanism naturally follows from the number of gems. Its artificial logic remains an editorial issue.

### Both share

The other explorer holds out open hands.
Fox: “They’re sharing. Let’s share too.”
Action: Share.
Three gems float into each bag.
Fox: “Three each. Sharing worked for both of you.”
Continue: Try grabbing.

### You grab; they share

The practice scene resets; both bags empty.
Fox: “This time, try taking more for yourself.”
Action: Grab.
Five gems go to you; none to the other explorer.
Fox: “You got five instead of three. They got nothing.”
Compare your share reward, 3, with your grab reward, 5.
Fox: “But you’re not the only one who can grab.”
Continue: Let them grab.

### They grab; you share

The scene resets. The other explorer reaches towards the chest.
Fox: “Now they’re grabbing. Let’s see what happens if you share.”
Action: Share.
Five gems go to them; none to you.
Fox: “They got five. You got none.”
No failure sound: this is an investigation.
Continue: Try grabbing too.

### Both grab

The other explorer is ready to grab again.
Fox: “What happens when you both try to take more?”
Action: Grab.
The chest releases one gem to each.
Fox: “One each.”
Recall the first attempt, three gems each.
Fox: “You both tried to get more. But sharing together gave each of you more than this.”
Continue: Show me why.

## Part 2 — Name and apply the pattern

### Why grabbing is tempting

Hold the other explorer’s move fixed in each comparison.

Fox: “When they share, grabbing gives you five instead of three.”
Fox: “When they grab, grabbing gives you one instead of nothing.”
Fox: “So grabbing gives you more whichever move they make. And they face exactly the same temptation.”

### Prisoner’s dilemma

Fox: “This is the prisoner’s dilemma: each person has a reason to grab, but both would get more if they shared.”

Keep the name visible alongside the comparison: both grab, 1 each; both share, 3 each. Defer Nash equilibrium. Do not imply that selfishness is the only possible human motivation or that sharing is strategically dominant in this reward model. The numerical game assumes each explorer ranks outcomes by their own gem count.

### Make your own move

Fox: “This time, you won’t see their choice until you’ve both moved.”
Fox: “What will you do?”
Actions: Share / Grab.
Reveal both choices together; use the same authored rewards. No correct-choice badge. Explain the actual outcome without moralising.

If both grab: “You both protected yourselves from getting nothing. But you each ended up with one.”
Continue: Does this happen outside games?

## Part 3 — Cigarette advertising

### A different setting

Show two unbranded cigarette companies and an old television. No character smokes; focus on advertising costs and competition.

Fox: “Companies can face a similar problem. Let’s look at cigarette advertising.”
Fox: “Imagine two companies competing for the same customers.”

This is a simplified teaching analogy, not historical company accounts or evidence that actual advertising markets always form a prisoner’s dilemma.

### The temptation to advertise

Both companies initially spend nothing on television ads.
Fox: “If neither advertises, both save that expense.”

One starts advertising. Some buyers move towards it.
Fox: “But if one advertises while the other stays quiet, it may win customers from its rival.”
Fox: “Now the other company has a reason to advertise too.”
Continue: See what happens.

### Both pay to compete

Both advertise. Money flows to the advertising space.
Fox: “Both are spending money to compete for those customers.”
Fox: “In this simplified example, both would keep more money if neither advertised. But either company is afraid to stop alone.”

Connection: in treasure, sharing while the other grabs leaves you worse off; here, stopping while your rival continues may lose you customers.
Fox: “Different situation. Similar temptation.”

### The rule changes

Television ads disappear together.
Fox: “In the United States, cigarette advertising on television and radio was banned from January 2, 1971.”
Fox: “A company no longer had to worry that its rival could keep using those channels while it stopped.”

Do not mistake the end of this toy cost animation for a full explanation of the historical ban.
Continue: What did researchers find?

### The historical twist

Fox: “Researchers Mark Mitchell and Harold Mulherin studied major tobacco companies’ share prices while the ban was being developed.”
Fox: “They found gains beyond what general stock-market movements would predict. That suggested investors expected established tobacco companies to benefit.”

Reveal separately: advertising restrictions could impede new-brand entry; required anti-smoking broadcast airtime previously associated with cigarette advertising also ended.
Fox: “So the real story involved more than saving money on ads. Changing the rules also changed the competition.”

Source note: Mark L. Mitchell and J. Harold Mulherin, Finessing the Political System: The Cigarette Advertising Ban, pp. 855–862, user-supplied reading. Publication metadata remains unverified. Stock returns concern investor expectations; do not present the paper as proof that advertising bans generally fail to reduce smoking. A second application waits for the founder’s material.

## Part 4 — Recap

Your outcome depends on both people’s choices.
An individually tempting move can leave both sides worse off.
Changing rules can change incentives.

Fox: “First you experienced the dilemma. Then you spotted it in a different setting.”
Chapter complete.
