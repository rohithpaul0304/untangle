# Chapter 1 — You’re not choosing alone

Status: text-level working draft, 5 October 2026. The founder accepts a long chapter; prioritise understanding over a short total duration. Scenario and dialogue need founder review before any prototype. This supersedes the old Chapter 1 assignments in the historical course notes. More supplied material can be added to the source ledger below.

## The chapter’s job

Help a beginner recognise a strategic situation: what happens to me depends on what I choose and what someone else chooses. Introduce the habit of looking at the other person’s goals, choices and information before acting. Competition and cooperation both belong in game theory. Do not make game theory sound like a method for tricking people, a guarantee of winning, or a claim that people care only about money.

The learner should leave able to describe who is involved, what each person wants, what they can do, what they know, and how their choices combine. They should also understand that a prediction depends on its assumptions.

## Cast and setting

You are visiting a festival with a friend. Your friend is introduced as “Your friend, Maya,” with an unmistakable human silhouette and a separate colour from yours. Labels continue to show “You” and “Your friend”; names are optional flavour, not required to understand the task.

The approved fox is your guide, compact at the bottom. It is not an opponent and does not secretly choose Maya’s moves. The scene contains two clearly different places: a fountain and a clock tower. Both are reachable and labelled; neither is automatically better.

This is an original coordination scenario, not an adaptation of the treasure chest or a textbook exercise. The actual task is to meet your friend. Reuniting the characters is the reward; no artificial money amounts are needed.

## Journey 1 — A choice you can make alone

You are at the entrance. A decoration stand offers a blue wristband and a yellow wristband. There is no shortage and no one else’s decision changes what you receive.

Fox: “Pick the colour you like.”

Action: tap either wristband. It appears on your explorer’s wrist. Both choices work; there is no correct-answer badge.

Fox: “That choice was about your own preference.”

Purpose: establish a simple personal decision before adding another decision-maker. Do not claim that choosing a product can never be strategic; in this simplified scene prices and availability are fixed and there is no bargaining.

## Journey 2 — Now someone else matters

You and Maya enter from different gates. You need to meet. You can each walk to the fountain or the clock tower.

Fox: “Find your friend. You can meet at the fountain or the clock tower.”

First guided attempt: Maya’s destination is visible. She walks towards the fountain.

Fox: “Your friend is heading to the fountain. Where will you go?”

Actions: “Go to the fountain” / “Go to the clock tower.”

If you pick the fountain: both arrive together, wave and reunite. Fox: “You met because you chose the same place.”

If you pick the tower: the camera shows both locations with one explorer at each. Fox: “You reached the tower. Your friend reached the fountain. Let’s bring you together.” Action: “Walk to the fountain.” This finishes the meeting without treating the learner as a failure.

Second attempt: clearly labelled “Try the other situation.” Both return to the gates; Maya now heads to the tower.

Fox: “This time, your friend is heading to the tower.”

The same two actions remain. Following her destination succeeds; choosing the other destination separates you. Explain the result without pretending that one landmark is always the right answer.

Reflection: Fox: “What changed between those two trips?” The scene highlights Maya’s changed destination. Optional action “Show both trips” replays the contrast; optional spoken or typed reflection is never required to continue.

### Concept reveal — Game theory

Fox: “Choosing your wristband was about what you liked. Meeting your friend depends on what both of you do.”

Definition: “Game theory studies situations where each person’s outcome depends on their own choices and other people’s choices.”

Fox: “Here, the goal is to meet—not to beat each other.”

This is the first formal concept reveal. Keep “Game theory” visible during the next practice.

## Journey 3 — Look at the other person’s goal

An action area lets you inspect the scene, not answer a multiple-choice quiz. Tap a character, either landmark, or the plan card to see the relevant detail.

“You”: wants to meet Maya.
“Your friend”: wants to meet you.
“Available choices”: fountain or clock tower.
“Outcome”: together if the choices match; separated if they differ.
“Information”: in the guided attempts you saw where Maya was going.

Reveal the words beside their concrete meaning, one at a time:

- Players: the people whose choices matter.
- Choices: what each person can do.
- Outcomes: what happens when their choices combine.
- Information: what each person knows before choosing.
- Rules: how the choices lead to outcomes, including who chooses when.

A strategy is a plan for choosing what to do. This is a short vocabulary note, not a separate lesson on formal strategy spaces.

Now introduce different preferences. Maya says: “The fountain is loud. I’d rather meet somewhere quiet.” The tower is marked quiet. She has not announced her destination.

Fox: “We know something she cares about. Where might she prefer to meet?”

Actions remain physical destinations. In this authored scenario Maya selects the tower. If you go there, you meet. If you go to the fountain, she leaves a note directing you to the tower and you can reunite. Explain that her stated preference was useful evidence, not a promise or mind-reading power.

### Concept reveal — Rationality

“Rationality means choosing in a way that fits your goals and what you believe.”

Fox: “Your friend cares about a quiet place. That matters when we think about her choice.”

Add one sentence: “People can care about comfort, fairness, friendship or money—and they can still make mistakes.”

Author note: goal-directed, internally consistent choice is the beginner approximation. Do not equate rationality with selfishness, emotional detachment, perfect information, or always reaching the best outcome. Many models assume rationality as a useful starting point, not an established fact about every human. Not all game-theory models require common knowledge of rationality.

## Journey 4 — Do we both know the plan?

Return to the two gates. Both landmarks are available again. This time Maya’s destination is hidden.

Fox: “You can’t see her move this time. What would help you meet?”

Action: “Check our plan.”

Situation A: you receive a private festival update: “Meet at the fountain.” Maya’s phone shows “Update not received.” You know the suggestion, but have no confirmation that she knows it.

Fox: “You got the message. Your friend hasn’t. Knowing the plan yourself isn’t enough.”

Action: “Make sure we both hear it.”

Situation B: the organiser addresses both explorers together. Each visibly acknowledges the instruction and sees the other acknowledge it: “You two, meet at the fountain.”

Fox: “Now you both know the plan—and you both know the other heard it.”

Action: “Go to the fountain.” The characters reunite.

### Concept reveal — Common knowledge

“Something is common knowledge when everyone involved knows it, everyone knows that everyone knows it, and that shared understanding continues.”

Fox: “We don’t have to keep guessing whether the other person heard the plan.”

Author note: the public-announcement scene is an idealised demonstration. We assume both hear and understand it, the instruction is credible, and their awareness of the shared announcement is itself shared. Two private messages, delivery receipts alone, or one mutual acknowledgement do not by themselves establish the full formal infinite hierarchy. Common knowledge of the meeting instruction is distinct from common knowledge of rationality. Do not teach that shared information forces agreement or removes all uncertainty.

### Independent practice — A new meeting point

A public announcement addresses both explorers together: “Meet at the clock tower.” Their shared acknowledgement is visible; Maya’s actual move is not shown.

Fox: “You both heard the new plan. Where will you go?”

The learner selects a destination. The reviewed character behaviour follows the announcement. Both reunite at the tower; if the learner selects the fountain, show the separation and allow a purposeful correction.

Do not reveal Maya’s destination before the learner acts. This is post-teaching evidence about using the shared plan, not proof of complete mastery of common knowledge.

## Journey 5 — Why do people pay after a taxi ride?

Transition from the festival to a short everyday story. A taxi has completed its trip. A passenger sees the agreed fare; the driver waits for payment. Neither is labelled dishonest by default.

Fox: “The ride is over. Why do most passengers still pay?”

Action: “Look at both people.” Tapping the passenger and driver reveals short, original descriptions of possible goals: keeping a promise, receiving a fair payment, avoiding conflict, earning a living, and being treated fairly. These are possibilities to consider, not a universal diagnosis of either person.

Fox: “If we only count money, we might miss something important.”

The passenger pays. The driver acknowledges the payment, and both move on.

Fox: “People may care about fairness and promises even when they don’t expect to meet again.”

Research connection: the supplied Mostly Economics post discusses Kaushik Basu’s taxi-payment thought experiment. It challenges an explanation of orderly exchange based entirely on selfishness: explaining why the passenger pays also requires considering the driver’s response and shared values. Present this as a thought experiment, not an empirical study proving why all passengers pay.

Do not reward fare evasion, turn the scene into a moral correctness quiz, or teach that fairness is inherently irrational. If someone values fairness, that value can be part of what they are trying to achieve. The original book excerpt’s different taxi anecdote is separate: its later driver letter is explicitly invented by the authors and must not be presented as real testimony.

Source: [Mostly Economics, 20 July 2012](https://mostlyeconomics.wordpress.com/2012/07/20/why-we-do-not-try-to-walk-off-without-paying-after-a-tax-ride/). The linked underlying Basu paper has not been independently read here.

## End-of-chapter check — Find what needs another look

Added at the founder’s request on 5 October 2026. The teaching journey remains scenario-based. A short multiple-choice check follows the learning scenes and comes before the personalised final recap. Multiple choice is intentional here; it must not replace the playable journey.

Fox: “Let’s see what stuck—and what we should try once more.”

Show one question at a time. Start with five questions, each checking a specific learning goal. Correctness comes from a reviewed answer key, not an AI guess. AI adapts the explanation and next reviewed question based on the chosen answer and the learner’s prior attempts. No extra “I understand” checkbox is needed; it would not establish understanding.

### Question 1 — The assumptions introduced here

Prompt: “Which two assumptions did we introduce in this chapter?”

A. Rationality and common knowledge. [Correct]
B. Rationality and partial knowledge.
C. Random behaviour and common knowledge.
D. Random behaviour and partial knowledge.

Reviewed explanation: “Rationality means choosing in a way that fits goals and beliefs. Common knowledge means a fact is known by everyone involved, with that awareness shared at every level.”

Important qualifier: these are the two assumptions introduced in this course’s starting model, not requirements of every game-theory model. Other models explicitly study incomplete information, mistakes and bounded reasoning. Avoid the unqualified wording “the two assumptions of game theory.”

If B: provisionally target common knowledge; rationality was selected correctly. If C: provisionally target rationality. If D: both may need attention; ask a discriminating follow-up rather than claiming two misconceptions from one answer. Even A only establishes terminology recall; later questions test meaning.

### Question 2 — When do choices interact?

Prompt: “Which situation is a game-theory problem?”

A. You pick a wristband colour from a stand with unlimited stock and fixed prices.
B. You and a friend separately choose where to meet; you meet only if your locations match. [Correct]
C. You choose which song to hear alone, with no effect on anyone else.
D. You choose a solo walking route; its travel time is fixed and nobody else affects it.

Reviewed explanation: “In B, the result depends on both people’s choices. In the other simplified situations, another person’s decision does not change your result.”

If A, C or D: return to a brief contrast between a fixed personal choice and an interacting choice, then use a fresh reviewed scenario. Do not score a bare action such as “turn left” as inherently non-strategic; its context could make it strategic.

### Question 3 — Rational does not mean selfish

Prompt: “A passenger pays the agreed taxi fare because keeping promises matters to them. Can that fit rationality?”

A. Yes. Keeping promises can be part of what they value. [Correct]
B. No. A rational person always keeps as much money as possible.
C. No. Values and emotions can never be part of a rational choice.
D. We can only call them rational if the driver threatens them.

Reviewed explanation: “A person’s goals can include fairness and keeping promises. Rationality does not require valuing money above everything else.”

B targets narrow monetary self-interest; C targets the emotionless-choice misconception; D targets an unsupported requirement for punishment. These remain tentative interpretations of the response, to be checked by another example.

### Question 4 — Knowing versus shared awareness

Prompt: “Which situation best illustrates common knowledge of a meeting plan in our simplified scene?”

A. Only you receive the plan privately.
B. Both receive private messages, but neither knows the other received one.
C. A clear announcement addresses both of you together, and it is understood to have been heard by both, with that awareness shared. [Correct]
D. You guess your friend probably heard the plan.

Reviewed explanation: “C provides shared awareness. Knowing something yourself—or even both knowing it privately—is different from knowing that it is shared.”

A targets private versus shared information. B targets everyone-knows versus common-knowledge confusion. D targets assumption versus confirmed shared awareness. The formal idealised public-announcement assumptions in Journey 4 still apply; real-world hearing alone is not proof of common knowledge.

### Question 5 — Prediction versus certainty

Prompt: “Your friend says they prefer a quiet place, but does not promise where they will go. What can we conclude?”

A. They must choose the quiet meeting point.
B. Their preference is useful evidence, but we do not know their choice for certain. [Correct]
C. Their preference tells us nothing at all.
D. Their preference guarantees that you will meet.

Reviewed explanation: “A stated goal helps you consider possible choices. It is not a guaranteed prediction or an agreed plan.”

A and D target overconfidence; C targets ignoring relevant goals. Use a follow-up that distinguishes an expressed preference from an explicit shared plan.

## Adaptive follow-up rules

Example: learner selects “Rationality and partial knowledge.”

Fox: “Rationality is right. Let’s look at the other part: knowing something isn’t always the same as knowing it is shared.”

Show a ten-second authored contrast: a private meeting message on one phone versus a public announcement to both friends. Then ask a NEW question about a team captain changing the assembly point. Do not repeat the just-revealed question as evidence of understanding.

Example: learner correctly recognises the two assumptions but chooses an isolated personal decision as a game.

Keep the assumptions result. Focus the next explanation on interacting outcomes, then ask about two drivers trying to pass on a narrow road. A changed destination alone is not enough; make the relevant interaction explicit.

Example: all initial answers correct.

Avoid unnecessary repetition. Finish the recap with specific evidence: “You recognised interacting choices, distinguished a shared plan, and considered goals beyond money.” Do not claim permanent mastery. A brief future review can still be appropriate.

For a wrong answer: acknowledge the part supported by the response, explain one distinction, optionally replay the relevant scene, then give a fresh reviewed question on that distinction. If another answer is wrong, use a simpler contrast. Do not turn the chapter into endless questions. There is no fixed three-follow-up limit. Continue with useful, varied practice while the learner is engaged and unresolved concepts remain. If attempts stop providing new evidence, change the explanation or replay the relevant scene rather than repeat similar questions indefinitely. Make pause and resume available; preserve progress and unresolved concepts between sessions. Longer duration does not change existing unlock requirements.

For a correct answer: show a one-sentence reason, then move to the next objective. One correct guess is evidence from one item, not proof of mastery. If earlier attempts conflict, use one transfer question to check the concept rather than repeat definitions.

Do not infer understanding or confidence from response speed alone. Optional “Not sure yet” helps choose a gentler follow-up but must not be compulsory. If we ask for a short explanation, interpret it against an authored rubric; accept plain language and never penalise grammar. Uncertain interpretations trigger a clearer question, not a confident diagnosis.

## Content and backend requirements for the adaptive check

Each published item has an ID, concept tag, relevant misconception tags per option, answer key, explanation, difficulty, prerequisite, source/rules version and reviewed follow-up IDs. At least two fresh alternatives per objective are needed before claiming the dynamic paths are ready. The five questions above establish the baseline; their full alternative bank is still to be authored and reviewed.

Convex stores question version, selected option, correctness, previous evidence, explanation/hint exposure, and the reason for selecting the next item. Scoring is checked against the published key. AI can phrase constrained feedback and recommend an eligible reviewed item; it cannot invent a different correct answer, change rules or fetch content from locked chapters. If AI is unavailable, use the saved explanation and a rule-based next item.

Completion and understanding remain distinct. Save unresolved concepts explicitly; do not award mastery because the learner exhausted the question cap. Keep the existing independent-check chapter-access requirements until an explicit product decision changes them. Hints and corrections do not subtract chapter XP. The final recap names what was demonstrated and what needs another look.

This is text-level assessment design. Convex storage, AI routing and the alternative question bank are not implemented or tested yet.

## How this shapes the app

Every reviewed scenario records players, goals, available choices, rules, information, timing, authored outcomes and the exact concept being taught. AI can identify which part the learner struggled with and select another reviewed scenario or explanation. It must not invent motives as facts, silently change rules to force a desired result, or claim certainty about another person’s action.

A discovery attempt, a prompted move and a post-explanation independent action are different evidence. Save them separately. Correcting a journey after the destination was shown is not an independent success. A good outcome achieved by chance is not evidence of good reasoning. A bad outcome from a reasonable move under incomplete information is not automatically an error.

All learning starts with a concrete action and a visible consequence. Reflection follows the first attempts; the concept name and explanation follow reflection; guided and independent practice follow teaching; a recap follows practice. No points, clocks or animation should hide the actual learning task.

Do not build a new prototype until this text-level story is agreed. The chapter may be long. Organise it into short, resumable sections, with one idea and one clear action at a time. Let discovery, explanation, guided application, independent practice and adaptive checks take the time they need. Do not pad it with repetitive questions or add advanced topics solely to extend its duration. Runtime and screen count need a later pacing test; do not claim this fits five minutes.
