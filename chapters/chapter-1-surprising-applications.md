# Chapter 1 — Applications that change how you think

Text proposals, 5 October 2026. These refine the existing chapter rather than adding another compulsory set of stages. Source review: supplied professor transcript, The Art of Strategy Chapter 1, and supplied McKinsey article. New scenes and numbers below are original teaching models, not quotations or empirical results. No player testing has occurred.

## Editorial change

An everyday application must do more than recommend communicating, planning or considering others. It needs an initially plausible choice, an observable consequence, an explanation of the hidden mechanism, and a second situation where the learner can apply or qualify the lesson. Do not force the learner to make a mistake to unlock the explanation. A sound first choice receives an appropriate outcome and the same conceptual depth.

Retain the visual opening as a controls-and-foundations encounter. Its basic basketball lesson is not sufficient as the chapter's main intellectual hook. Preview a later surprising question, then progress to deeper reasoning. Avoid claiming every learner will find an example surprising.

## 1. You won. Why should that worry you?

Full candidate storyboard: [The Mystery Box](chapter-1-auction-story.md), including every bid outcome, concept reveal, personal-use contrast and everyday transfer.

Placement: expand the private-information stage. Source: the book's discussion of the winner's curse in tale 9.

Play: three restorers bid fictional tokens for a sealed box of parts. Everyone can resell the contents for the same amount, but has an imperfect estimate. You see your estimate, 80 tokens, and decide a maximum bid before the others reveal theirs. The first illustrative round locks the contents' resale value at 60 and the other estimates at 40 and 60. These are authored values, not a probability model or recommended bidding formula. The box does not change after bidding.

For this worked example each rival bids its estimate; state that this is a simple scripted policy, not an optimal auction strategy. Highest bid wins and pays its bid. Resolve ties with a disclosed fixed tie rule. If you bid 75, you win and then discover a 15-token loss. If you choose a lower bid or decline, resolve that choice honestly and let you inspect the same outcome comparison. Distinguish winning the box from making a profitable purchase.

Reveal: all bidders were estimating the same unknown resale value. Winning can select for the most optimistic estimate. Their lower estimates are a reason to reassess confidence, not proof they are right. One worked round illustrates the mechanism; it does not establish an optimal bid or prove all winners overpay.

Concept: winner's curse — winning a competition can be a warning when winning selects an overly optimistic estimate of a shared uncertain value.

Transfer: a second box has a known resale value but different personal uses for different buyers. Ask whether the same warning follows. It need not: another buyer may genuinely value it more. Everyday connection: competing for a used collectible or agreeing to a job because your time estimate was the lowest. The latter is an analogy about selected optimism, not an identical auction.

Important limit: numerical multi-round practice needs a reviewed value/noise distribution and bidding policies. Do not produce arbitrary repeated losses or generalise the example to financial advice.

## 2. Can removing a choice get you a better offer?

Placement: strengthen the commitment stage, replacing the current obviously bad locked-ticket example as its main event. Source: tale 5, Here I Stand. This numerical interaction is our model, not the book's historical negotiation.

Play: a partner proposes how to split ten game tokens. Your stated goal in this round is your own token count; the partner's stated goal is theirs. In the flexible version, you can accept a positive offer or reject it and both receive zero. There is one offer and no later negotiation. Explain that the partner is a scripted, goal-directed model, not a prediction of all people.

Before the offer, you may install a visible rule that automatically rejects offers below four tokens for you. This changes what the partner knows can be accepted. With integer offers, a partner seeking the largest own share and expecting you to accept any positive amount offers one in the flexible model and four with the binding rule. Show the two sequences side by side. Rejection of zero is an explicit tie rule in the model. A spoken claim that you will reject is not the same as the enforced rule.

Reveal: the rule removed your ability to accept one, but changed the other player's offer. The gain came from their response to a credible restriction, not stubbornness by itself.

Changed situation: the partner has an outside option worth seven tokens. A split leaving them only six no longer beats that alternative. Holding the same rule may mean no deal. State the outside option before the learner chooses a commitment. Show both outcomes without changing rules after the choice.

Concept: credible commitment — a restriction others believe will really bind your future actions. It can influence their choices and also destroy a possible agreement.

Everyday connection: agreeing to a genuine minimum contribution before organising a shared activity changes what others can expect from you. Do not teach fake claims about authority, coercion or that hard rules always improve relationships. Keep fairness and repeated relationships visible as later limits.

## 3. Why would someone turn down free tokens?

Placement: deepen rationality and the taxi perspective stage. Source: professor's explicit rejection of rationality-as-emotionlessness. The course menu lists ultimatum games, but their actual lectures were not supplied; this is an original introductory split-game adaptation, not a reconstruction of those lessons.

Play: another character offers you one token from ten, keeping nine. Accept distributes the tokens; reject gives both zero. Let the learner choose without labelling a personal preference right or wrong.

Then switch roles. Offer a split to two explicitly different character models. One ranks any positive personal payout above zero. The other ranks rejecting a strongly unequal split above accepting it, according to a visible preference card. The same 9/1 offer receives different responses. Do not secretly invent the second character's priorities after rejection.

Reveal: the second character did not necessarily fail to count. They valued the response to unfairness more than that token. Rationality concerns consistency with goals and beliefs; it does not establish that the goal is admirable or that every real rejection is rational.

Transfer: a character chooses a smaller reward to keep a promise. Ask which stated goal explains that choice, then contrast with a genuinely inconsistent choice under fixed stated preferences. Everyday use: when a proposed deal fails, examine fairness, trust and priorities before assuming the other person cannot reason.

Concept: preferences and rationality. Formal ultimatum-game predictions and evidence belong in a later lesson, not an unsupported behavioural claim here.

## 4. Everybody knows. Why is everybody still waiting?

Placement: improve the shared-knowledge opening; retain the optional small green-eyed puzzle. Source: professor's distinction between knowing and knowing that others know.

Play: two teammates must press their start buttons together. Each privately receives “the equipment is ready,” but neither can see whether the other received it. Show both viewpoints to the learner while clearly distinguishing the player's overview from each character's knowledge. Starting alone fails the team task under the announced rules. Characters are waiting for confirmation of readiness and a shared start signal, not lacking the readiness fact themselves.

A public countdown is now seen and heard by both. Make mutual visibility explicit. The equipment has not changed; the characters can now coordinate on a start. The countdown supplies a coordination signal as well as shared awareness, so do not attribute the entire effect solely to common knowledge of readiness.

Concept reveal: distinguish shared facts, knowledge about others' information, and coordination. In the ideal model, everyone observing and understanding the same public event, with shared awareness of that observation, can establish common knowledge. A normal notification or two read receipts does not automatically prove infinitely nested shared awareness.

Transfer: compare private messages cancelling an old meeting location with a shared announcement and an agreed confirmation process. Ask what uncertainty each step removes, not whether pressing “send” magically ensures coordination.

Use the guided green-eyed one/two-person example when demonstrating the pure information reasoning more precisely. Its exact announcement remains “at least one,” never “everyone.”

## 5. Why would you let a competitor take customers?

Placement: McKinsey railway finale, preserved after the preceding learning encounters. Source: the full supplied article, Making game theory work for managers.

Play: a small new morning train service appears on one route. The intuitive urge is to defend every passenger. Inspect the established network's useful connections, operating costs, whether new travellers are entering the market and the newcomer’s limited scope. Compare responding aggressively with tolerating small entry under the article's stated growing-demand scenario.

Reveal: keeping every passenger may cost more than the business it protects. A rival may attract additional rail users rather than taking all its riders from the established service. Under suitable conditions tolerating entry can be preferable to a costly fight.

Then change conditions: demand stops growing and the rival competes more broadly. Keep network and cost assumptions explicit. The earlier conclusion is no longer automatically justified; demand alone still cannot identify the best response. Preserve all four entry approaches, four responses and four drivers across the full case, plus the machinery comparison.

Concept: evaluate consequences across possible conditions; success is not identical to defeating every rival. This is qualitative analysis of model scenarios, not invented profit data or a universal recommendation to accommodate competitors.

Everyday transfer: two student clubs want attendance. Compare different event dates that could grow total participation with the same date and a fixed audience. State the clubs' goals and costs. Ask what would need to be true for both to benefit; do not claim student clubs reproduce railway economics exactly.

## Acceptance check for every application

Can we name the plausible initial belief, the consequence that challenges it, the mechanism behind the consequence, and a changed case where the learner must reconsider? Is the learner making a meaningful choice with enough information? Are game rules fixed before action? If the scene only concludes “plan ahead” or “communicate clearly,” revise it again.
