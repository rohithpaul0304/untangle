# Chapter 1: six stories built around choices

Draft 3 storyboard · 5 October 2026 · Text review only; not learner-tested.

This document proposes revised presentation for all six main stories in CHAPTER-1-SCRIPT.md. It supersedes opening storyboard v2 as the current storyboard proposal, not the full curriculum. Keep the master’s twelve MCQs, twelve separate practice items, optional stories and source notes. No prototype change is implied.

## Shared direction

Player chooses before the explanation. Choices may be predictions, experiments, questions to ask, evidence to inspect or plans to test. Not every screen needs an MCQ. Each committed response replaces the screen with a visible consequence and short dialogue. No appended green boxes.

A choice must affect the next scene, the evidence order or the plan being examined. Do not give two cosmetic answers that lead immediately to the same speech. A later shared teaching point is fine. No hidden rule changes after choosing. Exploratory decisions receive consequences, not moral judgments. Knowledge checks receive clear feedback.

Fox stays compact at the bottom, asks the question, reacts to the scene and points at the cause. No background jiggling. One instruction at a time; readable without sound; reduced-motion equivalent; saved progress at every response. Each story ends with its concept and a usable takeaway. Short screens do not require simple ideas.

## 1. The ads go dark

**Activity:** predict, experiment, compare. **Concept:** strategic interdependence.

### 1A — Who gains?

Two televisions switch off. Headline: “Cigarette ads disappear from TV and radio.”

Fox: “Would you expect the established cigarette companies to lose money—or benefit?”

Choices: **Expect them to lose** / **They might benefit** / **Not sure yet**.

New screen acknowledges without grading: “Let’s investigate.” This appears over the next scene; it is not another Continue screen. History is not scored using invented figures.

### 1B — Your experiment

Label: “Fictional companies · Before the ban”. Both advertise. Your receipt animates: 100 received − 40 on ads = 60 left after ads.

Fox: “Your rival keeps advertising. Will you keep your ads or stop them?”

Rule: “Total customer spending stays the same. Ads affect which company gets it.” Optional complete rules are available before choosing.

Choices: **Keep my ads** / **Stop my ads**.

- Keep: both ad bills take coins; your total stays 60. Fox: “You kept your sales, but you both still pay for ads.”
- Stop: your bill disappears; customers move to the rival; you keep 40. Fox: “You saved 40 on ads, but lost 60 in sales.”

These are experiments, not tests of calculating undisclosed demand. Same authored model for both branches. Button: **See the ban take effect**.

### 1C — The changed condition

Both TVs are now off. Each company receives 100 and pays no ad bill. If yours was already off, only the rival’s switch moves.

Fox: “Now neither company pays. Neither has the advantage of advertising alone.”

Two snapshots: your ads OFF/rival ON → 40; your ads OFF/rival OFF → 100. For Keep players, first is labelled “If you had stopped alone”.

Question: “Your ads are off in both. What changed?”

Choices: **The rival’s ads** / **Your ad bill**.

Rival: highlight the other TV. “Yes. Their choice changed your result.” Bill: highlight zero cost twice. “Your bill is zero in both. The other TV is the difference.”

Then: **Strategic interdependence — your result depends on what you choose AND what others choose.**

### 1D — Back to the real mystery

Remove all coin figures. Label “One explanation researchers considered”. Show a familiar brand with customers who recognise it; then a new brand looking for ways to reach them.

Fox: “Both lose TV advertising. Which already has customers who recognise its name?”

Choices: **Established brand** / **New brand**.

Established: customers recognise the familiar sign. New: focus on the empty recognition space before showing the familiar sign. Both receive: “Established brands already had recognition. Removing an advertising channel could make entry harder for new brands. Other advertising channels remained.”

Fox: “Stock-market evidence suggested investors expected established firms to benefit. Our invented coins aren’t the historical results.”

Close: “Losing an option can help if it changes your rivals’ position too.” Short boundary visible: “Our experiment held spending fixed. Lower spending could change the result.” Move the full assumption exercise into follow-up practice; retain it in the curriculum.

Stop or **Try a workplace decision**. Opening choice remains a prediction, not a mark of mastery.

## 2. Your idea works. Your manager says no.

**Activity:** investigate a refusal and revise a proposal. **Concept:** rationality and incentives.

### 2A — The refusal

You tested a reporting tool. Result: “Five hours saved every week.” Manager: “Keep doing the report manually.”

Fox: “The trial worked. What will you ask?”

Choices: **“Do you trust the results?”** / **“What worries you about using it?”**

Results branch: manager opens the trial report. “The saving looks real. My concern is what leadership does with it.” Offer **Ask about that concern** on the next screen.

Concern branch: manager opens a staffing review directly. “Reported workload helps justify our team. I’m worried fewer hours will mean losing a position.” Label: “Manager’s concern—not a confirmed decision.”

### 2B — Change your proposal

Fox: “What will you do with that information?”

Choices: **Ask leadership about staffing** / **Show what the freed time could cover**.

- Leadership: “No staffing decision has been made. Show us how you would use the capacity.” A question mark remains on staffing; no false promise.
- Capacity: player moves a five-hour block from reporting into delayed work. Manager: “This gives us a stronger case. We still need leadership’s response.” No automatic approval.

### 2C — Name what changed

Display your original pitch “less reporting” beside revised pitch “less reporting, time for delayed work, clarify staffing”.

Fox: “You saw time saved. Your manager saw a possible cost to the team.”

**Rationality — choosing what best serves your goals, given what you believe.**

**Incentive — a benefit or cost that makes a choice more attractive.**

Neither means selfishness or perfect information. Checking the belief is part of understanding the decision.

Short transfer: Mira values keeping a prior promise more than an extra ₹300. Ask **Does taking less money prove irrationality?** Yes / No. Explain goals and beliefs after either. Keep the fuller Mira illustration available in practice.

Takeaway: “Before repeating your pitch, ask what accepting it would cost the other person.” Stop or **What if everyone agrees—but nobody speaks?**

## 3. Everyone agrees. Nobody speaks.

**Activity:** uncover viewpoints and choose how to share information. **Concept:** common knowledge.

### 3A — The silent meeting

You manage a launch. Two private messages appear one at a time: Engineering needs another week; Sales supports delaying rather than releasing a broken product. Both permit sharing their recommendations.

Meeting: you propose the delay. Nobody backs you aloud.

Fox: “Whose view will you inspect first?”

Choices: **Engineering** / **Sales**.

Engineering: “Does Sales still want Friday? I don’t want to look like the blocker.” Sales: “Does Engineering still think Friday is possible? I don’t want to block them.” Reveal the other with **See the other view**. Different order, same declared facts.

### 3B — Your intervention

Fox: “Both agreed with you. How will you share that?”

Choices: **Message each lead separately** / **Ask both to confirm in the meeting**.

- Separate: each reads the other’s recommendation. Engineering: “Does Sales know I agreed too?” Private messages helped, but this delivery did not establish mutual receipt.
- Meeting: both confirm, and both see each other confirm. No cheering or automatic date change. Director: “Now we can discuss the delay together.”

Private branch offers **Compare a shared confirmation**; shows the public result without deleting the first choice.

### 3C — The unchanged opinions

Their recommendations stay “Delay”. Lines representing who heard whom become visible.

Question: “What changed?” **Their preferred date** / **What they knew about each other**.

Explain using unchanged date cards, then introduce **Common knowledge — everyone knows, everyone knows everyone knows, and so on.**

Fox: “Public confirmation can help create that shared awareness. Real people can still miss or misunderstand a message.” Do not claim a meeting literally proves unlimited layers of knowledge. Director still decides the schedule.

Takeaway: “Sometimes agreement is already there. What’s missing is knowing that others share it.” Stop or **Undo a decision—and see what stays changed**.

## 4. We spent more. We gained nothing.

**Activity:** inspect evidence and test a reversal. **Concept:** strategic response.

### 4A — The disappointing report

You manage an online store. Next-day delivery first increased orders. Orders are now back near the original level; delivery costs remain higher. Finance: “Let’s cancel it.”

Fox: “Which evidence will you open first?”

Choices: **Our delivery costs** / **What competitors changed**.

- Costs: shows the persistent extra cost, not invented profit figures. Fox: “The cost is real. Now check what changed outside the store.”
- Competitor: dated announcement: “Next-day delivery now available.” It arrived two weeks after your launch. Fox: “They matched your offer. Check the delivery bill too.”

After both, timeline has three stages: both slow → only you fast → both fast.

### 4B — Test your decision

Fox: “Try an offer. What would this customer see?”

Customer needs the identical product by the weekend. Same total price at both stores; rival delivers tomorrow.

Choices: **Keep next-day delivery** / **Return to five-day delivery**.

- Keep: both offers meet the deadline. Customer: “Either arrives in time.” No invented guaranteed sale.
- Return: your arrival moves beyond the deadline. Customer selects rival: “I need it this weekend.”

Button: **Compare the other offer**. This illustrates one customer, not a market forecast.

### 4C — The discovery

Show original “both slow” and proposed reversal “you slow/rival fast”.

Fox: “You can reverse your decision. You can’t reverse your competitor’s.”

**Strategic response — other players can react to your move, changing the situation you face next.**

Finance still needs a decision. Choose **Estimate business fast delivery retains** / **Test fast delivery on fewer products**.

First opens retained-business and extra-cost questions. Second opens a narrower service comparison. Neither reports invented profitability. Preserve the learner’s next investigation as the result.

Takeaway: “Before going back, check whether the old situation still exists.” Stop or **You won the project. Look closer.**

## 5. You won. Did you miss something?

**Activity:** quote, investigate a price gap, revise scope. **Concept:** winner’s curse.

### 5A — Place your quote

You run a design studio. Estimated website work cost: ₹60,000, based on unconfirmed scope. Client selects the lowest suitable quote.

Fox: “What will you quote?”

Choices: **₹70,000** / **₹90,000**. Explain the trade-off evenly: lower price may help win; higher price leaves more room for unexpected work. Other bids are not yet known.

Both win against the fixed other bids, ₹1,10,000 and ₹1,20,000. Show actual chosen quote prominently. Client: “We’d like to go ahead.” Fox reacts warmly, then looks at the price gap.

### 5B — What explains the gap?

Fox: “Your quote is much lower. Which possibility will you check first?”

Choices: **We can do it more cheaply** / **We priced a smaller job**.

- Efficiency: inspect your estimate. Most work is manual; no documented shortcut explains the gap. Highlight “20 pages assumed”. Then **Check the client’s page list**.
- Scope: open client’s page list directly: 200 product pages to rebuild.

These are investigations, not character judgments. Both reach the same fact before confirmation; remove the earlier obvious “check / blindly confirm” choice.

### 5C — Decide with better information

Revised cost: ₹1,00,000. Show shortfall at the selected quote: ₹30,000 or ₹10,000. The page count signals missing work, not a linear price formula.

Fox: “What will you propose before confirming?”

Choices: **Requote for all 200 pages** / **Offer a smaller first phase**.

- Requote: full scope appears beside “New price needed”. Client: “Send the updated quote. We’ll compare it.” No guaranteed retention.
- Phase: launch subset and later pages appear as separate work. Client: “Show what is included now and what the rest would cost.” Do not invent a subset that fits the original budget without estimating it.

### 5D — Name the signal

Fox: “Your low estimate helped you win. Winning didn’t prove the estimate was right.”

**Winner’s curse — competition can select the person whose uncertain estimate is most optimistic.** Here, the lowest estimated cost won. A genuinely efficient studio could also bid lower; the gap invites checking, not a verdict.

Takeaway: “When your offer beats everyone else’s by a lot, ask what you understood differently.” Stop or **Make a plan that can survive a surprise**.

## 6. A rival arrives

**Activity:** choose a provisional response, inspect evidence, stress-test it. **Concept:** robust strategy. Final teaching story, before MCQs and separate practice.

### 6A — Your first response

You manage a railway connecting several towns. A newcomer announces two morning trains on one route. Operations: “Do we cut fares?”

Fox: “Pick a plan to investigate. You can change it.”

Choices: **Prepare a fare cut on this route** / **Keep fares while we investigate**.

Cut branch highlights affected tickets: “A discount also reaches passengers who would have stayed.” Hold branch highlights overlapping departures: “These passengers now have another option.” Neither choice is executed or graded yet.

### 6B — Find what matters

Offer two evidence choices at a time: **Who is riding?** / **Why would they switch?** Then the other pair: **Our connections** / **Their costs**. Each choice replaces the screen and changes evidence order.

- Riders: some previously drove; the new service is adding journeys as well as competing.
- Switching: some choose on price; discounts also reduce revenue from existing loyal passengers.
- Connections: your trains connect to towns the newcomer does not serve.
- Costs: the newcomer’s smaller operation costs less to run.

These are stipulated conditions for this scenario, not a forecast of actual railway outcomes.

### 6C — Choose a plan, then test it

Now choose **Hold fares and monitor** / **Compete on this route**. Provide **Explore other plans** for allowing a limited niche or leaving the route, with their trade-offs preserved from the master.

Keep the chosen plan pinned. Ask: “Which future will you test first?”

Choices: **No growth in total passengers** / **The rival expands its service**.

- No growth: total passenger pool stops growing; costs, connections and rival timetable stay the same. Fox: “There is less room to grow without taking each other’s passengers.”
- Expansion: rival adds more departures and lower fares; retain the original demand condition for this test. Fox: “More of your service now faces competition.”

Test the other future afterward. Keep them visibly separate; do not silently combine assumptions.

For Hold: show more business potentially exposed. For Compete: show the broader area a price response might cover and the question of its cost. For Niche: show whether the limited-entry assumption still holds. For Exit: show connections put at risk and ask whether changed conditions justify giving them up. No fake profit meters.

### 6D — Your decision, with a condition

Choose **Keep my plan** / **Revise my plan**. Keep asks for a reason; Revise reopens the response cards. Both produce:

“For now I would ___. I would reconsider if ___.”

Reason cards use observed facts: additional riders, useful connections, price-sensitive passengers, competitor cost advantage. Reconsider cards include broader competition or no demand growth. Optional own words; no single universally correct plan.

**Robust strategy — a plan chosen after weighing how it could perform across several plausible futures.** It is not a plan guaranteed to work everywhere.

Fox: “The useful answer includes what would change your mind.”

Takeaway: “Before committing, ask which assumption could break your plan.” Stop or **Check what you learned**.

## Editorial review of all six

1. Ads: stronger learner agency, but two mechanisms remain cognitively demanding. Historical evidence is separate from the fictional saving model.
2. Manager: inspect an authored concern; no mind-reading or automatic job protection. Belief can be wrong even when the action serves a goal.
3. Meeting: private communication has value; public confirmation is not magic. Do not equate a real meeting with ideal common knowledge.
4. Delivery: now offers two useful evidence paths rather than sensible-versus-rash choices. One customer cannot determine profitability.
5. Quote: removes the obvious diligence test. Both bid levels still win under declared fixed rival bids; meaningful effects are the shortfall and revised proposal.
6. Railway: fewer choices appear together, but this remains the most demanding story. Keep source depth; allow pausing between evidence and scenario tests.

Remaining risks: too much reading in railway; numerical load in the opening; multiple workplace settings feeling repetitive despite different actions. These require a learner walkthrough, not another claim of being hooked. No human test or prototype validation has occurred.
