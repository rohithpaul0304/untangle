# Chapter 1 — MCQ check and independent practice bank

Text-level authored draft, 5 October 2026. This bank accompanies chapter-1-introduction.md and chapter-1-games.md. Twelve closing MCQs, followed by twelve fresh practice questions selected from twenty-four alternatives. Correct letters and explanations below are teacher-facing; never reveal them before a scored answer. No live AI or backend implementation is implied.

## Part A — Closing MCQ check

One question per screen after the McKinsey capstone. Feedback appears after each answer. This checks recall and interpretation, and identifies topics for the separate practice quiz. It is not the practice quiz itself.

### C01 — assumptions
Which two assumptions did this chapter introduce as a starting model?

A. Rationality and common knowledge
B. Rationality and private knowledge only
C. Random behaviour and common knowledge
D. Random behaviour and private knowledge only

Key: A. These are the course’s starting assumptions, not requirements of every game-theory model.

### C02 — interacting-choices
Which situation requires considering another person’s choice?

A. Choosing a flavour at fixed prices with plenty of stock
B. Picking a solo walking route whose time cannot change
C. Choosing a meeting place when your friend independently chooses one too
D. Choosing a colour nobody else sees or responds to

Key: C. Meeting depends on both people’s destinations; the other situations were defined as independent.

### C03 — rationality-values
Someone accepts less money because keeping a promise matters more to them. Which statement fits?

A. They must be irrational because their cash is lower
B. Their choice can fit their goals and values
C. Values cannot be included in a model
D. Only fear of punishment could explain it

Key: B. Rationality need not mean maximising money; the stated preference matters.

### C04 — common-knowledge
In the idealised example, what makes a clear public instruction different from separate private messages?

A. A public instruction forces everyone to obey
B. Private messages can contain no useful information
C. Public instructions predict every later action
D. Everyone can know the instruction and know that others know it, with awareness shared at every level

Key: D. Common knowledge involves shared awareness; it is not a guarantee of obedience.

### C05 — looking-ahead
A tournament round winner must choose a finalist. What should you consider before trying to win that round?

A. Later opponent selection and final voting, as well as this round
B. Only the number of rounds you have won
C. Assume losing any round always helps
D. Ignore what other contestants want

Key: A. A local win may change later choices in ways that help or hurt the final objective.

### C06 — strategic-response
Defenders leave another player open while covering a star. What explains why the team may benefit from a pass?

A. The defenders cannot change their approach
B. The star’s own score is the only outcome that matters
C. Defensive choices change opportunities for teammates
D. Passing always scores in real basketball

Key: C. The other side’s response can create opportunities elsewhere; the simplified game is not a universal sports rule.

### C07 — goal-dependent-risk
In our equal-boat model, why might a trailing boat choose another wind lane?

A. It knows the other lane is always faster
B. Following preserves its deficit; a different lane offers a chance to change the order
C. The leader’s choice is irrelevant
D. Risk never matters to a leader

Key: B. The trailing position and overtaking goal change the appeal of taking a risk; success is uncertain.

### C08 — commitment
What makes locking an offer different from calling it final while keeping a revise button?

A. Locked offers are always accepted
B. Both have exactly the same available choices
C. Locking improves every possible deal
D. Locking really removes a later choice

Key: D. A commitment changes what can happen later; a statement alone may not do so.

### C09 — incentives-collective
A team must spend its break cleaning a camp that everyone enjoys. What should the explanation include?

A. Its own effort cost and the shared benefit
B. Only the clean camp, because individual costs do not matter
C. A claim that waiting always pays more
D. A claim that all teams value their break equally

Key: A. Group benefits and individual costs can differ; stated preferences determine which moves look attractive.

### C10 — unpredictability
Which rock–paper–scissors approach is genuinely random in our symmetric game?

A. A public Rock–Paper–Scissors cycle
B. Always picking whatever won last time
C. Giving each move an equal random chance independently each round
D. Changing moves in a fixed rotation

Key: C. A known rotation is exploitable; equal random choice is a baseline, not a guarantee of winning.

### C11 — private-information
An opponent inspects a sealed token before selectively offering a bet. What matters before you accept?

A. Only whether their character looks friendly
B. What they know and how that affected the offer
C. A rule that every offer is dishonest
D. The belief that accepting makes the process fair

Key: B. Private information and selective offers can create an advantage; neither friendliness nor acceptance removes it.

### C12 — robust-decisions
What is the main lesson of the McKinsey railway application?

A. Always cut fares
B. Predict one future with certainty
C. Ignore rival adjustments
D. Compare actions and responses under different plausible assumptions

Key: D. The authors use scenarios and changing assumptions to support a robust decision, not guarantee one universal move.

## Part B — Separate practice quiz

Use new scenarios with no hints or answers shown before submission. Choose one of two variants for each of the twelve objectives. Default quiz: twelve questions. The remaining variants supply fresh retries and later review; learners can choose extended practice. Present a summary and explanations after the submitted set so later items are not contaminated by immediate hints. Save the original result separately from corrected and hinted attempts.

### P01 — assumptions
A course model starts by assuming consistent goal-directed choice and facts whose shared awareness is known at every level. Which pair is it using?

A. Rationality and common knowledge
B. Perfect forecasting and selfishness
C. Random choice and secrecy
D. Emotionlessness and obedience

Key: A. The two stated assumptions match rationality and common knowledge.

### P02 — assumptions
A different model allows players to have private information. Which conclusion follows?

A. It cannot be game theory
B. All players must be irrational
C. Game theory can use assumptions different from this chapter’s starting model
D. It can have no interacting choices

Key: C. Private-information models are still game theory; this chapter’s assumptions are not universal requirements.

### P03 — interacting-choices
Two delivery robots choose a narrow corridor at the same time; if both enter, both must stop. Why is this strategic?

A. Each robot has only its own goal
B. Each result depends on both choices
C. Corridors always make every choice strategic
D. The robots need human emotions

Key: B. The waiting outcome depends on the combination of entry choices.

### P04 — interacting-choices
You choose music alone. Nobody can hear it, respond to it, or affect playback. Which description best fits this simplified situation?

A. An adversarial game because you make a choice
B. A coordination game because music has a rhythm
C. A game because every preference involves another player
D. An individual decision with no modelled strategic interaction

Key: D. The prompt explicitly removes another actor’s effect on the outcome.

### P05 — rationality-values
A shop owner values honouring an agreed price more than earning an extra fee. Accepting the agreed price can be what?

A. Consistent with the owner’s stated goals
B. Proof that everyone has the same values
C. Evidence that fairness cannot be modelled
D. Impossible under rational choice

Key: A. The owner’s objective includes keeping the agreement, not only cash.

### P06 — rationality-values
A player chooses a less comfortable seat to sit with a friend, because friendship matters more to them. What should a model consider?

A. Only comfort
B. Only the seat price
C. The player’s preference for being with the friend
D. A rule that all emotional preferences are irrational

Key: C. The stated preference can explain the choice; emotional significance is not automatically inconsistent.

### P07 — common-knowledge
Two teammates separately know a new start time, but neither knows whether the other received it. What is established?

A. Common knowledge at all levels
B. Both know the time, but shared awareness is not established
C. Neither can know anything
D. Both are guaranteed to arrive together

Key: B. Mutual knowledge alone does not establish common knowledge.

### P08 — common-knowledge
One green-eyed prisoner is alone and hears the truthful public statement that at least one prisoner has green eyes. Under the stated rules, what can they infer?

A. Their eyes might still be another colour
B. The visitor must be wrong
C. They must wait for another prisoner
D. Their own eyes are green

Key: D. With only one prisoner, the statement identifies that prisoner. This is the base case, not the harder two-person puzzle.

### P09 — looking-ahead
Winning a qualifying event commits you to play the strongest opponent next. What should you inspect?

A. How qualification affects your overall chance, not just the immediate win
B. Only how impressive the qualifying trophy looks
C. Assume losing is always best
D. Ignore the later opponent

Key: A. The overall goal requires considering the next stage. No universally best action follows without more rules.

### P10 — looking-ahead
Taking a reward now means losing a more valuable agreed reward tomorrow. You value both and know the rule. Which consideration is relevant?

A. Today is always more important
B. Tomorrow can never affect a present choice
C. Both immediate and later consequences
D. Only what another player did last week

Key: C. Looking ahead means accounting for the stated later consequence; preference and timing still matter.

### P11 — strategic-response
A rival copies your successful service, making it less distinctive. What assumption should you reconsider?

A. That services can have prices
B. That the rival would remain unchanged after your move
C. That customers have goals
D. That success is possible

Key: B. A strategic rival can respond; the original advantage need not persist.

### P12 — strategic-response
A defender moves to cover your usual passing lane. What should you do before using the old plan?

A. Assume the lane is still open
B. Treat any failed pass as bad luck
C. Ignore the defender’s action
D. Check the new arrangement and available alternatives

Key: D. Changed defensive behaviour changes the opportunities relevant to the decision.

### P13 — goal-dependent-risk
Two equal boats follow the same wind lane and preserve their order. If your goal is overtaking from behind, what does copying offer?

A. No overtaking in this stated model
B. A guaranteed overtake
C. A different wind advantage automatically
D. Proof copying never helps in any race

Key: A. The fixed equal-boat rule preserves the deficit; real races can have other factors.

### P14 — goal-dependent-risk
One runner wants any finish; another needs a chance to beat the leader. They face a safe route and a risky shortcut. What can differ?

A. Only their names
B. Nothing, because the map is the same
C. Which risk they find worthwhile given their different goals
D. Whether their choices have consequences

Key: C. Different objectives can change the ranking of risky options. The shortcut is not guaranteed to help.

### P15 — commitment
A buyer announces a budget limit but can immediately raise it. Compared with a binding rule, what is missing?

A. Any possible value in communicating
B. An actual restriction on future choice
C. Any goal for the buyer
D. Any uncertainty

Key: B. A statement alone does not necessarily impose the restriction others are being asked to believe.

### P16 — commitment
A character wants to focus tomorrow and puts a distraction out of reach until work ends, while keeping an emergency exception. What mechanism is being used?

A. Perfect knowledge of future feelings
B. A guarantee that no new need will occur
C. A change to everybody else’s goals
D. A present arrangement that changes future options

Key: D. This is self-commitment; the exception also shows that preserving some flexibility can matter.

### P17 — incentives-collective
One volunteer’s effort benefits everyone, while the volunteer bears the cost. What can create hesitation?

A. The difference between personal cost and shared benefit
B. The fact that helping can have no consequence
C. The certainty that all members will help
D. The rule that groups have no individual decision-makers

Key: A. An individual may prefer that someone else pays the effort cost; preferences and others’ actions matter.

### P18 — incentives-collective
A club makes a previously unpaid task rewarded. Which statement is justified?

A. Every member will certainly volunteer
B. Everyone values the reward identically
C. The reward changes the costs and benefits members consider
D. The club’s goals cannot affect choices

Key: C. A changed incentive can alter choices but does not guarantee an identical response from everyone.

### P19 — unpredictability
Your opponent sees a publicly fixed Left–Right–Left–Right pattern. Why might changing sides every turn fail to help?

A. Alternation has no possible purpose
B. They can still predict the next side
C. Randomness guarantees losing
D. Only repeating one move is predictable

Key: B. A known alternating sequence is still a pattern.

### P20 — unpredictability
You choose rock, paper or scissors equally at random. You lose the next round. What follows?

A. The method must have failed to randomise
B. The next round must be a win
C. Your opponent must have read the hidden move
D. A random strategy can still lose an individual round

Key: D. Randomisation reduces exploitability in the symmetric model, not the possibility of individual losses.

### P21 — private-information
A seller has inspected an object; you have not. Before accepting a special offer, what is useful to consider?

A. Whether inspection helped the seller choose that offer
B. A guarantee that the object is bad
C. Only the offer’s colour
D. A rule that private information never matters

Key: A. The seller’s extra information may affect selection; it does not alone prove a bad object or dishonesty.

### P22 — private-information
Both parties agree on bet rules before an unknown random outcome is selected, and neither sees it early. What advantage has been removed?

A. All possible uncertainty
B. Every possibility of a loss
C. One party’s ability to selectively offer a side after seeing the outcome
D. The need to define any rules

Key: C. The procedure removes this particular information advantage, not risk itself.

### P23 — robust-decisions
A new train service attracts travellers who previously drove. Which conclusion is supported?

A. Every new rider must have left the existing railway
B. Total rail demand may grow, but profits still depend on other factors
C. Both companies are guaranteed to earn more
D. Costs no longer matter

Key: B. Market growth differs from redistribution; costs, networks and price responses still matter.

### P24 — robust-decisions
A plan works only if one exact demand forecast is correct. What additional analysis fits the McKinsey approach?

A. Treat that forecast as certain
B. Ignore the rival’s next move
C. Hide the assumptions from the decision-maker
D. Compare plausible demand and rival-response scenarios, including downside risks

Key: D. The authors seek decisions informed by a range of conditions, with assumptions and break points visible.

## AI routing and evidence rules

Use the closing check to choose explanation depth and the order of practice objectives. Select only an eligible reviewed question not already revealed. Correctness is checked against this published key; AI does not invent a new key. Treat one wrong selection as a tentative clue, not a diagnosis. Ask a discriminating follow-up when the mistake could have multiple causes.

For C01: B tentatively targets common knowledge; C targets rationality; D may concern both. Check those interpretations with a fresh meaning question rather than only more terminology. For other records, the objective tag identifies the first follow-up family; a chosen distractor can inform feedback but cannot establish a misconception without further evidence.

Keep game interests separate from learning needs. Use sports, mystery, cooperation or business examples where reviewed equivalents exist; do not claim this draft already has a complete question in every interest for every objective. Do not interpret a lucky in-game win, disclosed answer or hinted retry as independent understanding.

After a practice error, show the relevant explanation or short replay, then use an unrevealed alternative in a new attempt. If both variants were already shown, use a later reviewed alternative or record that independent confirmation is still needed. Never relabel a revealed-item retry as fresh evidence. No arbitrary three-question cap; pause/resume and change the teaching approach when repetition stops helping.

Report the submitted score and concept-specific evidence. Do not overwrite the first score with later corrected responses. Do not claim mastery because a learner reached the end. Existing chapter-access rules remain until a product decision changes them; no new percentage threshold is invented here.
