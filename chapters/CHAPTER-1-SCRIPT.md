# Chapter 1 — You’re not choosing alone

**Consolidated script · draft 2 · 5 October 2026**

Six core stories, a closing understanding check and separate fresh practice. This is the active content draft. The previous anthology is archived as `chapter-1-script-anthology-archived-2026-10-05.md`; its sequence and tests are not build instructions. Optional stories are retained separately.

**Status:** approved conversational directions consolidated, with new connecting dialogue and complete branches proposed for review. Not learner-tested. No prototype development or publication is authorised by this document.

## The experience

The learner moves from “What do I choose?” to “What do other people want, know and do—and how does that change my decision?”

| Stop | Story | What the learner does | Concept |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The ads go dark | Predict and compare | Strategic interdependence |
| 2 | Your idea saves time. Your boss says no. | Investigate motives | Rationality and incentives |
| 3 | Everyone agrees. Nobody speaks. | Reveal viewpoints and share information | Common knowledge |
| 4 | We spent more. We gained nothing. | Reconstruct a timeline | Strategic response |
| 5 | You won the project. Why are you worried? | Quote and inspect | Winner’s curse |
| 6 | A rival arrives | Plan, test assumptions and revise | Robust strategy |

The main activity must genuinely change between stories. Do not implement this as six identical multiple-choice lessons with different illustrations.

### Reading and presentation rules

Each numbered beat contains the required scene, dialogue, learner action and response. An action screen and its consequence are separate screens. Do not interpret seven beats as necessarily seven screens or make every sentence require Continue.

- Learner-facing dialogue and screens contain no citations, source footers or reference links.
- **Author notes** below specify rules and branches; they are not fox dialogue.
- Keep one clear purpose per screen. Maintain only the visual context needed for the next decision. Responses replace the screen rather than accumulating below it.
- Use the approved fox as an expressive companion, not an opponent. Its movement explains or reacts to events; no perpetual bouncing.
- Strategic choices show trade-offs. Explicit understanding questions receive explanations. Opening predictions are not mastery checks.
- Every choice leads to teaching; mistakes do not trap learners before the explanation. Optional short explanations supplement tap choices rather than forcing typing.
- Record predictions, committed actions and viewed explanations separately. Return to the same scene and committed outcome after a pause. No rerolls, lost progress, duplicate rewards or compulsory marathon.
- Keep concept names available after their introduction. Show a short practical takeaway and a genuine stopping point at each story's end.

## 1 — The ads go dark

**Interaction:** predict, change a condition, compare outcomes.

**Author rules:** two fictional companies divide 200 practice coins of customer spending. This total is held constant in the first comparison. Advertising costs 40 per company that advertises. Both on: 100 received minus 40 spent = 60 left each. Both off: 100 left each. Only one on: advertiser receives 160 minus 40 = 120; the other receives 40. These figures isolate a mechanism, exclude other costs and are not historical company accounts. The rival's advertising choice is fixed during each comparison, not secretly changed after the learner acts.

### 1.1 — Predict before explaining

**Scene:** a television goes dark. Headline: “United States · 1971 · Cigarette ads banned on TV and radio.”

**Fox:** “Would you expect this to hurt the established cigarette companies financially?”

**Choose:** “Yes, hurt them” / “No, it could help” / “I’m not sure.”

**Response for all:** “Hold that thought. Let’s look at what changes for competitors.” Save the answer without grading it.

**Transition:** rewind to “Before the restriction · made-up example.” No claim that the model will prove the historical prediction right or wrong.

### 1.2 — Make your own move

**Scene:** your company and a competitor both advertise. Each shows “Money left after ads: 60.” The competitor's switch is visibly fixed on.

**Fox:** “Ads cost 40. For this experiment, customers spend the same total amount. Ads change which company gets their business.”

**Prompt:** “Your competitor keeps advertising. What will you do?”

- “Keep my ads.”
- “Stop my ads.”

**Keep:** both remain at 60. “You both keep advertising—and paying for it.”

**Stop:** your ads go dark; money shifts toward the competitor. Your total becomes 40; theirs becomes 120. “You saved the ad cost, but your competitor gained business. You kept less.”

Show a compact, labelled comparison of the two possible choices: “Competitor on: you keep 60 with ads, 40 without.” Preserve which was actually chosen. No compulsory replay. Expandable receipts show the arithmetic.

**Next:** “What if neither could advertise?”

### 1.3 — Change the competitor’s options

**Scene:** the restriction applies to both. Both televisions go dark and both advertising bills disappear. Each now keeps 100.

Show: “Both advertised: 60 each → neither advertises: 100 each.”

**Fox:** “Here, both keep more. Stopping alone and stopping together are different situations.”

**Prediction response:**
- Expected harm: “This shows a possibility your first prediction may have left out.”
- Expected benefit: “This gives one possible reason for your prediction.”
- Unsure: “Now we have one possible explanation to investigate.”

**Next:** “What made the difference?”

### 1.4 — Show the cause before naming it

**Scene:** hold your action constant. Your ads are OFF in both pictures.

| Your ads | Competitor’s ads | Your money left |
|---|---|---:|
| Off | On | 40 |
| Off | Off | 100 |

**Fox:** “Your ads stayed off. Why did your result change?”

**Choose:** “My competitor stopped too” / “I saved my ad bill only the second time.”

**First response:** “Exactly. You saved your own ad bill both times. The difference was whether your competitor could still advertise.”

**Second response:** highlight zero advertising cost on both receipts. “Your ad bill was zero in both cases. The competitor's switch changed. It no longer had the advantage of advertising alone.”

**Then, for everyone:** “You made the same choice, but got a different result because of their choice.”

**Concept card:** **Strategic interdependence.** “Your result depends on what you choose AND what others choose.”

**Fox:** “That’s why game theory asks what the other person might do.”

### 1.5 — Challenge the model

**Fox:** “We held customer spending steady. What if people bought much less when both companies stopped advertising?”

**Choose:** “The saved bill still guarantees a gain” / “Compare the lost income with the saved cost.”

**First response:** “A smaller bill is not enough if income falls by more. That assumption mattered.”

**Second response:** “Yes. Both changes matter. The bill alone cannot tell us the result.”

Do not invent a numeric second outcome. The point is identifying information needed, not guessing an unsupported number.

### 1.6 — Resolve the real-world mystery carefully

**Scene:** the fictional model closes; the historical news scene returns.

**Fox:** “A study of this ban found stock-market evidence consistent with benefits to established tobacco companies.”

**Next line:** “That concerns investors’ expectations—not our fictional receipt. The real explanation also involved barriers facing new brands.”

**Close:** “A restriction can remove an option and still change competition in ways that favour established firms.”

Author boundary: do not show fabricated stock-price jumps or claim this mechanism alone explains the study. Company financial effects do not settle public-health effects. Keep detailed provenance in internal notes, not a learner-facing citation panel.

### 1.7 — Apply and stop

**Scene:** your café's delivery costs have risen. A nearby café also delivers.

**Fox:** “Before cancelling delivery, what would you investigate?”

Open two evidence cards, in either order. Each replaces the scene:
- **Delivery bill:** “Cancelling removes this cost.”
- **Customer choices:** “Some customers may switch to the other café if it still delivers.”

**Check:** “Is the delivery bill enough to decide?”

**Choices:** “Yes, the bill disappears” / “No, compare the saving with business we might lose.”

Explain: “The saving matters. So does what customers can get elsewhere.” No invented optimal decision.

**Takeaway:** “Before judging your move, ask what everyone else can do.”

**Transition:** “To understand their choices, we first need to understand what they want.”

**Stop:** “Save this idea” or “Continue.” Defer “Why didn’t both stop voluntarily?” to the prisoner’s-dilemma chapter.

## 2 — Your idea saves time. Your boss says no.

**Interaction:** investigate a surprising refusal. The learner chooses what evidence to seek, rather than guessing a secret motive from two personality labels.

**Author rules:** fictional workplace. A small automation trial worked and saves five hours of weekly reporting. The manager believes that a lower reported workload could cause leadership to remove a team position. This is their stated concern, not an established fact about what leadership would do. Other workplaces can have different motives.

### 2.1 — The refusal

**Scene:** you are a team member who tested the automation. The trial summary says “Five hours saved each week.”

**You:** “Can we use this for the weekly report?”
**Manager:** “Keep doing it manually for now.”

**Fox:** “You’ve shown a real saving. What will you investigate before proposing the next step?”

**Choose a first question:**
- “Which part of the result concerns you?”
- “What would change for the team if we used it?”

Both are useful. Neither is the foolish foil to a correct answer.

### 2.2 — Hear the concern

**If result question:**
**Manager:** “The time saving looks real. My concern is how leadership will use it.”
**Follow-up action:** “Ask what leadership might do.”

**If team question:** go directly to the following statement.

**Manager:** “Reported workload helps justify our staffing. I’m worried fewer reported hours will be used to remove a position. We’re already stretched.”

**Scene:** your perspective shows freed time; the manager's shows a possible staffing cut, labelled “Concern—not confirmed.”

**Fox:** “You saw work disappearing. Your manager saw a reason the team might get smaller.”

### 2.3 — Act on the new information

**Prompt:** “What will you propose next?”

- “Ask leadership how saved time would affect staffing.”
- “Map the backlog the saved time could help clear.”

**First branch:** leadership says no staffing decision has been made and requests a plan for using the saved time. Fox: “You checked the belief instead of treating it as fact. A decision is still needed.”

**Second branch:** the team identifies delayed work the freed hours could address. Manager: “That would help explain why we still need the team. We should confirm how leadership sees it.” Fox: “You made an alternative use visible. You haven’t secured a staffing promise.”

Both converge on a revised proposal: automation plus an explicit plan for freed capacity and a request to clarify staffing implications. Do not invent automatic approval or guaranteed job protection.

### 2.4 — Explain the different goals

**Fox:** “Your goal was to save time. Your manager was also trying to protect capacity. A proposal can benefit the company while threatening something another person values.”

**Reflection:** “What did you need to understand before improving the proposal?”

Allow a short answer or choices: “Their priorities and expectations” / “Only the number of hours saved.” Explain the first regardless of response.

**Concept card:** **Rationality.** “Choosing what best serves your goals, given what you believe.”

**Fox:** “It does not mean emotionless, perfectly informed, or always choosing the most money. A choice can fit a goal while resting on a belief we should check.”

**Small companion label:** **Incentive:** a benefit or cost that makes a choice more attractive. The feared staffing consequence is part of what makes the manager hesitate.

### 2.5 — A short contrast, not another full lesson

**Scene:** Mira promised a friend a performance for ₹500. Another event offers ₹800 at the same time.

Mira chooses the promise: “Keeping my word matters more to me than the extra ₹300.”

The other event moves to a free day. She accepts it too.

**Fox:** “She did not stop caring about money. Once the conflict disappeared, she could pursue both goals.”

**Check:** “Does accepting less money by itself prove a choice is irrational?”

**Choices:** “Yes” / “No, we need the person's goals and beliefs.”

Explain: “Compare the choice with what that person values—not only with your preferred outcome.” This is a brief transfer illustration, not evidence that all stated motives perfectly explain behaviour.

### 2.6 — Work tip and transition

**Takeaway:** “Before pitching a change, investigate who gains, who bears the risk, and what each person is rewarded for.”

**Fox:** “Don’t assume you’ve uncovered a secret motive. Ask and check the evidence.”

**Transition:** “But what if everyone agrees with your proposal—and nobody backs it in the room?”

**Stop:** save the idea; optional meeting-incentives extension remains outside the core route.

## 3 — Everyone agrees. Nobody speaks.

**Interaction:** reveal different viewpoints and compare private information with shared confirmation.

**Author rules:** fictional product launch. Engineering and Sales both privately support moving Friday's launch by one week. Both permit the project manager to share their recommendations. Each worries the other may oppose a delay. These motives are authored dialogue, not an inference that silence always means agreement. The director decides the schedule; visible agreement is not automatic authority to change it.

### 3.1 — The silence

**Scene:** you manage the launch. Before the meeting you received two messages.

**Engineering:** “We need another week.”
**Sales:** “Agreed. A broken launch would hurt more than a delay.”

In the meeting:
**You:** “I recommend moving the launch.”
*Silence.*

**Fox:** “Both agreed privately. Before interpreting the silence, inspect what each person knows.”

**Action:** open Engineering's view or Sales' view first; reveal the other afterward. No right first viewpoint.

### 3.2 — Two incomplete pictures

**Engineering:** “I know we need more time. But Sales might insist on Friday. I don’t want this framed as Engineering failing.”

**Sales:** “I’d accept a delay. But Engineering might say they can deliver. I don’t want to look like I’m blocking the launch.”

**Fox:** “Each told you their position. Neither heard the other say it.”

### 3.3 — Share the information

**Fox:** “Both gave you permission to share their views. How will you bring them into the discussion?”

- “Send each lead a private summary of the other's view.”
- “Show both recommendations and ask each lead to confirm.”

**Private branch:** both read the separate summaries. Engineering asks, “Has Sales also been told that I support it?” Fox: “They now know each other's position. They still lack confirmation of who else received which message.”

**Public branch:** you show both recommendations. Engineering says yes; Sales says yes. Both see and hear the confirmations. Fox: “Nobody changed their recommendation. Their awareness of each other's position changed.”

After the private branch, offer “Compare a shared confirmation,” then show the public branch. Do not erase the private choice or describe private communication as inherently useless.

### 3.4 — Hold the opinions constant

**Scene:** both recommendations remain “Delay.” Only who can see the acknowledgements changes.

**Fox:** “Before: ‘I agree, but I don’t know whether you do.’ Now: ‘We both agree, and we both heard each other confirm it.’”

**Question:** “What changed?”

**Choices:** “Their preferred launch date” / “What they knew about each other's position.”

Explain after both: “The recommendations were already the same. The shared confirmation removed uncertainty about those recommendations.”

### 3.5 — Name the stronger idea carefully

**Concept card:** **Common knowledge.** “Everyone knows, everyone knows that everyone knows, and that shared awareness continues.”

**Fox:** “A clear public confirmation helps create shared awareness. In an ideal version, everyone hears it, everyone knows everyone heard it, and so on.”

**Boundary:** “Real meetings can involve missed messages and misunderstandings. Seeing a read receipt is not automatically common knowledge.”

**Fox:** “And shared knowledge does not force agreement, honesty or action. Here, it removed one barrier to discussing the delay.”

Director response: “Now we can assess the delay together.” Do not show a guaranteed successful launch or automatic acceptance.

### 3.6 — Apply the distinction

**Scene:** two teammates privately receive the same updated plan. Neither knows whether the other received it.

**Check:** “What remains uncertain?”

**Choices:** “Whether the other person knows the update” / “What my own message says.”

Explain: “Knowing the plan and knowing what someone else knows are different.” Optional deeper badge puzzle is offered only after this explanation and does not gate completion.

**Takeaway:** “When people agree privately but hesitate publicly, investigate whether their agreement is visible to one another.”

**Transition:** “Knowing what others think is one part. Watching how they respond is another.”

## 4 — We spent more. We gained nothing.

**Interaction:** investigate a timeline and a changed competitive situation. Preserve the founder-approved central line: reversing your decision does not reverse your competitor's.

**Author rules:** fictional online store. Next-day delivery initially attracts more orders. Competitor matches it; orders return near baseline and fulfilment costs stay higher. These observations alone do not prove causation or determine profitability. A single illustrative customer decision is not a demand forecast.

### 4.1 — The uncomfortable report

**Scene:** you manage the store. Finance puts two facts on your desk.

“Orders: back where they started.”
“Delivery costs: higher.”

**Finance:** “Let’s cancel next-day delivery.”

**Fox:** “You’re responsible for the decision. What do you do first?”

- “Prepare to return to five-day delivery.”
- “Investigate why the extra orders disappeared.”

### 4.2 — Discover the missing event

**Cancellation branch:** before the prepared change goes live, Sales asks you to inspect a competitor's announcement. The learner has not irreversibly cancelled delivery.

**Investigation branch:** the team checks events around the date orders stopped growing.

Both reveal the same announcement: “Next-day delivery. Now available.”

**Sales:** “They introduced it two weeks after us.”

**Action:** place that announcement on the timeline. The dates are visible, so this is evidence organisation, not a date-memory quiz.

**Fox:** “Their move belongs in the picture. It doesn’t tell us the whole financial answer yet.”

### 4.3 — Investigate the proposed reversal

**Fox:** “What would a customer see if you cancelled now?”

**Action:** switch your offer in the comparison from “tomorrow” to “five days.” Competitor remains “tomorrow.” Product and total customer price are explicitly identical in this comparison.

**Customer:** “I need it for the weekend. I’ll order from them.”

**Fox:** “Before your improvement, both stores were slow. If you cancel now, only yours is.”

The customer is an illustrative response with a stated need, not every customer's preference. Do not infer exact lost sales.

### 4.4 — Let the sequence explain the idea

**Scene:** four frames:
1. Both slow.
2. You fast, rival slow.
3. Both fast.
4. You slow, rival fast if you reverse.

**Fox:** “You can reverse your decision. You can’t reverse your competitor’s.”

**Concept card:** **Strategic response.** “Other players can react to your move. Their reaction changes the situation you choose in next.”

**Check:** “What comparison would help decide whether to cancel?”

**Choices:** “Only our sales before and after launch” / “Keeping versus removing fast delivery with the rival now offering it.”

Explain: “The second compares your actual options now. Before-and-after figures alone mix your change with other events.”

### 4.5 — Preserve the real decision

**Finance:** “We still need to reduce the delivery bill.”

**Fox:** “What will you investigate?”

- “Whether the retained business covers the extra cost.”
- “Whether fast delivery on fewer products would cost less.”

**First:** open a worksheet with two missing quantities: added delivery cost and business retained because of faster delivery. Fox: “We need evidence for both, not an invented profit total.”

**Second:** mark eligible products on a comparison plan. Fox: “A narrower offer might reduce cost. We would test what customers give up as well.”

Neither is an automatic correct business strategy. Both are defensible next investigations.

**Takeaway:** “Before undoing a change, check whether the world you’re returning to still exists.”

**Transition:** “Next, a result that looks excellent: you win a project. What might the win itself be telling you?”

## 5 — You won the project. Why are you worried?

**Interaction:** choose a quote, then inspect the scope before confirming. Avoid treating checking as a moral virtue; show the timing and information consequences.

**Author rules:** fictional studio. Brief leads you to estimate a total cost of ₹60,000, explicitly uncertain. Other suitable studios quote ₹1,10,000 and ₹1,20,000. Client chooses the lowest suitable quote. Your options are ₹70,000 and ₹90,000; both win under these fixed rival quotes. The actual scope includes 200 product pages rather than the 20 you assumed. The revised total work cost is ₹1,00,000. Page counts explain omitted work, not a linear per-page pricing formula. All facts are authored before the choice; no rival quote changes to manufacture a win.

### 5.1 — Quote with uncertainty

**Scene:** you run a small design studio. A client requests a website. Your estimated work cost is ₹60,000, subject to confirming scope.

**Fox:** “Two other studios are bidding. What will you quote?”

- “₹70,000 — less room for unexpected work.”
- “₹90,000 — more room, but a higher price.”

No rival figures revealed before the bid. The learner cannot yet know both prices win.

### 5.2 — Win the assignment

Reveal competing quotes: ₹1,10,000 and ₹1,20,000.

**Client:** “We’d like to proceed with your quote.”

**Fox:** “Yours is much lower. What will you do before confirming?”

- “Confirm using my original estimate.”
- “Check whether I missed any work.”

### 5.3 — Reveal the same fact at different times

**Check branch:** ask what “move the existing website” includes. Client: “All 200 product pages need rebuilding. We assumed that was included.”

**Confirm branch:** the identical fact appears at kickoff, after the learner confirms the price. No arbitrary different scope to punish a choice.

**Scene:** estimate shows “20 pages assumed”; scope shows “200 pages included.” Revised full cost: ₹1,00,000.

**Fox:** “You had priced a smaller job.”

### 5.4 — Preserve the amount and timing

| Your actual quote | Revised cost | Shortfall if delivered at that price |
|---|---:|---:|
| ₹70,000 | ₹1,00,000 | ₹30,000 |
| ₹90,000 | ₹1,00,000 | ₹10,000 |

Show only the learner's primary row first; comparison remains optional.

**Checked first:** “You can revise the scope or price before confirming. The client may decline.”

**Confirmed first:** “You need to discuss changing an agreed expectation. The client may refuse.” Do not assert a legal obligation or invent a guaranteed recovery.

**Fox:** “Winning didn’t make your estimate accurate. Your unusually low estimate helped you win.”

### 5.5 — Explain selection, not just missing pages

**Reflection:** “Why might the winning estimate deserve an extra check?”

Allow a short explanation, or “Winning can select the most optimistic estimate” / “Winning verifies the estimate.”

**Concept card:** **Winner’s curse.** “When people compete using uncertain estimates, the winner can be the person whose estimate was most optimistic.”

**Fox:** “Here, that meant underestimating cost. When buying something, it can mean overestimating its value.”

**Follow-up:** “Would every low quote be a mistake?”

**Choices:** “Yes” / “No, a studio could genuinely have lower costs.”

Explain: “Different methods, costs and scope can justify a lower quote. The gap is a reason to investigate, not proof of error.”

### 5.6 — Use the signal

**Takeaway:** “When your offer is far better than everyone else’s, ask what you understood differently—and what you might have missed.”

**Transition:** “You’ve learned to question one estimate. Now use the same discipline on an entire business plan.”

## 6 — A rival arrives

**Interaction:** assemble a provisional plan, reveal drivers, change assumptions, examine responses and revise. This is the final teaching case, before quizzes.

**Author rules:** qualitative railway case. Never invent passenger, profit or break-even numbers. Distinguish a possible scenario from an observed outcome. Rival service types: similar service, more aggressive price/frequency competition, a small niche, or a differentiated service. Current scene begins with a small morning niche. Available responses: keep service, compete on challenged routes, make room for limited entry, leave a route. Outcomes depend on demand, costs, network advantages and price sensitivity; no action is universally best.

### 6.1 — Take responsibility for a route

**Scene:** you manage an established railway connecting several towns. A newcomer announces two morning trains on one route.

**Operations lead:** “Should we cut fares before they take our passengers?”

**Fox:** “Choose a first response to investigate. You can revise it as you learn more.”

- “Prepare a fare cut on this route.”
- “Keep fares while we investigate the newcomer.”

**Fare-cut response:** pin “Provisional: lower fares.” Fox: “You may defend bookings. Now check who would benefit from the discount and what it costs.”

**Hold response:** pin “Provisional: keep fares.” Fox: “You avoid an immediate cut. Now check whether waiting leaves important business exposed.”

No payoff or success verdict yet.

### 6.2 — Choose the evidence order

Open four labelled folders in any order, each replacing the scene:

**Passengers:** “Some new riders previously drove. The newcomer is attracting extra train journeys, not only taking yours.”

**Connections:** “Your network serves journeys the newcomer's short route cannot complete.”

**Operating costs:** “The newcomer runs a smaller operation at lower cost. Your larger network brings obligations as well as advantages.”

**Prices:** “Some passengers would switch for a lower fare. A fare cut also lowers what you receive from passengers who would have stayed.”

After each, mark inspected; do not ask a quiz merely to unlock the next folder. Continue after all four, with a compact summary available on demand.

### 6.3 — Form a plan for these conditions

**Scene summary:** extra rail demand; useful existing connections; a small rival service; lower rival operating costs.

**Prompt:** “Which response will you carry forward for this scenario?”

- “Keep our service and monitor the rival.”
- “Compete on the challenged route.”
- “Make room for the limited service.”
- “Leave this route.”

**Keep:** “This avoids an immediate costly reaction. Decide what evidence would tell you waiting is no longer sensible.”

**Compete:** “This may protect share, but you could sacrifice revenue to defend a relatively small threat. Compare the threatened business with the cost of fighting.”

**Make room:** “Additional demand and your connections give coexistence a plausible case here. It still depends on the rival remaining limited and the economics working.”

**Leave:** “You give up the route and possibly valuable connections. Specify what disadvantage would justify that loss.”

These are implications to examine, not calculated company outcomes. Retain the learner's plan for the next change.

### 6.4 — Change one assumption first

**Scene:** the “extra passengers” card turns over: total rail demand is no longer growing. All other facts remain unchanged.

**Fox:** “Now the two operators compete for the same pool. Which part of your plan needs another look?”

Learner selects a reason card: “Room created by new demand” / “Value of connections” / “Cost gap.”

**Demand selection:** “Yes. That is the assumption we changed.”

**Other selection:** “That factor still matters, but it has not changed in this comparison. Look at what happened to total demand.”

**Fox:** “This weakens one reason to tolerate entry. It does not, by itself, prove a fare cut or an exit is best.”

### 6.5 — Let the rival act again

Explore both rival branches in either order:

**Stays small:** two morning trains, limited route. “Your network still serves different journeys. Recheck whether both services can operate sustainably without growth.”

**Expands aggressively:** more departures and lower fares across the day. “The threat is broader. Holding your plan unchanged now needs a new justification.”

Then show a separately labelled stress case: flat demand, a large cost disadvantage for you, and little network advantage. Fox: “This changes several conditions, unlike our first comparison. A weak operator could be squeezed out. It is not the prediction for every railway.”

**Action:** keep or revise the plan using the same four response cards. Preserve both versions and the condition under which each was selected.

### 6.6 — State what would change your mind

Build a short decision note using cards or optional text:

“For now I would ___, because ___. I would reconsider if ___.”

Available reason cards: extra demand; valuable connections; exposed bookings; large operating-cost disadvantage. Trigger cards: demand stops growing; rival expands; network advantage weakens; cost gap narrows or widens. Each card shows the scenario it refers to; do not let facts from different scenarios merge silently.

**Feedback by reasoning:**
- Clear matching reason and trigger: “Your plan names the condition it relies on. You have also said what would make you review it.”
- Unsupported reason: “That fact was not established in this scenario. Choose evidence we have, or label it as something to investigate.”
- No trigger: “What change would make this plan less attractive?” Offer the relevant cards.
- Unclear short answer: ask one clarification, rather than infer mastery or invent the learner's intent.

No single selected response proves understanding. Assess the link between evidence, plan and reconsideration condition.

### 6.7 — Name the approach and close

**Concept card:** **Robust strategy.** “Compare a plan across plausible conditions—its opportunities, its risks, and when you would change it.”

**Fox:** “Game theory doesn’t remove uncertainty. It helps you reason about choices and reactions without pretending one future is guaranteed.”

**Takeaway:** “Before defending your plan, name the assumption that could break it.”

**Transition:** “Now let’s see which ideas you can use without the story guiding you.”

## 7 — Understanding check

**Fox:** “Twelve short questions. After each answer, we’ll explain the reasoning. You can pause anytime.”

One question per screen, then a replacement explanation screen. Options are learner text; keys and explanations below are author-only until submission. No optional-topic prerequisites. Two items per core story.

### C01 — Interacting choices
In the advertising comparison, your ads stayed off. What explains the change from 40 to 100 left?
A. Your own advertising cost fell again.
B. Your competitor stopped advertising too.
C. Your company received more money because it spent more on ads.

**Key B.** “Your ad cost was already zero. The competitor's advertising advantage changed.”

### C02 — Model versus world
Why couldn't the model establish that the historical ban must benefit firms?
A. It held total customer spending constant and left out other effects.
B. It compared both companies rather than focusing only on one.
C. It showed the outcome after the restriction instead of before it.

**Key A.** “A result follows the model's assumptions. Historical effects require evidence about the real setting.”

### C03 — Goals and beliefs
The manager accepts the time-saving result but fears a staffing cut. What belongs in your next investigation?
A. Whether the tool could save even more time on other reports.
B. Whether another team would adopt the tool first.
C. Whether staffing would actually be reduced and what freed time could do.

**Key C.** “Investigate both the believed consequence and the goal it threatens.”

### C04 — Rationality
Mira knowingly chooses less pay to keep a promise she values more. What does this show?
A. The choice can fit her priorities.
B. She must have misunderstood the amounts.
C. Emotional priorities are excluded from rational choice.

**Key A.** “Assess the choice against her goals and beliefs, not money alone.”

### C05 — Shared awareness
Both leads privately support a delay. What can public confirmation add?
A. A new preferred date for each person.
B. Awareness that the other supports it and heard their support.
C. A guarantee that the director agrees.

**Key B.** “The recommendations may stay fixed while knowledge about others' positions changes.”

### C06 — Limits of common knowledge
Which claim goes beyond what the meeting established?
A. Both leads heard the confirmations.
B. One source of uncertainty was reduced.
C. Shared awareness guarantees the director will delay launch.

**Key C.** “Knowledge does not remove other goals, constraints or decision rights.”

### C07 — Changed competition
Why doesn't cancelling next-day delivery restore the original situation?
A. The rival now offers next-day delivery.
B. The store can never change its service.
C. Every customer must prefer speed over price.

**Key A.** “Your reversal leaves the competitor's changed offer in place.”

### C08 — Relevant comparison
Which comparison helps assess whether to keep faster delivery now?
A. Today's sales against a past period with no other changes considered.
B. The cost of launch, without future benefits or costs.
C. Expected costs and retained business with versus without it under current competition.

**Key C.** “Compare current alternatives. Before-and-after totals alone do not isolate the effect.”

### C09 — Winning and estimation
Why inspect a quote much lower than every rival's?
A. Every winning quote is necessarily unprofitable.
B. Selection can favour a quote that underestimated the work.
C. Losing studios must know the exact true cost.

**Key B.** “An optimistic error can help you win. It is a reason to check, not proof rivals are right.”

### C10 — A different explanation
What would weaken the conclusion that a low quote reflects an error?
A. Verified lower costs for the same full scope.
B. The client choosing that quote.
C. The quote being submitted confidently.

**Key A.** “Genuine efficiency can support a lower price. Winning and confidence do not establish it.”

### C11 — Changed assumption
Total rail demand stops growing; all other facts stay fixed. What follows?
A. Every operator should leave immediately.
B. A fare cut is automatically profitable.
C. The plan's reliance on extra demand needs review.

**Key C.** “One changed condition triggers reconsideration; it does not supply a universal response.”

### C12 — Robustness
Which decision note best supports later review?
A. “Our first plan won the discussion, so keep it.”
B. “Keep the plan while the rival stays limited; reconsider if it expands across the day.”
C. “We need the one plan that cannot fail in any possible future.”

**Key B.** “A reason and a relevant change condition make a plan inspectable. They still need evidence.”

**After the set:** show “Ideas you explained” and “Ideas worth another look.” Never call the learner a bad strategist. Offer targeted explanation before fresh practice, with that help recorded.

## 8 — Fresh practice

**Fox:** “New situations. I’ll save each answer and explain them after the set. You can pause, or ask for help.”

Twelve items, two per core objective. Explanations stay hidden until submission of the set unless help is requested. If help is requested, identify that item as helped; do not count it as independent evidence or silently reveal later answers. No points or speed threshold substitutes for understanding.

### P01 — Two venues
Two nearby event venues offer free parking. One considers charging while the other keeps it free. What should it examine alongside the parking revenue?
A. Only the price nearby car parks charge.
B. Whether organisers or guests would shift to the other venue.
C. Whether charging always signals higher quality.

**Key B.** “The competitor's offer can change the effect of your own charge.”

### P02 — A cost-saving comparison
A model says two services save money when both stop a costly perk. It assumes customer spending stays unchanged. What evidence could overturn its gain prediction?
A. Lost customer spending exceeds the saved cost.
B. Customers divide their unchanged spending equally between them.
C. Both services pay zero for the discontinued perk.

**Key A.** “The result depends on the assumption about money coming in, not only spending going out.”

### P03 — A supplier decision
A buyer knowingly selects a more expensive supplier because it has a more dependable delivery record. Is higher price alone evidence of irrationality?
A. Yes, because price is the only relevant goal.
B. Yes, if another quote was available.
C. No, reliability may be worth the extra price to that buyer.

**Key C.** “Compare the decision with the buyer's priorities and beliefs about reliability.”

### P04 — A resisted handover
A colleague resists a handover process because they believe they will still be blamed for mistakes but lose authority to prevent them. What should you check?
A. How responsibility and authority would actually change.
B. Whether calling the process efficient makes the concern irrelevant.
C. Whether all resistance proves unwillingness to help.

**Key A.** “Investigate the believed consequence and the incentive, rather than assign a motive without evidence.”

### P05 — Separate updates
Two departments each receive the same revised schedule privately. Neither knows whether the other received it. What can you conclude?
A. Each must know that both received it.
B. Each has the schedule, but mutual awareness is not established.
C. Neither has any useful information.

**Key B.** “Receiving information is different from knowing what others received.”

### P06 — A public acknowledgement
In a joint meeting, both departments acknowledge the revised schedule. Does this alone prove they prefer that schedule?
A. Yes, acknowledgement proves preference.
B. Yes, shared knowledge removes disagreement.
C. No, understanding and preference are different.

**Key C.** “People can share knowledge while disliking the same plan or pursuing different goals.”

### P07 — A copied guarantee
Your repair shop adds a guarantee. A rival matches it. You consider withdrawing yours. What changed since the original decision?
A. The rival's offer customers can choose instead.
B. Nothing relevant, because you control your own guarantee.
C. Proof that every guarantee is profitable.

**Key A.** “Undoing your action does not undo the rival's response.”

### P08 — An apparent non-result
Your team improves a product, competitors respond, and market share stays flat. Which conclusion is supported by that fact alone?
A. The improvement caused no benefit.
B. The improvement fully paid for itself.
C. Neither; further evidence is needed to separate effects and costs.

**Key C.** “An observed total does not establish what would have happened without the improvement.”

### P09 — The shortest estimate
Your proposed two-week timeline wins a project; other teams estimated five weeks. What is a useful check before confirming?
A. Treat being chosen as verification of the estimate.
B. Compare included work, dependencies and actual capacity.
C. Automatically replace your estimate with the longest one.

**Key B.** “Investigate why estimates differ. You may be more efficient or may have omitted work.”

### P10 — Buying to resell
You win a sealed equipment auction with the highest estimate of its resale value. Does winning establish the equipment is worth your bid?
A. No; the winning estimate may be an optimistic error.
B. Yes; losing bidders confirm the winner's valuation.
C. No; all auction winners necessarily lose money.

**Key A.** “Selection can favour overestimation, but neither profit nor loss follows from winning alone.”

### P11 — An expansion plan
A shop expansion depends on new residents moving into the area. That development is delayed. What should be revisited first?
A. A launch promotion that assumes the original number of new residents.
B. A plan to copy the nearest competitor without checking demand.
C. The demand assumption and the alternatives under delayed growth.

**Key C.** “A changed supporting assumption calls for comparison, not automatic persistence or cancellation.”

### P12 — A review trigger
You tolerate a competitor serving a small niche. Which event most directly challenges the premise that it remains limited?
A. Your operating costs rise while the rival keeps its original service.
B. The competitor expands into your main service throughout the day.
C. More customers arrive, but the rival continues serving only its niche.

**Key B.** “A trigger should challenge a reason the plan depended on. You still examine costs and other conditions before responding.”

- **O1 confusion:** replay the two pictures with the learner's ads off in both. Ask what changed outside their company; explain before another assessment.

- **O4 confusion:** restore the four-frame timeline. Ask which rival action would remain if the learner reversed theirs.
- **O5 confusion:** compare omitted scope with a genuinely lower-cost method. A win alone is neither proof of error nor proof of accuracy.
