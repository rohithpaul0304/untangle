# Rejected patterns

Status: Draft consolidation, 7 October 2026. Every row records a failure the founder actually rejected or a defect reviewers actually found. Reviewers cite the row; writers avoid it.

| Pattern | Why it fails | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Opening with a conclusion or a diagram | "I'm not able to understand the flow." No role, no event, no question. | Role, event, question in the fox's first line. |
| Rival or second party introduced as a label | "I still didn't understand B." | Say who they are and why their action touches yours before any number. |
| A choice with no acknowledgment | "When I selected … there was no explanation." | Every branch acknowledges the selection and bridges to the next scene. |
| Lesson-administration choices | "What do you want to test first… I don't want things like this." | The story supplies the next event; the learner predicts or acts in the world. |
| One number, two meanings | "Why is the 20 and 15 again?" | One current state per object; separate a price from money earned. |
| Every sentence its own tap | 26 taps, first decision at tap 10. "Too long, not conversational." | Keep a question with its necessary setup; first consequence within 12 taps. |
| Explaining a change that is not shown | Text said customers switched; both groups stayed unchanged on screen. | Animate the exact causal chain; counts reconcile. |
| Generic praise | "Green box … not helpful." | Address the actual answer; explain through the fox and the scene change. |
| Appending blocks below | "On every response, new screen, adding at the bottom." | Replace the beat with its consequence; keep familiar objects. |
| Decorative motion | "Static … jiggle … hands." | Purposeful gestures that respond to events; both paws; settle. |
| Concept name missing or buried | "Concept name should be seen … what did you learn." | Mechanism, then visible name, plain definition, fresh application. |
| Obvious advice in plain words | "Very simple, no takeaway, work." | Require an inference the learner can use at work. |
| Grading an uninformed guess on a hidden rule | Saving 40 in costs did not determine money left; the twist came from an invented payoff. | Unscored intuition, then teach; scored only when the rule is visible. |
| Arbitrary restriction to force the answer | The locked-freelancer-price example. | Rules the world would plausibly have. |
| Forced actions dressed as decisions | Nothing changes whichever you pick. | Choices change a visible outcome, the investigation order or the plan. |
| Prudent-versus-careless options | One option is obviously foolish. | Two plausible actions, each protecting something. |
| Correct answer in the longer option | Length and moral tone give it away. | Parallel options of similar length. |
| Payoff explained before the move | No reason to act. | Act, then see. |
| Same example with new nouns called transfer | It is repetition, not transfer. | Change the relevant condition: window, constraint, cause, recoverability. |
| Withholding teaching after a wrong attempt | Learner left stuck. | Targeted feedback, a fresh item, then a worked example and a pause. |
| Chronology or arithmetic that contradicts the prose | "Saturday work meets Friday's deadline." | Check dates and sums in every branch. |
| A whole chapter of choose → explain → card | "More surprising examples alone won't prevent boredom." | Vary the central activity; curate the strongest stories. |
| Report editing and draggable fractions | "It's not fun." Correct answers, worksheet feel, no personal stakes. | A role with something to lose, a consequential action, a visible result, a reason to continue. |
| Three identical designers, same brief, no lenses | All three converged on bottleneck puzzles. | Assign each designer a different lens from the pattern library. |
| Checking arithmetic before judging play | Full logic traces on candidates that were then discarded. | Fun gate first; blind check the winner only. |
| Disclaimers throughout the body | The reader reads hedges instead of content. | One status line; retain material assumptions and evidence limits beside the relevant claims. |
| Claiming fun, engagement or retention from an AI review | None of these can be observed without a person. | Status words only; human test to claim anything about people. |
| Restoring old files over newer work | Lost corrections. | Archive, never overwrite; the locked opening stays as snapshotted. |
| Same rejected premise in three presentations | Founder rejected A/B/C and the interview-prep scenario: “It's bad. There's no hook.” All three inherited the same narrow feature-fit exercise. | Reopen the situation and meaningful decision before scripting; independent interaction lenses cannot rescue a weak shared premise. Compare different premises when premise quality is in doubt. |
| Quiz format replaces play; answers echo the stem | Correct classifications can be earned by word matching while no choice changes anything. A blind answer-key match does not establish inference or play. | Preserve the opening before Q&A, test lexical matching, use behavioural evidence and plausible alternatives, then ask for a product action with a visible consequence. |

| Obvious choices followed by a restated answer | Founder rejected the generated content’s “obvious choices and explanation” on 8 October 2026. | Let the learner manipulate a consequential situation; explain the cause and limits. In prisoner’s dilemma, expose why individually better actions can produce a jointly worse outcome; do not hide the table to fake difficulty. |
| Rules in a top card before an abrupt choice | Founder: “Without any context we have jumped into the screen.” The first B deck put essential setup and the whole sentence deal above the fox. | Fox establishes the story before choices. Refined later the same day: the deal is one in-world event stated by a character and kept visible as a prop, not one screen per rule; one beat per rule produced a 45-tap lecture. Teaching dialogue comes through the fox or a character in the scene; supporting visuals never carry untold rules. Coordinator must play from a fresh start and verify this before sharing. |

| Crude flat illustrations beside the polished fox | Founder said the visuals looked drawn by a kid and asked for another model. | Use art with matching dimensional quality; review it in the actual scene before scaling. Graphs serve exact comparisons; motion explains state changes. Never claim animation is perfect from technical checks. |
| Hypothetical and rewind framing: "Imagine Ravi stays silent", "Imagine both discounting", "Reset · before" | Founder, 8 October 2026: "Don't want anything that says 'imagine Ravi is silent' or anything. This has to be bleeding. Don't rewire things. It has to flow." What-ifs narrated by the guide are not events; the learner never lives the consequence. | Things happen in the world and stay happened. Comparisons come only from the deal itself (stated once by a character, kept visible), from what another character actually did, and from what the learner actually does across rounds. Repetition with a visible history replaces imagined branches. |
| Characters that do not react | Ravi and the café owner sat in the same pose whether betrayed or not; the counters carried all the meaning. | Expression states tied to outcomes for every character in the scene (scared, ashamed, cold, hurt, thawing), produced like the fox stills. A face changes only when something happened. |

| Dense inspector offer duplicated in a numerical grid | Founder found the flow-v2 deal screen too complicated. | Present three plain-language outcomes once; label people and years directly instead of slash pairs. Preserve full rules before choice and the approved preceding flow. |

| Offer appears before conversational setup | Founder found “Ravi is getting the same offer” too abrupt and requested a pre-screen. | Introduce the inspector and offer first; disclose one rule per screen; mention Ravi’s same offer only after the learner knows what it is. Present the choice after all outcomes. |


## 2026-10-08 — Prison outcome continuity

Outcome → explanation felt disconnected. Start from the learner’s actual choice and keep the actual result visible. Explain one conditional comparison per screen; no repeated full payoff matrix.


## 2026-10-08 — Fox dialogue hierarchy

Fox dialogue was too small and easy to overlook. Narration must lead supporting inspector text and optional details.


| Café earnings detached from a real product | Founder could not interpret what was sold or how revenue followed. | Name the commodity, show actual selling prices, distinguish sales from money left after costs, and explain the cause visually before totals. |


| Fixed two-price café controls and unclear earnings terms | Founder requested dynamic prices including ₹80, ₹70 and ₹50. | Let learners set a numeric price, teach customer response, and show customer pays − making coffee = money you keep. |


| Cafés distinguished only by colour | Founder could not clearly interpret which café was which. | Use distinct building silhouettes and architecture plus explicit ownership labels. Preserve dimensional art quality. |


| Price-list introduction mixed choice, cost and earnings | Founder found six-price list and long explanation confusing. | Use a practice counter with separately labelled selling price, fixed making cost and money kept; changing it must not record sales. |


| In-play price history as a table or expandable panel | Founder: this is a game, not SaaS. | Show only the relevant price-copy connection; no in-play history panel. |
