# Untangle: how to write learning stories

Derived from founder feedback through 5 October 2026. These are project preferences, not measured learner-test results. Apply them before proposing or writing new lessons. The founder should not have to explain them again. Later explicit feedback overrides these rules.

## Product purpose

Help people replace some habitual Instagram scrolling with satisfying learning. Earn attention through curiosity, meaningful decisions and useful discoveries. Animation, coins, a roadmap and a mascot cannot rescue an unclear or shallow lesson.

Plain language does not mean basic reasoning. The chapter can be long; each scene should be easy to follow. Give learners an insight they can use in everyday life, especially work, rather than advice they already know.

## Chapter editing and interaction variety

Founder-approved direction: more surprising examples alone will not prevent boredom. Curate the strongest stories rather than continually adding compulsory concepts. Give each story a different central activity: predict and compare, investigate motives, reveal viewpoints, reconstruct a timeline, inspect a deal, or revise a plan.

Do not repeat “choose → fox explains → concept card” for an entire chapter. The concept still needs a clear name and explanation; vary how the learner arrives there. Keep questions at meaningful moments: predictions, evidence-gathering, causal explanations and changed assumptions. Do not interrupt every scene with a quiz. Retain closing MCQs and a separate fresh practice set, aligned to concepts actually taught on the main route.

Approved storytelling improvement: start with a concrete dilemma, reveal one missing fact, show how it changes the decision, then name the concept. Use recognisable features and customer problems (live delivery tracking versus shared lunch orders), not vague labels such as “better dashboard.” Keep optional source coverage without forcing every example into the main route.

## Required authoring order

1. Read PRODUCT.md and relevant supplied source material.
2. Write the story and all outcome branches before building the prototype.
3. Review it from an end user's perspective, without relying on explanations outside the script.
4. Resolve story problems before adding presentation or animation.
5. Carry approved scripts into implementation; do not quietly substitute weaker examples.

Current direction: script-first. Approval of dialogue or one example is not automatic authorization to resume prototype development.

## The teaching pattern the founder approved

Familiar situation → puzzling behaviour → player prediction or action → visible consequence → reveal the missing incentive or condition → explain the cause → name the concept → check understanding → practical work application.

The hook should create “I thought I understood—now I see what I missed.” Do not manufacture surprise by hiding decisive rules, choosing convenient outcomes after a move, or forcing a wrong answer. A learner who predicts correctly still needs an explanation and a meaningful assumption check.

Before naming a concept, make its mechanism visible. Then show the exact name prominently, define it in plain language, connect it to the learner's action and check understanding in a fresh situation.

Do not confuse strategic **interdependence** with independence. Correct a mistaken term kindly and explain its meaning immediately.

## Every decision needs five things

- **Role:** Who am I in this scene?
- **Goal:** What am I trying to achieve?
- **Facts:** What do I know before choosing?
- **Control:** What can I actually change?
- **Stakes:** What does each option protect or risk?

The learner should understand the available actions even if they cannot predict which outcome is best. Useful uncertainty concerns consequences; avoid uncertainty about what a button means.

Write options as concrete actions in the learner's voice. Keep them parallel and short. Two meaningful choices usually beat several vague ones. Do not pair an obviously foolish response with a long, thoughtful answer. Do not make the learner feel judged or patronised.

Strategic decisions show consequences, not generic right/wrong banners. Explicit understanding checks may have correct answers and explanations. Distinguish preferences, predictions and demonstrated understanding.

## Approved clarity example

**Role and situation:** You lead a design team. This week is fully booked. Sales promised another client a finished design by Friday without checking with you. Delivering it requires two evenings of overtime.

**Prompt:** “How will you handle this request?”

- “Accept Friday. Ask the team to work late.”
- “Offer Tuesday. Ask Sales to change the deadline.”

Both protect something and cost something. Show the outcomes before explaining the process that created the problem.

Rejected earlier choices included “Late promises won't work / The team will probably rescue them.” They asked for an abstract interpretation before giving a clear role or decision. Another version claimed Saturday work could meet Friday's deadline: check chronology as well as prose.

Only this decision setup has been approved. Its complete commitment lesson still needs convincing outcomes and source-grounded explanation.

## Approved concept introduction: strategic interdependence

Keep the learner's action identical and vary only the competitor's action:

| Your ads | Competitor's ads | Your money left in the fictional model |
|---|---|---:|
| Off | On | 40 |
| Off | Off | 100 |

First establish: “Your ads are off in both cases.”
Then explain: “Your competitor's advertising advantage disappeared when it stopped too.”
Then state: “You made the same choice, but got a different result because of their choice.”
Only then reveal: **Strategic interdependence — your result depends on what you choose AND what others choose.**

A celebratory card alone does not teach the term. A prediction about actual historical companies cannot be graded by an invented numerical model.

## Approved rationality sequence

Mira declines an ₹800 engagement to honour an existing ₹500 promise. She explains that keeping the promise matters more than the extra ₹300. The higher-paying event moves to another day; she accepts because the conflict disappears.

Explain before naming: her choice fits her priorities; she did not stop caring about money.
**Rationality:** choosing what best serves one's goals given one's beliefs. It does not mean emotionless behaviour or always taking more money. Do not imply any choice can be declared rational without checking goals, beliefs and consistency.

## Work applications should reveal something useful

Founder prefers stronger workplace applications over generic restaurant choices or “ask your manager about timing.”

Approved example: a successful automation trial saves hours, yet a manager resists. The fictional manager explains a concern that reduced reported workload could cost the team a position. The learner discovers that a company-level benefit can threaten something another person values.

Practical tip: before pitching a change, investigate who gains, who bears the risk, and what each person is rewarded for.

Approved next example: everyone dislikes repetitive status meetings, yet attendance signals commitment to the manager. Skipping alone has a reputation cost. Changing what counts as participation changes incentives. Teach the difference between individual incentives and collective outcomes.

Use the intrigue of hidden incentives, not claims of “dark psychology” or mind-reading. In fictional scenes, reveal motives through authored statements or evidence. In work tips, invite investigation rather than assert that real colleagues have secret motives. Understanding a goal does not guarantee agreement.

## Source discipline

Founder preference: no citations, source footers, book attributions or reference links in learner-facing scripts or conversational lesson examples. Keep source verification and attribution in internal author notes only. Preserve plain-language distinctions between fictional examples and real evidence where needed; accuracy does not require a citation footer.

- Record the exact source in internal author notes and distinguish its idea from our invented story. Do not append citations to learner-facing content.
- Never present an original designer/client example as a case from the book or professor.
- Preserve the reasoning, not merely the source's topic label.
- Supplied professor material contains one duplicated foundations transcript and course listings, not the whole course. It challenges definitions of rationality, explains its role as a benchmark, develops shared knowledge and distinguishes individual decisions from games.
- His cigarette lecture was not supplied. The cigarette paper was supplied separately.
- The cigarette opening is a practical mystery; its model has invented numbers and fixed spending. The research reports stock-market evidence consistent with benefits to established firms, with other mechanisms including entry barriers. Neither proves every advertising ban raises profits or settles health effects.
- McKinsey belongs at the end of teaching, before closing MCQs and separate practice. Compare changing assumptions and responses; do not invent a quantitative model from missing exhibits.
- Keep all previously requested source coverage traceable. Do not drop a case silently during revision.

## Avoid these repeated failures

- Obvious tasks dressed up as strategy: “pass to the open player” without the deeper insight about indirect contribution.
- Arbitrary restrictions introduced only to guarantee the intended answer, as in the weak locked-freelancer-price example.
- Too many names, moving numbers or rules at once.
- Forced actions presented as meaningful decisions.
- Explaining the payoff before letting the learner act.
- Long setup and repeated receipts that bury the original hook.
- Repeating an example with different nouns and calling it independent transfer.
- Putting the correct answer in a longer or more respectable-sounding option.
- Withholding teaching after an incorrect discovery attempt.
- Claims that a script is compelling because the assistant likes it.

## Screen and character requirements for later implementation

Use an original Duolingo-inspired guided journey, not a quiz dressed up with a mascot. Each committed response opens a replacement consequence screen. The next activity replaces that screen; do not keep adding blocks below it.

Approved fox remains consistent, compact and expressive. Gestures and scene changes explain what happened. Avoid perpetual jiggling or decorative motion. Respect reduced motion. Use one clear next action, exact progress restoration and genuine stopping points.

The chapter ends with explicit MCQs and a separate practice set. The main teaching remains scenario-led. Adaptive feedback selects reviewed material; do not invent scored questions or silently alter rules live.

## Review checklist before presenting a draft

1. Can I describe the interesting question without a technical term?
2. Does the learner know their role, goal, facts, control and stakes?
3. Are both options understandable actions rather than one foolish and one virtuous answer?
4. Does each branch follow the same declared rules, including dates and arithmetic?
5. Does the learner see the cause before the concept name?
6. Can I define the concept using exactly what happened in the scene?
7. Does someone who anticipated the outcome still learn something?
8. Does the practical tip change how someone interprets or handles work?
9. Are model assumptions and source evidence clearly distinguished?
10. Can a learner finish without the author supplying extra explanation?

If a check fails, revise before presenting. Label assistant review honestly; it is not a human test, a second independent reviewer, or proof of retention. Do not invent quality scores. When reviewing, report substantive remaining weaknesses instead of repeatedly declaring the draft ready.

## Narrative continuity

Do not move the learner from observer to executive and back to historian without a compelling reason. Opening now keeps one role: investigator testing the same question. Label fictional companies consistently as A and B. Both paths must actually show the two conditions used in the explanation. Never say “your ads stayed off” after the learner kept them on. Latest opening: `chapters/chapter-1-opening-investigation.md`.

Introduce relationships before numbers: A/B labels alone do not explain the cast. Establish both as rival cigarette companies competing for the same customers, show why one company's ads affect the other, then introduce equal starting receipts and the experiment. Do not describe only A's finances while leaving B unexplained.

## Show the claim happening

A screenshot described customers switching while both TVs and both customer groups stayed unchanged. Remove such explanatory-only screens. Animate the exact causal chain: ad switches, customer distribution changes, payments go from customers to firms, ad costs leave, remaining balances settle. Do not animate money transferring directly between firms. Hide setup/model prose behind optional details; keep the primary fox line short.

## Choices are about the story, not lesson administration

Founder rejects “What should we test first?” and similar experiment-order prompts. The narrative supplies the next event; learners predict its consequence or make an in-world decision. Opening: A stops ads to save money → predict → watch customer payments shift → ban stops B too → predict → watch both balances. Keep agency through meaningful predictions, not choosing which teaching explanation to receive first.
