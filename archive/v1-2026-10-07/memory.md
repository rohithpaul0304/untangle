## Most important learning: a fair discovery needs a learnable rule

Our cigarette scene asked whether stopping ads would leave more or less money. It showed a cost of 40, but did not establish how much customer spending would be lost. It then revealed 40 remaining as though that outcome followed from the information provided.

That prediction was under-specified. Saving ad costs can leave more or less money depending on the sales effect. The exact result comes from our invented model. Neither a prettier ledger nor shorter dialogue repairs this missing causal rule.

Before a scored or reasoned choice, establish enough information to reason. An initial intuition question may deliberately precede instruction, but it must be unscored, acknowledged, and followed by teaching. Do not call the learner wrong for lacking a hidden assumption. Surprise should reveal a useful relationship, not arbitrary information withheld to force a twist.

The old 40-versus-100 comparison can illustrate interdependence after the rules are understood. Earlier approval of that comparison does not validate its onboarding or the current implementation.

## How to write each interaction

- Establish role, goal, known facts, available control, and stakes before a consequential decision.
- Keep the role stable. Do not switch from investigator to company owner to historian without a necessary, explicit bridge.
- Use concrete, parallel options that sound like actions or predictions a person would make. Avoid vague labels, abstract interpretations, and long options that signal the correct answer.
- Preserve choice before explanation where meaningful. Do not turn every line into an MCQ or every sentence into a Continue tap.
- Never ask “What should we test first?” as a substitute for story agency. Let the story provide the event; ask about a meaningful move or consequence.
- Acknowledge the actual response before changing context. A learner who predicted correctly still deserves a useful explanation or deeper condition to consider.
- Show cause, then explain the relationship, then name the concept clearly, then use a fresh situation to check understanding.
- Explain strategic interdependence as outcomes depending on your choices AND others’ choices. Do not call it strategic independence.
- Explain rationality through goals and beliefs; it is not always choosing money or having no emotions.
- Workplace intrigue should expose incentives through evidence, not pretend to read minds or promise “dark psychology.”

## Voice and language

Conversational, direct, concrete, and brief. One immediate thought or question at a time. Familiar words; explain new concept names when introduced. Respect adult intelligence while making instructions easy enough for a beginner.

Do not shorten copy until context disappears. “Only A stops its ads. B keeps advertising” is more useful than a vague “What happens now?” However, words alone still cannot supply a rule we never taught.

Prefer a response that connects to the learner’s thinking: “You did save the ad cost. Now watch the customers.” Then show the effect. Do not replace a causal explanation with “Nice work,” a green box, or a claim that the result is obvious.

Avoid labels such as “strong” and “popular” when their strategic meaning is unclear. Introduce relationships before names and quantities. Avoid repeated visible model disclaimers and source footers; keep source notes internally while preserving truthful distinctions between fiction and history.

## Visuals, motion, and transitions

The approved fox is the consistent guide, compact enough not to obscure content. It needs meaningful expressions and both hands where appropriate. Do not use disconnected limbs, frightening proportions, or perpetual jiggling. Approval of mascot imagery does not approve a lesson.

Use the supplied Duolingo recording as a hierarchy reference: white space, a clear character prompt, readable response area, playful outlined controls, and visible feedback. Earlier muted cream/sage styling was rejected.

A committed answer should flow into a replacement consequence view, then the next task. Do not append endless blocks. Preserve the setting and relevant objects across the change so replacement does not feel like a jump.

Motion should explain what changed: ads turn off, customers move, customer payments follow, costs leave, balances settle. Do not animate money moving directly from one company to another. Show one causal event at a time; a final spreadsheet is not the event itself.

The fox should not compete with the important movement. Let people replay a consequence and understand its final still state. Support reduced motion. Never require catching a fleeting animation to understand the rule.

Twenty-six tiny taps are not good pacing. Keep necessary context together. Count meaningful actions, not only screens or words.
