# Three different JTBD situations

Status: Draft. Three different source-grounded situations retained for discussion; earlier weak treatments preserved separately. No founder selection, playable test or human learning result.

## 1. The milkshake that should take longer to drink

**Opening:** “You run a fast-food chain. Your team can make the milkshake easier to drink—or make it last longer. Which change do you launch?”

**First decision:** Make it thinner / Make it thicker.

**What changes:** An illustrative journey shows the selected drink emptying sooner or lasting longer alongside the commute. The first choice is a prediction, not a graded question: the customer circumstances have not yet been disclosed. The scene then reveals the documented morning purchase pattern and the hunger-and-boredom need. It does not fabricate recipe timings, sales or a customer study.

**Discovery:** A drink is serving part of a journey. Speeding up consumption can remove something the customer values. Contrast this with checkout: waiting to pay does not serve that purpose. The same person can want a faster purchase and a slower drink.

**Practical product lesson:** Before removing friction, find out what the customer is trying to achieve. Time saved is not automatically value gained.

**Coordinator assessment:** Strongest current opening. Familiar object, visible physical change, counterintuitive improvement. Risk: familiar JTBD case; the checkout contrast must make the lesson more than “thicker lasts longer.” This is a design judgment, not proof of interest.

## 2. The slower way to rent a film

**Opening:** “Your rival lets people collect a film today. You could send films by post. What would make the wait worth it?”

**First decision:** Invest in nearby shelves of new releases / Invest in a personal film queue and postal delivery.

**What changes:** The first direction builds local availability for an evening's viewing. The second builds a catalogue and delivery route that preserves the postal wait while removing a return deadline. The player sees what each direction gives up. These are simplified service-design comparisons, not a replay of historical executive decisions.

**Discovery:** Both services rented films, but the HBS account describes different circumstances: getting something for tonight versus discovering suitable films and watching on one's own schedule. Being slower on one measure does not make a service worse for every purpose.

**Practical product lesson:** Before copying a competitor's strengths, establish whether its customers are trying to accomplish the same thing as yours.

**Coordinator assessment:** Retain as an alternative or later application. Recognisable companies and a substantial product-strategy decision. Weaker visual mechanism than the milkshake; avoid a brand-winner quiz or claiming JTBD alone explains Netflix's success. This is the historical DVD-by-mail period, not streaming.

## 3. A new home loses to an old dining table

**Documented puzzle:** A developer attracted visitors but few buyers. Adding requested bay windows did not improve sales. Interviews with actual buyers surfaced decisions about the old dining table and what it represented about family. The eventual offer combined design changes with help moving, storing and sorting belongings.

**Proposed first decision:** Improve the home itself / Help buyers make the move.

This first response is a prediction, not a graded inference. The dining-table clue should follow it rather than appear as an answer cue. This is the coordinator's proposed simplification of the designer's table-versus-storage choice; it has not been scripted or play-tested.

**What the learner sees:** Begin with the desirable new home, then reveal what remains in the old one. A better window changes the destination; belongings and unresolved decisions remain. Moving/storage/sorting support changes what must be decided before moving day. Keep the actual historical outcome separate from this illustrative comparison: the developer combined home design and service changes; the source does not isolate one intervention's causal effect or guarantee that storage wins a sale.

**Discovery:** Customers may want the destination and still be unable to make the transition. The business opportunity includes making that transition possible.

**Practical product lesson:** Before adding another feature, ask what customers must leave behind, transfer or reorganise to start using your product. For software, migration and switching concerns are questions to investigate, not assumptions about every customer.

**Coordinator assessment:** Strongest hook of the three after checking the primary account. It joins a substantial business problem with an unexpected concrete object. Risk: turning it into generic emotional storytelling. Keep the product decision and the practical change visible; do not invent sentimental dialogue or promise a sale.

## Decision

Recommend the homebuyer case for the next short storyboard. Milkshake is the strongest alternative for a compact visual mechanism; Netflix works better as a later application. No new screens are authorised by this comparison. The founder has not selected a premise. All prior interview-prep openings remain rejected. No new scenario is an approved constraint on future designers.
