# The unwatched DVD

Status: Draft

**Grounded facts.** The supplied HBS account contrasts early-2000s Blockbuster movie-night trips with Netflix DVD-by-mail: discovering films suited to personal tastes, watching on one's own schedule, and no late fees or return deadlines. These are contrasting circumstances, not universal customer types or a complete explanation of either company's fortunes. The customer, calendar and competing offers below are explicitly fictional teaching assumptions; neither offer is presented as an actual historical plan.

**Exact opening.** “You run a DVD rental service. Leena has returned the film she wanted most—unwatched. You can change her next rental. Send something easier to finish, or let her keep this one longer?”

A plain label establishes: “Fictional customer. Based on the DVD-by-mail era.” Leena adds: “I still want this film. I keep saving it for an evening when I won't be interrupted.”

**First decision.** The player chooses a concrete offer, unscored:

- “Send a shorter film. Keep the weekly return.”
- “Send her film again. Remove the return date.”

Before committing, both offers show their costs: the shorter film matches her taste and fits a brief evening; keeping her chosen film means waiting longer before receiving another. She can hold one disc at a time in this fictional comparison. Her calendar shows short gaps this week and a free evening next week. Both actions protect something reasonable: getting to watch now versus getting to watch the particular film under the conditions she wants.

**Visual consequence of each choice.**

Shorter film: its smaller runtime strip fits into this week's gap. Leena watches it; its envelope moves back to the service. Her original film remains on her “Still want to watch” list. “That filled tonight. I still want an evening for this one.” The rental completed; the original purpose remains unfinished.

No return date: her chosen disc stays beside her player as this week's brief gaps pass. Next week's free evening arrives; its runtime strip fits. The disc moves into the player and off her waiting list. “That's the evening I was waiting for.” Fewer completed rentals over the same interval; the intended experience happens. Neither branch invents sales, satisfaction scores or historical customer behaviour.

**Earned discovery.** A completed rental can be the wrong measure of progress. Helping someone consume more films is different from helping them enjoy a particular film on their terms. Name **Jobs to Be Done** only afterward: the progress someone hires a product to help them make in their circumstances. Connect the invented comparison to the source's real contrast between immediate rental and viewing on one's own schedule.

**Reason to continue.** “Now she has guests coming tonight. Does the same offer still help?” Same person, different occasion: the player must reconsider rather than permanently label Leena a ‘Netflix customer.’

**Strongest failure risk.** The stated calendar may make the answer too apparent; removing it would create an unfair reveal. This is a modest paradox about measuring success, not a naturally dramatic story. Reject it if ‘an unwatched rental can be serving its purpose’ fails to provoke curiosity; another presentation will not rescue that weakness.
