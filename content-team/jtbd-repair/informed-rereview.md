## Seven gate answers

1. **Role and goal — yes.** “You run a fast-food chain. Make your morning shake worth coming back for” appears before the opening choices.
2. **Agency with uncertainty — yes.** Easier sipping versus longer duration remains a plausible unscored opening choice. The afternoon alternatives also initially appear plausible, but the feedback does not fairly resolve them.
3. **Fair discovery — no across the complete candidate.** Opening cup/drive discovery remains explicitly unscored and illustrated. Afternoon evidence says only that a child took “a few slow sips” and stopped after lunch. It never shows whether slow sipping was valued, tolerated, or caused stopping. Smaller is depicted finished; thinner is left unknown. The displayed facts do not justify calling preservation of slow sipping the stronger hypothesis.
4. **Pull — yes as an engagement hypothesis.** The opening's unfinished drive creates a reason to investigate. A child leaving half a shake creates a second question. These are design affordances, not human reports of wanting to continue.
5. **Usable idea — yes with a serious teaching caveat.** The learner can articulate that a commute and an after-meal occasion may call for different product choices. However, this instance risks teaching that repeating observed behaviour is equivalent to identifying desired progress.
6. **Second move — no, blocking.** Context genuinely changes from commute to meal, a material improvement over prepay matching. Yet “few slow sips” is repeated in the observation, smaller rule, and corrective rationale. The question resolves by preserving those words rather than by discriminating why the child stopped. “The smaller sample keeps the few slow sips you observed. It is a stronger idea to test here” makes that failure explicit.
7. **Stop signs — present, blocking.** The afternoon exercise asks learners to “launch” one sample, then funnels the thinner choice into the smaller plan. Its correction privileges a preference the evidence has not established. The tested phone controls are clear and visible; the issue is worksheet-like answer matching and insufficient evidence, not control ambiguity.
