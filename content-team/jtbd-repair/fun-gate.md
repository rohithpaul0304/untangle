## Seven answers

1. **Role and goal — yes.** First screen says “You run a fast-food chain. Make your morning milkshake worth coming back for.” The learner owns a product decision immediately.
2. **Agency with uncertainty — yes, opening only.** “Easier to sip, or longer to finish?” offers two plausible product instincts before revealing the drive. Choices produce different illustrated cup/drive consequences.
3. **Fair discovery — yes.** Thinner empties before the drive ends; thicker lasts further into it. The opening explicitly says “No score,” and consequences label the illustration as a possibility, not a measured recipe test. Surprise is a design possibility, not an observed human response.
4. **Pull — yes as a hypothesis, limited to the opening.** “Your cup finishes before this drive does” creates a concrete reason to follow the morning customer. Later explanatory screens reduce that uncertainty; no human motivation claim is made.
5. **Usable idea — yes.** The sequence makes it possible to say: decide which part of someone's situation needs improvement before changing the drink; duration during a drive and waiting before a drive can require opposite fixes.
6. **Second move — no, blocking.** The screen states “Prepay skips the till,” “Pay at arrival ... keeps the wait,” and “Now change the wait, not the drink. These regulars know their order and want to get on the road.” The learner can choose prepay by matching the supplied goal and mechanism, without using Jobs to be Done. It changes the lever, but not the reasoning demand.
7. **Stop signs — present, blocking.** The second choice functions as a comprehension worksheet after three intervening navigation taps. “I add prepay and quick pickup” is already dictated by the prompt. Controls otherwise have clear labels and reachable buttons in the viewed phone screenshots. The ending promises “The next puzzle” but has no control to play it; that is an engagement hypothesis rather than an observed broken promised route.
