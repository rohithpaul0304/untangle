# Prisoner’s dilemma: common designer brief

Status: AI-checked for candidate design, 8 October 2026. Founder approved the story direction; separate premise and learning reviews passed. Human tests: 0.


Founder direction of 8 October 2026 (`founder-direction-2026-10-08.md`, flow in `flow-v2.md`) supersedes two things below: the ending is a repeated-play loop (10 café days with a visible history), and no hypothetical or rewind framing is allowed anywhere. The one-shot dilemma, the story and all numbers stand.

## Scope and objective

Create one playable candidate for curious young Indian adults. Preserve the founder’s story: you and your accomplice Ravi rob a jewellery store, are caught, and are held separately. This identical brief feeds three different interaction lenses; it prescribes no favourite lens. No full scene, app edit, deployment or change to the locked chapter-one opening. Current endpoint: three candidates for review.

Teach **prisoner’s dilemma** only. No Nash equilibrium, repeated games, probability, reputation or second named framework.

Learner outcome: check each person’s incentive while holding the other’s choice fixed; distinguish one person changing from both changing; recheck the conclusion when a stated cost changes. Useful tomorrow: before proposing “everyone should stop,” check what happens to someone who stops alone and what makes continuing attractive.

Mechanism: each can improve their own result through the same action regardless of the other’s fixed choice, yet both doing so leaves both worse off than the alternative shared outcome.

Tempting wrong model: “If both prefer that shared outcome, choosing my part will get me there.” The separating evidence is solo silence giving you five years versus joint silence giving you one. The missing insight is **my move versus both moves**, not a hidden rule. Surprise comes from the competing comparisons; never force a mistaken answer.

## Exact prison rules

Before choosing, show role, goal, control, stakes and the complete deal through short scene beats. Keep the deal available. Each player seeks the fewest prison years for themselves. This is an assigned objective, not their moral worth or everyone’s real values. You control only your statement. Neither sees the other’s final statement beforehand; no communication or enforceable agreement. One fictional decision; replays are labelled what-if comparisons, not later encounters with memory or retaliation.

Actions: “Stay silent” / “Confess.” Confess accepts this fictional deal and names the accomplice; silence refuses it. Zero means no prison years under this invented deal.

| You | Ravi | Your years | Ravi’s years |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Silent | Silent | 1 | 1 |
| Confess | Silent | 0 | 5 |
| Silent | Confess | 5 | 0 |
| Confess | Confess | 3 | 3 |

All consequences are fixed. First choice is unscored; acknowledge the actual selection without inventing its reason. Expose both conditional consequences or state Ravi’s fixed scenario before your choice. Never choose his action in reaction to the learner. Confess/confess is an illustrative branch, not inevitable human behaviour. Viewing Ravi’s room is a teaching comparison, not new power for your character.

Required comparisons:

- Ravi silent: your confess gives 0 versus 1; Ravi confessing: your confess gives 3 versus 5.
- Mirror these from Ravi’s perspective: if you are silent, he gets 0 versus 1; if you confess, he gets 3 versus 5.
- From both confessing: your solo silence changes 3/3 → 5/0; Ravi’s solo silence changes 3/3 → 0/5; joint silence changes 3/3 → 1/1.

Candidate gate: capture an unscored expectation before a solo-switch result within the interaction; for example, what happens to your years if only you become silent while Ravi still confesses? Exact phrasing is open. The opening statement choice alone is not prediction or misconception evidence. Invite the learner to explain or construct the solo/joint difference before the guide supplies it; correct predictions need no invented failure.

Explain the cause: confessing rewards you when Ravi stays silent and costs you fewer years when he confesses. He faces the same pressure. Both silence beats both confession for each, but it is not each person’s individually best possible outcome: zero beats one. Name and define **prisoner’s dilemma** after the learner explores this mechanism. Both initial choices receive the teaching.

## Café application

A fictional one-day model, not business evidence. You run one café; another owner runs a nearby café. Each independently chooses today’s action without seeing the other’s final choice. Goal: greatest own daily profit, meaning money left after costs already counted. No future effects or unlisted costs. Actions: “Run a discount” / “Keep usual prices.”

| You | Other café | Your profit | Other profit |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Usual prices | Usual prices | ₹5,000 | ₹5,000 |
| Discount | Usual prices | ₹6,000 | ₹2,000 |
| Usual prices | Discount | ₹2,000 | ₹6,000 |
| Discount | Discount | ₹3,000 | ₹3,000 |

Discount improves own profit in either fixed case: ₹6,000 > ₹5,000 and ₹3,000 > ₹2,000. Same for the other owner. Yet both discounting earns ₹3,000 each versus ₹5,000 each without discounts. Switching alone from both discounting earns you ₹2,000, not ₹5,000. This is practice recognizing the same structure; it is not the changed-condition challenge.

## Changed condition: one new cost

After naming the concept and exploring baseline cafés, disclose a separate what-if: **each café running a discount pays an additional ₹2,000 campaign cost that day; usual prices incur none. Everything else remains unchanged.** This is an authored condition, not a claim about real discount campaigns. Show the cost and resulting profits before choosing, not as a surprise deduction.

| You | Other café | Your final profit | Other final profit |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Usual prices | Usual prices | ₹5,000 | ₹5,000 |
| Discount | Usual prices | ₹4,000 | ₹2,000 |
| Usual prices | Discount | ₹2,000 | ₹4,000 |
| Discount | Discount | ₹1,000 | ₹1,000 |

Now usual prices wins in either fixed case: ₹5,000 > ₹4,000 and ₹2,000 > ₹1,000. Same for the other café. The taught conflict is gone: individually preferable choices also reach ₹5,000/₹5,000. Best action explicitly changes **discount → usual prices** because the incentive changes. Do not add loyalty, future rounds, promises or enforcement. Require a reason tied to the cost; repeating the old action visibly costs ₹1,000 in either case.

## Agency, feedback and learning checks

- Let the learner cause at least one comparison by changing an action while the other stays fixed. Let them test solo versus joint changes; controls disclose whose choices change. Alternatives remain inspectable. No forced wrong move or erased choice.
- A “which number is smaller?” quiz alone fails. Acknowledge actual action, show before → changed choice → consequence, then explain its cause. Verdict-only feedback fails.
- Use short parallel action labels. No longer correct answer, reasoning embedded in only one option, preferred decoration or foolish-versus-wise contrast.
- Keep player, unit, fixed action and current café version visible. Explicitly switch from minimizing years to maximizing profit. Target consequence within 12 taps, approximately one-minute opening and three-to-five-minute lesson; timings are untested.
- Later practice: a fresh situation and a near-match with changed incentives; require both fixed-other and shared comparisons. After two misses, work one example then offer a fresh attempt. Hinted retries are not independent evidence; retention requires a delayed check.

## Boundaries

Tables and comparisons are checkable; best actions are conditional on stated goals and rules, not predictions of people. Trust alone does not change zero versus one. Loyalty or fairness are not wrong values. No moral scoring, real legal-procedure claim, legal guidance, robbery detail, real café forecast or recommendation to coordinate prices. Better for both cafés says nothing about customer or societal welfare.
