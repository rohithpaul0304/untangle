# Teaching brief: Prisoner’s dilemma

Status: Draft. Founder approved the story direction; this brief awaits separate subject and learning-design review. Human tests: 0.

## Assignment, audience and learning objective

Preserve the approved story: you and your accomplice Ravi rob a jewellery store, are caught, and are separated. Audience: curious young Indian adults who avoid long courses. One concept: **prisoner’s dilemma**, a strategic interaction with conflicting individual and shared incentives. This identical brief serves three independent designers; no favourite interaction lens is prescribed. No app edits, full scene, or locked-opening changes.

Afterwards the learner can check each person’s incentive while holding the other’s choice fixed, distinguish that from both changing together, and recheck the conclusion when a cost changes. Tomorrow’s useful action: before proposing “everyone should stop,” check what happens to someone who stops alone and what makes continuing attractive.

Mechanism: each can improve their own result through the same action regardless of the other’s fixed choice, yet both taking it leaves both worse off than the alternative shared outcome.

Tempting wrong model: “If both prefer a shared outcome, choosing my part will get me there.” The separating evidence: at confess/confess, your silence alone gives you five years; joint silence gives you one. The missing insight is **my move versus both moves**, not an undisclosed rule. Surprise must arise from these competing comparisons; never force a mistaken prediction.

## Exact fictional rules and visible prerequisites

Before the first choice the learner can tell:

- Role: you are one of two accomplices, separated from Ravi after the robbery.
- Goal in this model: each seeks the fewest prison years for themselves. This is an assigned game objective, not a claim about the learner’s values or all human behaviour.
- Control: you choose only your own statement. Ravi chooses his. Neither sees the other’s final statement before choosing; you cannot communicate or make an enforceable joint agreement in this scene.
- Actions: “Stay silent” and “Confess.” Confess means accepting the fictional deal and naming the accomplice; silence means refusing that deal. These are the complete action choices for the game.
- Stakes: the complete four-outcome deal below, clearly labelled years in prison. Lower is better for the assigned objective; zero means no prison years under this invented deal.
- Scope: a single fictional decision. Replays are labelled what-if comparisons of that decision; they are not later encounters where Ravi learns, retaliates, or remembers.

Deliver these prerequisites through the scene in short beats, not a wall of instructions. Keep the deal available while acting. No legal detail or arithmetic knowledge beyond comparing small numbers is a prerequisite.

| Your action | Ravi’s action | Your years | Ravi’s years |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Stay silent | Stay silent | 1 | 1 |
| Confess | Stay silent | 0 | 5 |
| Stay silent | Confess | 5 | 0 |
| Confess | Confess | 3 | 3 |

These consequences never change. The result depends on both selected actions, not on whether the designer wants a dramatic reveal.

### Opponent and replay contract

The first choice is unscored. Acknowledge the actual move without moral judgment or inventing its reason. Expose both conditional consequences, or use an explicitly stated scenario with Ravi’s action fixed before yours. Never select his action in reaction to the learner. Confess/confess is an illustrative branch, not inevitable human behaviour. Each later case is a labelled what-if; moving into Ravi’s room does not grant your character control over him.

## Required causal comparisons

For your own prison years:

- Ravi silent: confess gives 0 instead of 1.
- Ravi confessing: confess gives 3 instead of 5.

For Ravi’s own prison years, with your choice held fixed:

- You silent: Ravi confessing gives him 0 instead of 1.
- You confessing: Ravi confessing gives him 3 instead of 5.

From both confessing: your solo silence changes your years 3 → 5 and Ravi’s 3 → 0. Ravi’s solo silence makes the mirrored change. Joint silence changes both 3 → 1. Both silence is better for each than both confess; it is not your individually best outcome among all four outcomes (zero is better than one).

Explain the cause before naming the concept: the deal rewards confessing when the other stays silent, and makes confessing the less costly choice when the other confesses. Ravi faces the same pressures. Those individual incentives conflict with the outcome both would prefer to mutual confession. Do not stop at “3 is greater than 1” or “you both lose.”

After the learner has acted and compared, name **prisoner’s dilemma** and define it using these observed facts. Both initial choices receive the teaching; a correct intuition does not skip it.

## Café application and changed condition

This is an illustrative model, not evidence about real cafés or business advice. You run one café and another owner runs the nearby café. Each chooses today’s discount independently; neither observes the other’s final choice beforehand. Your goal is your own café’s greatest profit for this one day. “Profit” means money left after the costs already counted in the table, not sales revenue. No future loyalty, retaliation, extra menu options, or unlisted cost may decide an answer.

Actions: “Run a discount” and “Keep usual prices.” All figures are fictional rupees of daily profit.

| Your action | Other café’s action | Your profit | Other profit |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Keep usual prices | Keep usual prices | ₹5,000 | ₹5,000 |
| Run a discount | Keep usual prices | ₹6,000 | ₹2,000 |
| Keep usual prices | Run a discount | ₹2,000 | ₹6,000 |
| Run a discount | Run a discount | ₹3,000 | ₹3,000 |

Hold the other café’s action fixed: discount improves your profit from ₹5,000 to ₹6,000 or from ₹2,000 to ₹3,000. The other café faces the mirrored comparison. Yet both earn ₹5,000 without discounts, versus ₹3,000 with both discounting. Moving alone from both discounting to usual prices leaves you ₹2,000; it does not create ₹5,000 for both.

This baseline application changes the setting but retains the structure. Do not claim it alone meets the changed-condition requirement.

### Bounded alternative: a disclosed new cost reverses the choice

After the concept is named and the baseline café conflict has been explored, change exactly one assumption: **in this separate what-if version, each café running a discount must pay an additional ₹2,000 campaign cost that day. A café keeping usual prices pays no campaign cost. All other outcomes remain as before.** Label it as an invented condition, not a claim about how discounts normally work.

Show the new cost and its resulting profits before the learner’s decision. The learner should reason with the changed incentives, not calculate a surprise deduction afterward. Do not change customer loyalty, add a future day, impose a cooperation promise, or introduce enforcement alongside the cost.

| Your action | Other café’s action | Your profit after new cost | Other profit after new cost |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Keep usual prices | Keep usual prices | ₹5,000 | ₹5,000 |
| Run a discount | Keep usual prices | ₹4,000 | ₹2,000 |
| Keep usual prices | Run a discount | ₹2,000 | ₹4,000 |
| Run a discount | Run a discount | ₹1,000 | ₹1,000 |

Now usual prices earn you more in either fixed case: ₹5,000 versus ₹4,000, or ₹2,000 versus ₹1,000. The other owner has the same incentive. This version no longer has the taught conflict: individually preferable choices also reach the shared ₹5,000/₹5,000 result. No new concept name is necessary.

The changed action is explicit: **baseline → discount; additional campaign cost → usual prices**, conditional on the stated one-day profit objective and rules. Repeating “always discount because competition” now visibly reduces your own profit by ₹1,000 in either case. Explain that the incentive changed; do not praise “cooperation” as if virtue made the numbers change.

## Learning design and agency criteria

- Predict: the unscored opening statement is a choice, not evidence of a misconception. Before revealing a solo-switch consequence, ask an unscored, bounded expectation: with Ravi still confessing, what happens to your years if only you switch to silence? Capture it before running the change, then acknowledge and explain the result. Do not grade guesses about Ravi’s psychology.
- Reveal: expose the incentive in both rooms, then let the learner produce a solo-change comparison and contrast it with a joint change. Known rules yield a new insight.
- Second move: the café cost variation reverses the best action. Require a consequence and a reason tied to the cost. Baseline café practice alone is not changed-condition transfer.
- Agency: at least one comparison must be caused by the learner changing an action while the other stays fixed. Controls disclose whose choices can change. Alternatives stay inspectable; no forced wrong move or erased choice. A sequence of “which number is smaller?” questions fails.
- Feedback: acknowledge the actual move, show before → changed choice → resulting years/profit, explain the cause. A verdict alone fails.
- Labels: short, parallel action phrases; no conspicuously longer correct answer, reasoning embedded only in one option, preferred-option decoration, or foolish-versus-wise contrast.
- Load: keep player, unit, goal, fixed action and café version visible. Explicitly switch from minimizing years to maximizing profit. Present the new cost before it matters; never confuse profit with sales. Keep the opening short: consequence within 12 taps; approximately one-minute opening and three-to-five-minute lesson are untested targets.
- Practice after selection: a fresh two-person situation requiring both fixed-other comparisons and a shared-result comparison, plus a near-match where a changed incentive removes the conflict. Require a causal explanation. After two misses, demonstrate one complete comparison then offer a fresh attempt. Hinted retries are practice, not independent evidence; retention needs a delayed check.

## Factual boundaries and review

Objectively checkable: all table entries, fixed-other comparisons, symmetry, solo-versus-joint changes and the campaign-cost deduction. Conditional: the best action depends on the stated own-outcome goal, available actions and single-decision horizon; incentives do not guarantee human behaviour.

Loyalty, fairness, guilt and future relationships can matter to real people. Do not score them as wrong values or silently add them to the answer key. Certainty that Ravi stays silent still leaves zero versus one under the assigned goal; trust alone does not reverse that comparison. No moral scoring, criminal-procedure claim, legal guidance, robbery instructions, café forecast or recommendation to coordinate prices. Better for both cafés does not mean better for customers or society, which this model does not measure.
