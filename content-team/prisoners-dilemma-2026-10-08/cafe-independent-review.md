## Findings

The café introduces neither a product nor concrete prices. “Usual prices” and “Discount” are underspecified actions. Daily profit values have no visible connection to customers, sales, or costs. Revenue (sales collected) and profit (money remaining after costs) must not be interchangeable. The current interface calls its totals profit.

Transition changes setting before explaining the new condition: choices now affect a continuing relationship. Introduce that through the fox before the new economy. Meera’s fixed copy-yesterday rule should be disclosed before an optimization challenge; discovery play must remain unscored and must not imply realistic prediction of people.

Taste guidance applied to audit: preserve existing identity, establish a clear focal point, reduce competing blocks, use purposeful motion. At 375 × 812, an empty ten-day grid comes first, followed by decorative buildings, a four-outcome money table, then the fox. The first decision appears below the initial viewport. Prioritize product and price signs, then fox guidance and action. Introduce history after the first result; place detailed arithmetic behind a breakdown.

## Proposed concrete model

Sell the same cup of coffee. Regular price ₹100; reduced price ₹90. Each cup costs ₹50 to make. For this simplified game, 200 daily customers split 100/100 when prices match; if one café discounts, 150 buy there and 50 buy at the other. These are disclosed simulation rules, not a market forecast. Other costs are excluded.

Daily money left after cup costs: both ₹100 → ₹5,000 each; only one at ₹90 → ₹6,000 for the cheaper café and ₹2,500 for its neighbour; both ₹90 → ₹4,000 each. One example receipt: 100 cups × ₹100 = ₹10,000 sales; ₹5,000 cup costs; ₹5,000 left. Show this through staged visuals, not a rules wall.

This changes current payoffs and requires recalculating summaries and rechecking all ten-day paths. Do not relabel current totals without changing their underlying arithmetic.
