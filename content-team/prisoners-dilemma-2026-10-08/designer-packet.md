# Prisoner’s dilemma: common designer brief

Status: Draft. Founder approved the story direction. Subject premises AI-checked; learning review preliminary pass, final sign-off pending. Human tests: 0.

## Scope and objective

Create one playable candidate for curious young Indian adults. Preserve the founder’s story: you and your accomplice Ravi rob a jewellery store, are caught, and are held separately. This identical brief feeds three different interaction lenses; it prescribes no favourite lens. No full scene, app edit, deployment or change to the locked chapter-one opening. Current endpoint: three candidates for review.

Teach **prisoner’s dilemma** only. No Nash equilibrium, repeated games, probability, reputation or second named framework.

Learner outcome: check each person’s incentive while holding the other’s choice fixed; distinguish one person changing from both changing; recheck the conclusion when a stated cost changes. Useful tomorrow: before proposing “everyone should stop,” check what happens to someone who stops alone and what makes continuing attractive.

Mechanism: each can improve their own result through the same action regardless of the other’s fixed choice, yet both doing so leaves both worse off than the alternative shared outcome.

Tempting wrong model: “If both prefer that shared outcome, choosing my part will get me there.” The separating evidence is solo silence giving you five years versus joint silence giving you one. The missing insight is **my move versus both moves**, not a hidden rule. Surprise comes from the competing comparisons; never force a mistaken answer.

## Exact prison rules

Before choosing, show role, goal, control, stakes and the complete deal through short scene beats. Keep the deal available. Each player seeks the fewest prison years for themselves. This is an assigned objective, not their moral worth or everyone’s real values. You control only your statement. Neither sees the other’s final statement beforehand; no communication or enforceable agreement. One fictional decision; replays are labelled what-if comparisons, not later encounters with memory or retaliation.

Actions: “Stay silent” / “Confess.” Confess accepts this fictional deal and names the accomplice; silence refuses it. Zero means no prison years under this invented deal.

| You | Ravi | Your years | Ravi’s years |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Silent | Silent | 1 | 1 |
| Confess | Silent | 0 | 5 |
| Silent | Confess | 5 | 0 |
| Confess | Confess | 3 | 3 |

All consequences are fixed. First choice is unscored; acknowledge the actual selection without inventing its reason. Expose both conditional consequences or state Ravi’s fixed scenario before your choice. Never choose his action in reaction to the learner. Confess/confess is an illustrative branch, not inevitable human behaviour. Viewing Ravi’s room is a teaching comparison, not new power for your character.

Required comparisons:

- Ravi silent: your confess gives 0 versus 1; Ravi confessing: your confess gives 3 versus 5.
- Mirror these from Ravi’s perspective: if you are silent, he gets 0 versus 1; if you confess, he gets 3 versus 5.
- From both confessing: your solo silence changes 3/3 → 5/0; Ravi’s solo silence changes 3/3 → 0/5; joint silence changes 3/3 → 1/1.

Before revealing one solo-switch result, capture an unscored bounded expectation: Ravi still confesses; what happens to your years if only you become silent? The opening statement choice alone is not prediction or misconception evidence.

Explain the cause: confessing rewards you when Ravi stays silent and costs you fewer years when he confesses. He faces the same pressure. Both silence beats both confession for each, but it is not each person’s individually best possible outcome: zero beats one. Name and define **prisoner’s dilemma** after the learner explores this mechanism. Both initial choices receive the teaching.

## Café application

A fictional one-day model, not business evidence. You run one café; another owner runs a nearby café. Each independently chooses today’s action without seeing the other’s final choice. Goal: greatest own daily profit, meaning money left after costs already counted. No future effects or unlisted costs. Actions: “Run a discount” / “Keep usual prices.”

| You | Other café | Your profit | Other profit |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Usual prices | Usual prices | ₹5,000 | ₹5,000 |
| Discount | Usual prices | ₹6,000 | ₹2,000 |
| Usual prices | Discount | ₹2,000 | ₹6,000 |
| Discount | Discount | ₹3,000 | ₹3,000 |

Discount improves own profit in either fixed case: ₹6,000 > ₹5,000 and ₹3,000 > ₹2,000. Same for the other owner. Yet both discounting earns ₹3,000 each versus ₹5,000 each without discounts. Switching alone from both discounting earns you ₹2,000, not ₹5,000. This is practice recognizing the same structure; it is not the changed-condition challenge.

## Changed condition: one new cost

After naming the concept and exploring baseline cafés, disclose a separate what-if: **each café running a discount pays an additional ₹2,000 campaign cost that day; usual prices incur none. Everything else remains unchanged.** This is an authored condition, not a claim about real discount campaigns. Show the cost and resulting profits before choosing, not as a surprise deduction.

| You | Other café | Your final profit | Other final profit |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Usual prices | Usual prices | ₹5,000 | ₹5,000 |
| Discount | Usual prices | ₹4,000 | ₹2,000 |
| Usual prices | Discount | ₹2,000 | ₹4,000 |
| Discount | Discount | ₹1,000 | ₹1,000 |

Now usual prices wins in either fixed case: ₹5,000 > ₹4,000 and ₹2,000 > ₹1,000. Same for the other café. The taught conflict is gone: individually preferable choices also reach ₹5,000/₹5,000. Best action explicitly changes **discount → usual prices** because the incentive changes. Do not add loyalty, future rounds, promises or enforcement. Require a reason tied to the cost; repeating the old action visibly costs ₹1,000 in either case.

## Agency, feedback and learning checks

- Let the learner cause at least one comparison by changing an action while the other stays fixed. Let them test solo versus joint changes; controls disclose whose choices change. Alternatives remain inspectable. No forced wrong move or erased choice.
- A “which number is smaller?” quiz alone fails. Acknowledge actual action, show before → changed choice → consequence, then explain its cause. Verdict-only feedback fails.
- Use short parallel action labels. No longer correct answer, reasoning embedded in only one option, preferred decoration or foolish-versus-wise contrast.
- Keep player, unit, fixed action and current café version visible. Explicitly switch from minimizing years to maximizing profit. Target consequence within 12 taps, approximately one-minute opening and three-to-five-minute lesson; timings are untested.
- Later practice: a fresh situation and a near-match with changed incentives; require both fixed-other and shared comparisons. After two misses, work one example then offer a fresh attempt. Hinted retries are not independent evidence; retention requires a delayed check.

## Boundaries

Tables and comparisons are checkable; best actions are conditional on stated goals and rules, not predictions of people. Trust alone does not change zero versus one. Loyalty or fairness are not wrong values. No moral scoring, real legal-procedure claim, legal guidance, robbery detail, real café forecast or recommendation to coordinate prices. Better for both cafés says nothing about customer or societal welfare.

Source notes: manifest.md, full brief.md and sources.md; researcher checked approved tables against the concept in Ben Polak’s Yale ECON 159 Lecture 1. New cost is an authored variation. Keep citations out of learner dialogue. No human enjoyment, comprehension, retention or transfer claim is earned.

# Untangle pillars

Status: Draft. Consolidates principles the founder approved one by one (history in CHANGELOG); this page as a whole awaits the founder's read. Read this first; it is the whole standard on one page. PATTERN-LIBRARY, VOICE and REJECTED are the detail. CHANGELOG is history. Untangle's team runs on the Learning Studio company (`/Users/rohithpaull/.codex/skills/learning-studio/SKILL.md`); its handbook says the same things for any client, and this page adds what is Untangle's.

## What we are making

Short, playable lessons that let a curious person learn anything by doing. The alternative is Instagram, not another course. A lesson earns attention with a question worth resolving, an action that changes the scene, and a discovery the learner can use at work or in everyday life. Coins, streaks, animation and the fox cannot rescue a lesson that is predictable or shallow. Plain language does not mean basic reasoning.

Founder preference (7 October 2026): connect concepts to recognisable real-world company examples so learners leave with a practical way to interpret products around them. Any engagement benefit remains to be tested.

Current direction (8 October 2026): founder dropped the exploratory NEET PG pivot and approved a standalone prisoner’s-dilemma story: robbery with accomplice Ravi → caught and separated → explore both people’s incentives → reveal and explain the dilemma → café application. Learning Studio resumes this task. Prior JTBD interview-prep openings remain rejected. Current authority: `content-team/prisoners-dilemma-2026-10-08/manifest.md`.

Founder standard (8 October 2026): aim for “That is not what I expected—but now I understand why.” Surprise must follow disclosed rules, not a reactive opponent or a hidden rule. Explain what the learner’s action changed, why it happened, and when a different action fits. Do not turn the mathematical dominance inside this established game into a fake moral quiz.

## Why play teaches

Our design hypothesis: discovering a useful pattern through play can be engaging. Discoverability is a design requirement here, not proof that a lesson is fun or that learning occurred. So every concept must become a mechanism the learner pokes at and watches respond. If a concept has no mechanism yet, it is not ready to be a lesson; send it back to the Teacher.

## The five sentences

After any lesson a newcomer should be able to say: *I know what is happening. I know what I can do. My choice did something. I see why. I learned something worth knowing, and I want the next one.*

Not: *Which company is this? Where did that number come from? Why did the story jump? What am I supposed to pick?*

## The shape of a lesson

Familiar situation → puzzling behaviour → the learner predicts or acts → visible consequence → the missing fact is revealed → the cause is explained → the concept is named → the learner uses it in a fresh situation → a satisfying place to stop.

Make the mechanism visible before naming the concept. Then show the name, define it with exactly what happened in the scene, and check it in a new situation. The hook should produce "I thought I understood, now I see what I missed." Never manufacture surprise by hiding a decisive rule, choosing an outcome after the move, or forcing a wrong answer.

## Before every decision

Role: who am I? Goal: what am I after? Facts: what do I know? Control: what can I change? Stakes: what does each option risk or protect?

Uncertainty should be about consequences, never about what a button means. A first move may be an unscored intuition that is acknowledged and then taught. A scored move needs enough visible information to reason with. Options are concrete actions in the learner's voice, parallel and short; never one foolish option beside one wise one.

## Hard numbers (targets until measured)

- One concept per lesson. Two concepts is two lessons.
- First consequence within 12 taps of the first screen.
- An opening is about one minute of play; a lesson is three to five.
- Two meaningful choices beat five vague ones.
- Rules arrive through play, one at a time, disclosed before they matter. Never a bullet list of rules before the first move.
- After the discovery, change one condition so the discovery changes the right answer (the second move).
- Count meaningful decisions separately from taps.

## The order of work

Teacher brief → three designers, each with a different lens → playable decks → fun gate → check the winner → write the full scene → full-scene Quality check → practice → blind check → working build with a delight pass (only when explicitly requested) → audience variants → human test. Candidate play is judged before exhaustive answer checks; Intake and Teaching still verify the premise first. An authoring task can finish ready for testing. Human understanding and enjoyment remain unverified until unfamiliar people play without coaching.

## Status words

Authored Markdown artifacts carry one status line near the top; decks carry it in metadata. Code and source references are exempt. Use these: **Draft · AI-checked · Fun-gate passed · Founder-approved · Human-tested (n=…) · Retained · Transferred · Locked · Superseded**. Multiple earned labels can share that line. Avoid repetitive disclaimers, but state material assumptions, uncertainty and evidence limits where needed. An AI review is never "human-tested"; a passed check is never "fun".

## Locked

The chapter-one opening through node `baseline` ("Your customers pay you five coins. Your ad costs two. That leaves you three.") is founder-approved and locked. Snapshot: `research/approved-opening-2026-10-05/`. Do not change its wording, sequence, visuals or money movement. Everything after `baseline` is open. Only a later explicit founder instruction reopens it.

# Voice and screen

Status: Draft. Consolidates lines the founder approved and lines the founder rejected, 7 October 2026; the guide as a whole awaits the founder's read. Character detail lives in CHARACTER.md; this file is what writers and designers need.

## The fox

A curious, cheeky friend who experiments alongside the learner. Puzzled, surprised, briefly frustrated with the situation, delighted by discovery. Never mocks, never looks disappointed in the learner, never pretends to know what they understood. The learner controls the decisions; the fox accompanies. Other characters have their own goals and answer in their own voice.

Fox lines are short, direct, second person, about what the player did and what changed. "Let's try it." "What changed?" "Wait, only your price moved." "Now that makes sense." Long explanations belong in the concept card, never in the bubble.

## One beat per screen

One short fox message at a time. Answer choices appear when the fox reaches the question. A committed answer replaces the current view with its consequence; the next beat replaces that. Never append a growing transcript. Keep the setting and relevant objects across the change so a replacement does not feel like a jump. Essential context goes in the fox's main message, not in small supporting text under a diagram.

White canvas, bright accents, rounded outlined controls with tactile depth, playful path. Compact fox at the bottom, roughly 125–150px; it must not compete with the important movement. Diagrams compact; no competing oversized headlines; no forced timers. Reduced motion shows labelled before and after states. Nothing important depends on catching an animation or reading a facial expression.

## The response pattern

Acknowledge the actual selection → show the consequence as movement → explain the cause → name the concept.

Acknowledge without presuming the reason: "Maybe. You'll save on ads. But could you lose customers too?" Not "you're thinking about savings." Acknowledgment is not a verdict.

Show the claim happening: ad switches off, customers move, payments follow customers, ad cost leaves, balances settle. Money never teleports between companies. Every displayed count reconciles.

Explain by connecting to what they did: "You did save the ad cost. Now watch the customers." Never "Nice work", a green box, or "as you can see".

Name after the mechanism: the exact concept name, large, then a plain definition built from the scene, then a fresh situation to use it in.

## Before and after

| Rejected | Approved | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "You both pay for ads. Neither gets ahead." as an opening | "You run a cigarette company. TV ads are about to be banned. Will you make less money—or more?" | Role, event and question before any diagram or conclusion |
| "Here's company A and company B." | "Here's your rival. You sell the same thing. Right now, you both run ads—and each has five customers." | Relationship before names and numbers |
| "What should we test first?" | "A stops its ads to save money. What happens to its customers?" | Choices are about the story, not which explanation to open |
| "Late promises won't work" / "The team will probably rescue them" | "Accept Friday. Ask the team to work late." / "Offer Tuesday. Ask Sales to change the deadline." | Concrete actions in the learner's voice, both with a cost |
| Green box: "Correct! Great job." | "You did save the ad cost. Now watch the customers." | Feedback addresses the answer and shows the result |
| A celebratory card reading "Strategic interdependence!" | Same choice, two rival conditions, two different results; then the name and definition | Mechanism before the name |
| "Strategic independence" | "Strategic interdependence: your result depends on what you choose AND what others choose." | Correct the term kindly, define it at once |
| "Rational means choosing the money." | "Rationality: choosing what best serves your goals given what you believe." | Mira declines ₹800 to keep a ₹500 promise; she did not stop caring about money |
| "Ask your manager about timing." | "Before pitching a change, find out who gains, who bears the risk and what each person is rewarded for." | A takeaway that changes how someone handles work |

## Language rules

- Familiar words; explain a new concept name when it first appears.
- Options are parallel, short, concrete. Do not put the right answer in the longer or more respectable-sounding option.
- One current state per object. A selectable price and money earned are different things; do not reuse a number for two meanings.
- Label a fictional company and a real case in plain words when it matters. No citations, footers, book attributions or links in learner copy; sources live in author notes.
- No "dark psychology", no mind-reading. Motives are revealed by what characters say and do; work tips invite investigation.
- Do not shorten copy until the context disappears. "Only A stops its ads. B keeps advertising." beats "What happens now?"
- Signal a change of time, role or setting before the next decision. Keep one role across an opening.

# Studio handbook

Status: the studio standard, one page. Everything else is detail.

## Mission

Build content out of anything and make learning fun for everyone. "Anything" means any material a client can hand us, from a textbook to a founder's half-formed idea. "Everyone" means any age, prior knowledge, reading level, language or accessibility need. Fun is a design goal; enjoyment and learning need separate evidence.

## Why play teaches

Our design hypothesis: discovering a useful pattern through play can be engaging. Discoverability is a design requirement here, not proof that a lesson is fun or that learning occurred. So every concept must become a mechanism the learner pokes at and watches respond. A concept with no mechanism yet is not ready to be a lesson; it goes back to Teaching.

## The five sentences

After any lesson a newcomer should be able to say: *I know what is happening. I know what I can do. My choice did something. I see why. I learned something worth knowing, and I want the next one.* Every team's work is judged against these five sentences.

## The shape of a lesson

Familiar situation → puzzling behaviour → the learner predicts or acts → visible consequence → the missing fact is revealed → the cause is explained → the concept is named → the learner uses it in a fresh situation → a satisfying place to stop.

Mechanism before name. Name, then a plain definition built from what just happened, then a fresh situation. The hook should produce "I thought I understood, now I see what I missed." Never manufacture surprise by hiding a decisive rule, choosing an outcome after the move, or forcing a wrong answer.

## Before every decision

Role, goal, facts, control, stakes. Uncertainty is about consequences, never about what a button means. A first move may be an unscored intuition that is acknowledged and then taught. A scored move needs enough visible information to reason with. Options are concrete actions in the learner's voice, parallel and short; never one foolish beside one wise.

## Hard numbers (targets until measured)

One concept per lesson. First consequence within 12 taps. An opening is about one minute; a lesson three to five. Two meaningful choices beat five vague ones. Rules arrive through play, one at a time, disclosed before they matter. After the concept is named, change one condition so the discovery changes the right answer. Count meaningful decisions separately from taps.

## The order of work

Intake → Teaching brief → three designers with different lenses → playable decks → fun gate → check the winner → full scene → full-scene Quality check → practice → blind check → optional authorized build and delight pass → audience variants → human test on deck or build. Candidate play before exhaustive answer checking; premise verification comes first. Builds run only when explicitly requested. Authoring can finish ready for testing; human learning and enjoyment remain unverified until observed.

## The evidence ladder

These are separate evidence labels, not guaranteed promotions or a required chronological order: Fun-gate passed (candidate retained after a recorded play review) · AI-checked (logic and rules checked) · Human-tested (unfamiliar people played; their successes and failures are logged) · Retained (supported by a delayed check) · Transferred (supported by unprompted use elsewhere). A human test can reveal failure; the label does not mean comprehension passed. Use status.md; keep material assumptions and limitations in the body instead of repeating generic disclaimers.

## Craft over checks

The quality of a lesson is decided by the inputs: a mechanism worth discovering, a lens that fits it, a world with stakes, a voice that talks to the player. Checks protect that; they cannot create it. When a lesson fails the fun gate, change the premise or the lens, not the number of reviewers.

# Play Designer (Play)

You are one of three designers working separately. You build one opening under the lens you were assigned. You do not see the other designers' work.

**Receive:** `brief.md`, `handbook.md`, your lens card and reference beat from `pattern-library.md`, `fun.md`, the client's local pattern examples and rejected list, the deck template and `check-deck.py`.

**Work:** design backward from the feeling (curiosity, agency, earned insight) to what the player does to the rules. First screen: role, event, immediate goal, the one control, nothing unexplained. Rules arrive through play, each just before it matters; the first move may be an unscored intuition. Every first-choice branch: the guide acknowledges the actual choice, the consequence is shown as movement with reconciled counts, the cause is explained, then the concept is named and defined from the scene. Second move: one condition changed so the idea changes the answer; every result written, including the unsuccessful ending with a worked successful path. A stopping point and one reason to continue. One concrete risk that could make it boring. Vary what the player does, not the nouns. Keep the first consequence within twelve taps. Build the deck; run the checker until it passes.

**Output:** `candidates/<x>/script.md`, `learner-only.md` (setup, visible rules and choices only; no outcomes, key or notes), `deck.html`.

**Done when:** every branch is written, the deck plays every branch, the checker passes, and the script states the lens and the reference beat used.

**Never:** read another candidate; produce more than one candidate; grade a prediction on a hidden fact; choose a payoff after the move; pair a foolish option with a wise one; call the draft fun.

# What makes it fun

Status: the studio's working theory of fun, used by Play, Story and Playtest.

**Fun is pattern-learning.** Raph Koster's observation: the pleasure of play is the brain grasping a pattern and then mastering it. When a lesson is boring, check whether its pattern is worth grasping or was revealed too soon; these are hypotheses, not the only possible causes. Fix the premise or the reveal order; do not add rewards.

**Show, don't tell** (the explorable-explanation tradition: Bret Victor, Nicky Case). Let the learner touch the model. If a sentence can be replaced by a thing the learner can change and watch, replace it.

**Mechanics → dynamics → feeling.** Designers set rules; rules produce behaviour; behaviour produces feeling. Design backward from the feeling you want (curiosity, agency, earned insight) to the behaviour that produces it, then to the rules.

**The anatomy of a hook.** A familiar situation; one thing that does not add up; a question the learner can act on in under twelve taps. The first consequence must be visible and must matter to someone in the scene.

**Agency with stakes.** A choice is meaningful when the options are both reasonable, the result differs, and something the learner cares about is on the line. Fake stakes (a score that nothing depends on) are worse than none.

**Fair surprise.** Surprise is the moment of learning only when the rule was visible or the guess was explicitly unscored. A twist built on a hidden rule teaches "the game cheats".

**The second move.** Application beyond recognition: change one condition and let the learner use the idea. This is where delight lives; the first discovery is only the setup.

**A guide, not a teacher.** The character is on the learner's side, curious with them, surprised with them, never disappointed in them. It speaks to what the learner did.

**Juice is not fun.** Motion, sound and confetti amplify a feeling that already exists. On a worksheet they amplify nothing. Animation explains cause; it never decorates.

**Stopping is part of the design.** A satisfying stop with saved progress and one clear next thing is how someone comes back. A lesson that only ends when the learner gives up has no ending.

**Signs a lesson is not fun yet** (use these in reviews): the learner's role is "person doing an exercise"; the options are "sensible" versus "careless"; the consequence is a number changing in a table; the concept card arrives before anything surprised anyone; the second move repeats the first with new nouns; the only reason to continue is that the lesson is not over.

# Deck: the playable unit of review

Status: Untangle copy of the studio deck tooling (source of truth: `~/.codex/skills/learning-studio/deck/`). This copy differs only by the character block, which holds the approved fox. A deck is a self-contained HTML file a person taps through on a phone: one guide line per screen, one thing to do, a consequence that replaces the screen. Every Play Designer delivers one; the fun gate plays them; people can test them before any build exists.

## Files

- `deck-template.html`: copy this to `candidates/<x>/deck.html` and replace the JSON in `<script id="deck">`. The guide character lives in `<script id="character">`; clients replace it with their own (Untangle's copy has the fox).
- `example-deck.html`: the v1 "customer who wants your whole team" opening (`../pm-pilot/opening-rethink.md`) rendered as a deck. Draft, unapproved; it exists to show what a candidate looks like when it arrives at the fun gate. The studio copy has a tutorial deck showing every visual type.
- `check-deck.py`: `python3 check-deck.py deck.html` validates structure and prints taps to the first consequence; `--export` prints the whole script as Markdown so `script.md` never drifts from the deck.

## The deck JSON

```
{ "meta": {"title", "lesson", "candidate", "lens", "status", "author"},
  "start": "s01",
  "scenes": { "<id>": { ...scene } } }
```

A scene: `line` (the guide's words; a string, or an object of conditions such as `{"choice=a": "…", "default": "…"}`), `mood` (`waiting`, `speaking`, `thinking`, `surprised`, `celebrating`), `visual` (optional, below), `choices` (list of `{"label", "next", "set": {"var": "value"}, "primary": true}`; `next` may also be a condition object), `input` (an unscored free-text reflection: `{"placeholder", "button", "next", "save"}`), `consequence: true` on the first screens where the learner sees the result of a choice (the checker measures taps to it), `end: true` on endings, `note` (author-only text; shown in author mode and in the export, never to learners), `set` (variables to set on entry). `{{var}}` in any text inserts a variable.

Visual types: `counters` (`items: [{"label", "value"}]`; numbers animate from the previous scene's value for the same label), `people` (`groups: [{"label", "count", "color"}]`), `cards` (`items: [{"title", "lines": [], "selected": true}]`), `board` (`columns: [{"title", "items": [], "empty": "…"}]`), `message` (`from`, `text`: another character speaking), `concept` (`name`, `definition`: the concept card, only after the mechanism), `image` (`src`, `alt`), `text` (`text`). Any visual may carry a `caption`.

## Rules the template enforces

One beat per screen; a committed choice replaces the screen; the guide stays compact at the bottom; reduced motion removes all transitions and number animation; every control is a real button at least 48px tall; keyboard and screen-reader order follow the beat. Taps and decisions are counted; the end screen shows a session log the facilitator can copy for `test-log.md`.

## What a deck is not

Not the build. Drag and drop, live simulations and real motion belong to the Prototype Engineer. A deck proves the opening works as a sequence of choices and consequences, which is what the fun gate and early human tests need.

## Structural pacing checks

`python3 check-deck.py deck.html` reports earliest and latest first-consequence taps across every declared branch. Routes ending or cycling before feedback, missing targets, unreachable scenes, and routes with no ending fail. Every declared opening route must reach feedback within 12 taps. Conditional targets need a default fallback; all declared branches are included conservatively. These bounds do not simulate variable state, voluntary restarts or elapsed time: confirm actual routes by playing the deck. A cycle anywhere makes the reported longest structural route unbounded.

`python3 test-check-deck.py` runs synthetic graph regression tests, not learner-content or enjoyment tests. The shared studio and Untangle checker copies must remain identical. Exported Markdown is an author view with outcomes and notes; never give `--export` output to a blind checker as a learner-only packet.
