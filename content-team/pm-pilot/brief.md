# Pilot brief — Which launch actually worked?

Status: original teaching model; not a historical case. Script only. No prototype changes.

Audience: curious adults with no product analytics background; short attention spans. Player leads a team launching a shared-task app. Goal: identify which launch brings people back, without confusing total signups with sustained use.

Learning objectives:
1. Compare return rates using each group's original signup count.
2. Recognise an unfair comparison when groups have had different time to return.
3. Distinguish an observed difference from proof of its cause.

Teaching scope: cohort means people grouped by the same starting event/time. Here return means completing at least one task exactly seven calendar days after signup. Count people once, not tasks. Both main groups have complete observation windows. These definitions belong in accessible scene language before any scored decision.

Main dataset: previous launch 1,000 signups / 80 people complete a task on day seven; current launch 200 / 40. 8% versus 20%; fewer returners in total, larger proportion. No universal verdict about commercial success. Compare return rate only. Both launched on different dates; invitation source and onboarding changed, so do not attribute the difference to onboarding.

Interaction plan:
- Unscored prediction from signup totals; acknowledge choice before showing return evidence.
- Compare equal-sized groups visually: 8 of 100 versus 20 of 100. Explain cohort retention only after discovery.
- Catch incomplete observation: a new group is only three days old, so day-seven result is pending, not zero.
- Choose a defensible team update: improved observed return rate; cause not established.
- Closing MCQs and separate practice use fresh numbers/settings. Wrong answer gets causal feedback and a targeted fresh question; preserve position and return to the interrupted question with its answer cleared.

Deliver exact dialogue, concrete options, every branch, visual movement instructions, concept names, answers and explanation, misconception routes, source notes separately. Keep fox messages short; never hide a decisive rule to produce a twist. No claims of human validation or autonomous readiness.

Reviewer constraints: normalized 100-dot displays represent rates, not a sampled subgroup. Explain signup day is day zero and day seven is the date one week later, in the same reporting timezone. Answer retries after feedback are supported practice, never fresh mastery evidence. Track navigation separately from meaningful choices.
