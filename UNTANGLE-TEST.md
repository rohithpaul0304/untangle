# Untangle: before-build test

Purpose: test whether the bill-split reveal produces understanding, then whether that understanding transfers. This is a facilitator script, not additional v1 screens.

## Before starting

Show one section at a time. Do not expose later reveals early. Record elapsed time and exact words. Say: “I'm testing this explanation. Please choose as you normally would; tell me anything that's unclear.”

## 1. Show the choice

> Eight friends. Everyone pays an equal share. You choose before seeing anyone else's order.
>
> The ₹200 dish is good. You would pay up to ₹150 extra for the ₹600 dish. Your friends feel the same.
>
> Which do you order: ₹200 or ₹600?

Record the choice and any spontaneous reasoning. Do not correct it.

## 2. Show the consequence

> All seven friends chose the ₹600 dish independently.
>
> Arjun: “The upgrade adds only ₹50 to my share. It's worth ₹150 to me.”

If the participant chose ₹200:

> The table's bill is ₹4,400. Your share is ₹550.
> Choosing ₹600 instead would make your share ₹600: ₹50 more for an upgrade you value at ₹150.

If the participant chose ₹600:

> The table's bill is ₹4,800. Your share is ₹600.
> Choosing ₹200 instead would make your share ₹550: you save ₹50 but lose an upgrade you value at ₹150.

Then show:

> If everyone chose ₹200, each would pay ₹200. Each saves ₹400 while giving up an upgrade worth ₹150 to them.

Pause. Record the unprompted reaction before asking anything.

## 3. Check understanding before naming the idea

Ask separately, waiting for each answer:

1. “If everyone else kept their order, what would happen if you changed yours?”
2. “How does that compare with everyone choosing the ₹200 dish?”

Record whether the participant explains the ₹50 personal cost versus the ₹400 shared change, and the assumed ₹150 upgrade value. A different personal food preference is a useful objection to the setup, not proof of misunderstanding.

Now show:

> Everyone ordering expensive is a Nash equilibrium: changing only your own order makes you worse off under these rules.
>
> Yet everyone would prefer the bill if all ordered cheap.

## 4. Test transfer

Show without additional explanation:

> You're in the middle of a concert crowd. The row ahead stands up. Standing gets your old view back with sore legs. Staying seated blocks your view. Seeing the show matters more to you than resting your legs.
>
> Do you stand or stay seated?

After the choice, ask: “What do you expect happens next, and why?”

Record whether the participant independently connects individual incentives and the shared outcome. If not, ask “Does this remind you of the dinner?” Record that as prompted recognition, not spontaneous transfer. Choosing stand alone is insufficient evidence.
