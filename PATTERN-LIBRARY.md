The Teacher names the concept's kind; the coordinator gives each of the three designers a different lens; designers may propose a lens not listed if they say what it borrows from.

## Choosing a lens

| The concept is mainly… | Try these lenses first |
|---|---|
| a causal mechanism, a system, a rate, a feedback loop | Toy · Predict and compare |
| a strategic interaction, "same choice, different result" | Predict and compare · Commit under others' moves |
| a tradeoff, opportunity cost, a bottleneck, an allocation | Commit a scarce resource · Toy |
| a hidden incentive, a motive, an unseen cause | Investigate · Configure a rule |
| a design, product or process principle | Repair the thing · Configure a rule |
| a policy, a classification, who a rule affects | Configure a rule · Reconstruct |
| a sequence, a timeline, correlation versus causation | Reconstruct · Investigate |
| a commitment, credibility, a promise | Commit under others' moves · Predict and compare |

## The lenses, with Untangle's examples

The studio catalogue also has Build then break, Find the exception and Teach it back; Untangle has no worked example for those yet.

### Toy — adjust one thing and watch the system respond
What the learner does: flips one switch or moves one slider, then watches a small visible world settle: things move, counts change, a balance updates.
Reference beat: Parable of the Polygons lets you change one tolerance rule and watch neighbourhoods separate; The Evolution of Trust states one rule, lets you play it, then adds one more.
Our example (Locked): the chapter-one market. Ads on or off, five customers each, customers pay coins, ad cost leaves, balance settles. "Your customers pay you five coins. Your ad costs two. That leaves you three."
Goes wrong when: the result is shown before the move; several knobs appear at once; the final ledger stands in for the event instead of showing customers moving and payments following them.

### Predict and compare — same move, changed condition
What the learner does: predicts an outcome, sees it, then sees the identical move under one changed condition and compares.
Reference beat: The Evolution of Trust tutorial. Rule, your move, their move, compare; then the other player's behaviour changes.
Our example (Founder-approved comparison): your ads off while the rival's ads stay on, versus your ads off while the rival's are off too. "You made the same choice but got a different result because of their choice." Then the name: strategic interdependence.
Goes wrong when: an uninformed prediction is graded against a hidden invented payoff. An intuition question before the rule is fine only if it is unscored, acknowledged, then taught.

### Commit a scarce resource — spend something you cannot get back
What the learner does: allocates time, money, people or attention; the balance stays visible; the consequence lands on something they care about.
Reference beat: SPENT. Pick a job, watch the balance and the day counter, live with it.
Our example (Draft): the biggest customer wants a dashboard by Friday; the team has one week. Promise the week, or ask what they actually need first. The task board fills or stays empty.
Goes wrong when: capacities are handed over as a table and the activity becomes arithmetic homework; when the "resource" is fake stakes with no visible balance.

### Investigate — choose what to look at; each reveal changes the decision
What the learner does: picks who to ask or what evidence to open; each reveal exposes a stake or incentive; the decision they would have made changes.
Reference beat: detective structure; Duolingo Adventures' in-world dialogue where characters answer with their own goals.
Our example (Founder-approved direction): a successful automation trial saves hours, yet the manager resists. Ask around: reduced reported workload could cost the team a position. A company-level benefit threatens something another person values.
Goes wrong when: the choice is lesson administration ("What should we test first?"); when motives are asserted as mind-reading instead of revealed through what people say and do.

### Repair the thing — inspect a failure, change the artifact, replay it
What the learner does: watches a user or process fail, edits the interface, rule or plan, replays and sees whether the failure is gone, then meets a situation where the same fix no longer works.
Reference beat: our Opening A (Draft). Maya deletes the wrong photo; add a review sheet or an undo bar; replay. Then Share, where undo cannot recall the recipient's copy. Name: reversibility.
Goes wrong when: two components make it a dressed-up two-option quiz; when the replay reveals nothing new.

### Configure a rule and watch who it hits
What the learner does: sets a rule once (who gets notified, who qualifies, what counts), then watches it apply to several people whose circumstances differ.
Reference beat: our Opening B (Draft). Ping or silent for a chat; one person needs a door code, another is asleep.
Goes wrong when: labels make the right rule obvious; when the people affected are interchangeable.

### Reconstruct what happened — order the evidence until it stops contradicting itself
What the learner does: arranges events, dates or claims; a wrong order produces a visible contradiction (a result before its cause, a day-seven count before day seven).
Reference beat: the pending-cohort moment in the PM pilot (Draft): a group three days old shows 0% on day seven; moving the calendar reveals the result is pending, not zero.
Goes wrong when: it becomes a sorting worksheet with no drama; when the contradiction is only explained, never seen.

### Commit under others' moves — promise, then live with what others do
What the learner does: makes a commitment with visible cost, then sees how others respond, then sees the same situation when the commitment is credible or not.
Reference beat: our approved decision setup (Founder-approved setup only): you lead a design team, the week is booked, Sales promised a client Friday without asking. "Accept Friday. Ask the team to work late." or "Offer Tuesday. Ask Sales to change the deadline." Both protect something and cost something.
Goes wrong when: one option is obviously careless; when chronology is wrong (Saturday work cannot meet Friday).

## Rules that apply to every lens

**Rules through play.** Disclose each rule just before it matters, through the scene, and let the first move be the demonstration. A rule the learner never needed to know yet does not belong on the screen yet.

**The second move.** After the concept is named, change one condition so the discovery changes the right answer. Someone who repeats the first answer should lose visibly and see why; someone who applies the idea should win visibly. This is the difference between "I read it" and "I can use it".

**Acknowledge, show, explain, name.** Every branch acknowledges the actual selection without inventing the learner's reasoning, shows the consequence as movement (before → change → after, counts reconciled), explains the cause in the fox's voice, then names the concept.

**The approved anchor and why it worked.** "Your customers pay you five coins. Your ad costs two. That leaves you three." The role was already established; the text follows the visible movement; one causal chain; no new rival or rule introduced mid-calculation. Borrow the shape, not the setting.

**Vary the central activity across a chapter.** Do not repeat "choose → fox explains → concept card" for every scene. Curate the strongest stories rather than adding compulsory concepts.

**A useful takeaway.** Every lesson ends with something the learner can do differently: before pitching a change, find out who gains, who bears the risk and what each person is rewarded for. "Ask your manager about timing" is not a takeaway.

## Reference shelf

These were reviewed in October 2026 (full notes: `research/storytelling-2026-10-05/competitive-analysis.md`). They are design hypotheses, not proof of retention.

- The Evolution of Trust: one rule, play, compare, change one condition. The pacing bar for an opening.
- SPENT: stable role and stakes, balance always visible, in-world consequences.
- Parable of the Polygons: local rule to collective outcome; controlled comparison.
- Duolingo Adventures and the supplied Duolingo recording: character prompt, clear answer area, feedback replaces the action area, in-world character responses.
- Brilliant: visual guidance and targeted practice (not played in a signed-in lesson; treat as a product claim).
