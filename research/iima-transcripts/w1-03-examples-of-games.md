In the subsequent videos much later, we'll extend it to, as I said, sequential move games, uncertainty games, and all. I call this as voluntary contribution game. There are five players. Number of players is quite arbitrary, honest. But there are five players in the market out there.
And I give each of them 10 rupees. I ask each of them to contribute certain amount of money to a common kitty. Whatever money I collect this way, I would double it and distribute it equally to all the people. Let's understand. What are the number of players here? The number of players in this game is five, individual A, B, C, D, E. Make sense?
Now, each player has a set of strategies. What is the strategy set each player has? Each player can choose to contribute one, two, three, four, all the way up to 10 rupees. We will ignore decimals for the time being. People understand getting change is the worst nightmare. So we'll stick to natural numbers, one, two, three, up to ten. Each player has access to ten strategies.
I also said for the game to work out, each player has to maximize something or optimize something. What is it that each player is doing here? Each player is trying to maximize the amount of money he or she has. Of course, I care about the money in my purse. But what is this dependent on? How much money do I get at the end of the day, is not just dependent on what I contribute. It also depends on what you contribute, what he or she contributes, what everyone else contributes.
So the fundamental idea is this is a game because not only does the decision I make influence my outcome, the decisions that every other co-player of mine makes influences my outcome. Imagine, just for the sake of argument, I am a seller of white shirts. How many shirts I sell in the market is not determined by me alone, by the price that I charge in the market. How many shirts I sell in the market is determined by what price I charge and what my co-seller of shirts charges us. If I'm cheaper, presumably I get more. If he or she is cheaper, they probably get more number of people. It also depends on our reputations, etc, etc, but the fundamental idea is what I do and what he or she does influences my net sales at the end of the day.
So that's a fundamental distinction I want to make. Another game that I play commonly at IIM Ahmedabad, I call it as grading game. I actually copied this game from Professor Ben Pollock at Yale from one of his sessions. Number of players is two. I asked my students a simple idea that they need to choose between two strategies, alpha or beta, simultaneously. They don't know who their partner is. The partner also doesn't know who he or she is.
It's completely anonymous. But they have to choose between two strategies, alpha and beta. If both of them, by chance, ends up choosing alpha and alpha, both of them would get a grade called B minus. Both of them end up choosing beta and beta, both of them would get a grade called B plus. If one chooses alpha and the other one chooses beta, the one who chooses alpha gets A, and the one who chooses beta gets C. Once again, there are two players. The end goal grade that a student gets depends on what he or she chooses and what everyone else chooses.
What is it that every student is optimizing out here? Every student is optimizing their GPA. In other words, every student is interested in maximizing their own grade. So in short, every strategic decision is not a game. A game is where there are set of players, each player has a set of strategies from which they choose, and they are optimizing something. And whatever they are optimizing depends on what they choose and what everyone else chooses. [MUSIC]
